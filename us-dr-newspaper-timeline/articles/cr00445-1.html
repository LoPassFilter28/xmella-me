The New York Herald

ST. DOMINGO. Condition of the Country—Disappearance of the Paper Currency—Manoeuvres of Cabral—The Annexation Question—Trade on the Rope.

I complained in my last—of the 12th instant or thereabouts—that the news went very far, but had not come. Since then they have been up to their duty. Nearly every day or night we have had at least one story to give us hope that the heavens are smiling gratefully in return. The roads will not improve as long as the vegetation continues, though a courier from the confines of Hayti, whom I caught on the wing, says they are in fine order compared with what they were. These stories are worth reading, not from their intrinsic importance nor from their truth, but from the light they throw on the statements. One minister writes that his 'government' will unite with that of the Dominican republic as soon as it can; if the United States if President Baez will share with his neighbor the loan he has lately obtained from England. Another of the ministers expresses his 'loyal desire for a lasting peace with our Dominican brothers;' but there can be no peace with a jack-pot, for I must tell you that Azua and Porto Plata are plotting to sell their country to the Spaniards. So he proposes to form a coalition of the English loan to extinguish the Cabral-Ocoa and establish a peaceful and united government over the whole island. These are the stories; and though I might have missed the pleasure of reading them—for the private correspondence is not open to all—upon the case the Dominican President should entertain them, but does he not know that 'the English currency' was not yet in existence, but proposed to be? And did he not know, or should have known, that of this coming government had it won it would, even since, have to be paid for in 'hard cash' or 'good paper?' There were more letters by this courier, but one and all of them show that the people on both sides of the line of war are sadly wanting in Hayti.

The paper currency of Baez, which has hitherto been the only circulating medium, is now almost played out. Dominican paper currency has entirely disappeared from the city of St. Domingo, though it is worth last months of Cabral, and I am told paper is now more than two hundred for one, and getting daily worse on the frontier. This paper currency is the key to the whole situation—and all the situations—on this island. The various chiefs printed it off by wholesale and sold it the merchants, who paid half or two-thirds of their duties with it, besides using it to buy produce. Soldiers received their pay in it, and everybody received it at some rate or other until the country was so overflooded with it that the thing became a nuisance. For a time such successions of paper as the Cabral, or a new or other, to be taken in preference to the old, and various other devices, were tried to overcome and repudiated, but they failed; and the poor people—the country people always felt most severely—have conceived a deep hatred for it. The government, however, has a promise to redeem with silver the Cabral issue was the cause of the paper currency in use; it did begin this in beginning of January, but even that bait did not take, and the paper is now worth nothing, and is not used in a week.

General Cabral entered the Dominican territory about three months ago, and has been in nominal possession of the depopulated border belt; but he cannot get further, and the people are gradually entirely out of it. Letters from both sides have reached here, and they say that the people have had skirmishes (they call them battles), in which both parties were claimed to be victorious, but the value of their victories may be estimated from this fact—that a dozen men have been killed or taken on either side up to the 10th, the very last date of reliable accounts.

The news that Congress adjourned without bringing the Dominican republic to enter the Union was not welcomed here by the better class of people; but flags had been found, or made, to be in readiness to hoist on the news. The merchants have set their minds to it that you will hear then talking of 'our silver' and 'our gold,' instead of 'our nice flour.' United States quarters and dimes are now plenty in all the West India islands, where the use of paper has made them scarce at home, and they are the only 'currency' which the people here feel good over.

On Friday, the 10th, a schooner came in from Porto Rico overladen with a 'live' cargo; in other words, she was flying that Spanish colour by the orders of the government, and she had on board, for safety and protection, as not one of them could be trusted since the last revolt in Porto Rico, fifty-five prisoners, and sixty-one persons came over with their little all by this same boat, which was only of about eighty tons burden to charter, and others are to follow as soon as they can get away. They were a respectable class, being mostly of the poorest class who had enlisted in the Spanish army during the period of occupation, and they were glad to get away for a time from the chief excitements of the hour. I repeat that there is but one topic other than the chief object of public excitement here about the border movements, and the opinion until this morning was that even if there should be no one would care except the officials who are expected to go.

Business is more animated with Europe, for 70,000 or 80,000 pounds of coffee have been shipped, and the trade is principally on German account, as is shown by the arrival of the three ships. All the other cargo that is coming in is of the leaf sugar, which its nearer neighbors ought to take from it. Porto Rico is now only sending us calicoes, sheetings and other cotton fabrics, and are a great relief to the want of United States manufactures. The only American cottons I have seen in the country were a few bales of sheetings, imported by Messrs. Read & Ricart, in the American barque, consigned to Read & Ricart, to try the market. They were from New Haven, and the vessel went on to England, and there is one small craft taking in cargo for Boston.

It is all wrong, for the United States ought to supply every yard of cotton goods used on this island, and it would be a better field than it is for us.

The news from the north side shows the tobacco crop to be larger than any previous year. It is estimated that not less than 12,000 or 15,000 ceroons of tobacco will be exported from Porto Plata this season.

General Baez is still here, and his health is good by this steamer. The President is very reticent as to his future movements, but there is a certain twinkle in his eye that betokens inward satisfaction.
