Bismarck lo recibió amablemente pero finalmente lo despidió con la observación de que los Estados Unidos no tolerarían la injerencia alemana en esas regiones.

Some fourteen or fifteen years ago Gen. [illegible] Taylor, then one of the directors in a company which claimed to have acquired a controlling interest in Samana Bay, went to Berlin and submitted to Prince Bismarck a plan for the acquisition of that harbor. According to Gen. Taylor's statement to a trusted friend, he was most kindly received, and listened to with the utmost interest. But finally the Prince dismissed him with the remark that the United States would not tolerate German interference in those quarters. About the same time, when Ferdinand de [Lesseps?], then on his way back from Panama to France, stopped at Washington, Mr. [Evarts?], then Secretary of State, exacted from the French Government a declaration to the effect that France would never have anything to do with the enterprise of the Panama Canal; and the French Minister at Washington, having been authorized to execute such a declaration, signed it and placed it in the hands of the Secretary of State. These various illustrations disclose a noteworthy situation which has not yet been fully apprehended by the general public. It is true that no step has been taken that could reveal a well defined outline of American policy; and it is equally true that no European power contemplates any act or measure that might indicate a tendency to increase its hold over any part of the Antilles.
