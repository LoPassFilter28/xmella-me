Writing from San Domingo on February 15, 1871, the correspondent describes the capital on the western bank of the river Ozama, a walled city 375 years old that is now utterly indefensible against modern artillery by land or sea. He walks through its ruins, above all the great Franciscan religious house covering several acres, where a tall orange tree laden with fruit grows in the very centre of the roofless church, bees swarm in the crevices of its walls, and animals are stabled by its altars.

Escribiendo desde Santo Domingo el 15 de febrero de 1871, el corresponsal describe la capital en la orilla occidental del río Ozama, una ciudad amurallada de 375 años que hoy es totalmente indefendible contra la artillería moderna por tierra o por mar. Recorre sus ruinas, sobre todo la gran casa religiosa de los franciscanos, que cubre varios acres, donde un alto naranjo cargado de fruta crece en el centro mismo de la iglesia sin techo, las abejas bullen en las grietas de sus muros, y hay animales estabulados junto a sus altares.

Past this runs the old Spanish highway along the southern coast to Azua, once a fine road, but now akin to everything else in this blasted and ruined land, if any one doubts the "great first cause," —the all-prevailing law that controls the universe, let him come and look at Dominica. If the cry of Cacique and Carib rises to heaven for plundered kingdoms, and slaughtered households; —if the land is red with the blood of her children;—so, too, it is full of the bones of her oppressors, and in less than four centuries their monuments have crumbled and their mightiest works have perished. This city is full of ruins, and no small part of its limited area is occupied by them. The most impressive are those of the great religious house of the Franciscans, located on the highest ground within the walls, and covering several acres, with the wreck of buildings and desolated gardens. Ascending a steep street over antiquated pavements which grandee and Caballero have trodden before you, you pass under a massive portal, and stand in a growth of shrubbery and trees, so thick that the eyes can hardly penetrate it. An orange tree 80 feet high, laden with fruit stands in the very center.
