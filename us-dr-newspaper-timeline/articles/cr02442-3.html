Writer David J. Davies argues that the secrecy surrounding the conduct of United States marines in Hayti and San Domingo is lifting, as Major-General George Barnett's letter calling for a full investigation focuses public attention on the occupation. He recounts stories told by marines who served in both republics, including a former steel worker he met at Santiago de los Caballeros who boasted of orders to kill and burn everything in a valley near Port au Prince and of natives being killed for the fun of it.

El escritor David J. Davies argumenta que el secreto que rodea la conducta de los infantes de marina de los Estados Unidos en Haití y Santo Domingo se está levantando, a medida que la carta del mayor general George Barnett pidiendo una investigación completa centra la atención pública en la ocupación. Relata historias contadas por infantes de marina que sirvieron en ambas repúblicas, incluido un ex trabajador del acero que conoció en Santiago de los Caballeros y que se jactaba de órdenes de matar y quemar todo en un valle cerca de Puerto Príncipe y de nativos asesinados por diversión.

[illegible] The statement in Col. Russell's letter that only twelve marines have been killed during the occupation of Hayti is at variance with the stories of the marines who served there with Waller and Butler and appeared to know whereof they spoke. I can recall the night we stopped at Monte Christi en route for Puerto Plata on our way from the States. A sergeant coming aboard with other officers reported that on the night before a party of marines had been waylaid and seven of them killed. There was in the party the wife of a Major, but she had escaped unhurt. Doubtless the marines — officers as well as men — suffered great provocation — not "doubtless"; they did! Little wonder then that their morals as well as their morale suffered to that extent that the Navy Department felt impelled to despatch a special agent to the island in an effort to work out the salvation — physical and spiritual — of [illegible] there. That special agent was a Pittsburgher. His report submitted after several months in Hayti caused a furor of doubt and excitement in official circles. At once preparations were made to ameliorate conditions, and beginning late in 1918 some improvements were worked out. Young men who went to Hayti clean and wholesome remained clean and wholesome only for a brief, brief time. Many of them left there wrecks of their former selves; they were in such condition as to be better dead. Others — and many of them — were sent home raving maniacs.
