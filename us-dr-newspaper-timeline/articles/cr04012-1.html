If any one will take the trouble to draw, on any good map of the United States, a line due South from the extreme point of the Peninsula of Florida, it will be found—after crossing the inlet, sixty miles wide, from the Atlantic Ocean to the Gulf of Mexico—to intersect the Island of Cuba at a point about one-third of its length. The Northern shore of the remaining two-thirds of that Island winds in a gentle curve, in a South-Easterly direction; finally terminating at Cape Maysi, which is nearly on the "Windward Passage"—a strait which connects the Atlantic Ocean with the Caribbean Sea—separated there from Hayti—the distance between Cape Maysi, the Eastern terminus of the former Island, and Port St. Nicholas, the Western terminus of the latter, being not more than twenty-five miles. In the Western portion of Hayti, covering about one-third of the area of the Island, is situated the Republic of Hayti, Port au Prince being its Capital. The Eastern division of the same Island, and by far the most mountainous, constitutes the Republic of Dominica, of which San Domingo is the Capital. The Mona Passage, another entrance from the Atlantic to the Caribbean Sea, separates on that side Hayti from the Island of Porto Rico, the distance between them being about the same breadth as the Windward Passage—twenty-five miles. It will thus be seen that Hayti occupies the midway between Cuba and Porto Rico both of which are Spanish dependencies, and that if that territory, these three islands would be in possession of the same power, and would command all the entrances to the Caribbean Sea, and all the States of Central America. We have from the latest advices from St. Thomas, the geographical situation of these Islands, and their relative positions to each other, and to us, because intelligence reaches us from Havana to the effect that Spain has accepted the surrender of the Island of Hayti which is known to us as the Dominican Republic, or more popularly still as San Domingo, and is about to re-annex it to the Spanish Crown. There is nothing at all improbable in this report, though it is not unlikely—if it should turn out to be true—that the fact of this reassertion of an authority which had been abandoned for nearly half a century, will lead to grave complications with this government. The history of San Domingo is the history of nearly all the Spanish possessions on this continent. Discovered by COLUMBUS in 1492, and claimed and held by right of discovery, Hispaniola, San Domingo, or Hayti, which are the names by which the Island has been known, continued under the exclusive dominion of Spain until 1697, when the Western part of it—that which is now styled Hayti—was ceded by the treaty of Ryswick to France. The remaining part, known as the Dominican Republic, continued a Spanish colony until 1795, when it too was ceded by the treaty of Basle to France, who thus acquired possession of the whole Island. It did not long remain under French rule. Shortly after the annexation, the negroes of the Western part rose in insurrection under TOUSSAINT L'OUVERTURE, and after a long and bloody war, and a series of massacres, scarcely paralleled anywhere, succeeded in either murdering or expelling all the white population, and in establishing a monarchy in that which then became, of the Island, under CHRISTOPHE, and a Republic in the South-West, under PETION. In 1808 the Dominicans, oppressed by the tyranny of DESSALINES, called upon Spain for assistance, who seized upon the opportunity which was thus presented to her, to regain possession of her former territory, but held it but for a brief period. The following year the Dominicans, again rising against the Spanish, declared their independence, expelled the Spanish Governor, General REAL, and established a republican form of government. The Island continued divided, for some years, into three separate and distinct principalities—a Republic, under PETION, in the South-West; the monarchy, under CHRISTOPHE, in the North-West, and the Dominican Republic, under various chiefs, in the East. This distinction was kept up until after the death of PETION, in 1818, when the popularity of BOYER enabled him to unite all the governments under one rule, for which himself being chosen President of the Republic for life. In 1844 the negroes of what is now the Republic of Hayti, obtaining the ascendancy, condemned the President to exile, and established a Republic of their own, fearing the expulsion or utter extermination of the Spanish population, which had so long existed, and, succeeding in maintaining their independence, chose GUERRIER, and, shortly after, PIERROT, for their President. His term of office expiring in 1846, RICHE was elected, but died, and SOULOUQUE, in 1847, was chosen to fill the vacant seat. It was not long after he had attained to power, before he began to manifest his hostility to the Dominicans, and in 1849 he led an army of 10,000 men against them, but was again encountered and defeated by SANTANA, in a well-pitched battle. The independence of San Domingo was thus secured, and SOULOUQUE, having assigned his Dictatorship in favor of BAEZ, retired from the field. In 1853, however, BAEZ becoming unpopular, by reason of his favoritism, was forced to leave the country, and SANTANA was elected to the Presidency in 1853; but, four years after, BAEZ returned, and SANTANA was in his turn exiled. BAEZ, however, had not been more than six months in power, before the general dissatisfaction with his policy led, in 1858, to a popular rising against him, and SANTANA, who had become the leader of the movement, was again restored to power. Theoretically, the Dominicans are as free as the people of the United States, with whose Constitution that of San Domingo is modeled. The laws in relation to the administration of justice are singularly liberal. "Free farms, farming utensils, and provisions for six months," are offered to settlers, and no distinction, as in Hayti, is made in favor of colored persons. These laws, it is now alleged, Spain has, for some years past, been taking advantage of, to introduce, under the auspices of President SANTANA, large bodies of persons from Cuba, who, with the view of promoting Spanish interests, and of "preparing the way of the Spanish dominion," on the first sign of resistance on the part of the "native population." The plan is said to have succeeded. This disaffection of the mixed breeds, we are told, became gradually so menacing, that, on the 16th of last month, Spanish troops from Porto Rico were on board the vessels of war in the harbor; the Spanish flag was hoisted on the fortress of the Capital, and, at the last advices from Havana, the details of the formal surrender, and reinforcements of troops, had been despatched to support the movement which has so successfully inaugurated, and which, if report speaks true, is designed to re-establish the authority of Spain in Hispaniola, the most ancient of all her South American dependencies. We forbear from making any comment, at this time, upon a movement which may have been misunderstood, and concerning which we have as yet but imperfect details. Until we know more with regard to the matter, the foregoing résumé of the history of the Island will serve, perhaps, to enlighten some of our readers, and will certainly enable us, on any further occasion, to render better understood such remarks as we may have occasion to make upon the subject.
