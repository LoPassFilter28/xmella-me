[From the Madrid Epoca, Dec. 18.]

In consequence of the effect produced upon the public mind by this announcement, Sir John Crampton, the English Ambassador at this Court, felt bound to request explanations from his government upon a matter in which he was without official instructions. Earl Russell lost no time in replying, and his despatch reached the English representatives yesterday evening, at the same time that the First Secretary of State received the despatches from our Minister Plenipotentiary in London, previously announced by telegraph to the Spanish Cabinet. These despatches contained ample details of all that had occurred. On account of certain questions arising from events that have taken place in consequence of the blockade of the Dominican coasts by the Spanish squadron, the English government considered it necessary to take the opinion of the legal advisers of the crown, following its practice in all matters of this kind. Their opinion was favorable in a certain manner to the recognition of the Dominicans as belligerents—Spain being bound, according to their view, by the maritime law laid down in the treaty of Paris, to maintain a real and effective blockade of all the points of the island where she undertook to prevent the entry of supplies destined for the rebels. Earl Russell communicated this opinion to our representative in London, who in his turn considered it urgently necessary to acquaint the Spanish government with the same. Hence the origin of a news which, having come to the knowledge of the telegraphic agents of various periodicals in this city, was transmitted by them with the urgency which its importance dictated, although the want of the details above mentioned caused some exaggeration in their communications. The reply of Earl Russell to the English Ambassador could not be expressed in more satisfactory terms. The English Minister confirmed the explanations given by Senor Comyn, and, wishing to afford a proof of the friendly attitude of England in questions so greatly affecting the prestige of our country, he authorized the English Ambassador to acquaint the Spanish Cabinet with his determination to indefinitely postpone any act which might be considered hostile, or as tending to exercise any sort of influence in the settlement of our internal affairs. We, who cannot be accused of being ardent admirers of the English policy, have satisfaction in declaring that its conduct in the present circumstances has been worthy of a great Power, and therefore merits our most sincere eulogies.
