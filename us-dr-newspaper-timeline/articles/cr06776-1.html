The Haytian Difficulty.

For some months past there has been going on in Hayti a quarrel between two rival faction, each claiming the right to govern the republic. Outside of the merchants and ship owners who do business in West Indian and Haytian waters and who have been rendered anxious as to the safety of their vessels and property now in Haytian ports or bound for that region, the rebellion attracted little attention in this country until the seizure and confiscation of the Haytian Republic, a Boston steamer, for alleged running the blockade at the port of St. Marc on the west coast of the island, occurred. Aside from the affront to the national dignity, hostile action of this sort is a serious matter to Americans interested in shipping and mercantile transactions with ports under the Haytian government's control. Last May figures submitted to Congress showed that the population of Hayti was 800,000, its imports amounted to $4,121,162, and its exports to $6,271,475, according to the official statistics for 1886. Since that time our trade with the country has doubtless increased as the result of the passage of the act of May 24, which invited closer commercial relations between South American West Indian States and the United States.

The present trouble between the Haytian and United States governments was begun on November 5, when the Haytian consul at New York waited upon the collector of the port and requested that official to send a revenue cutter down the bay and detain the steamship Saginaw, which sailed for Dominican ports with, as the consul alleged, a cargo of arms and ammunition for the insurgents, consigned to Haytian ports. A cutter was despatched as requested, but the vessel had gone too long and was out at sea long before the cutter got down to Sandy Hook and the chase was fruitless. A few days prior to this the Haytian Republic, the steamer above referred to, had sailed for Port au Prince. The vessel is now seized and held by the Haytian government as a prize, it being claimed by the government that she carried contraband of war and had aided the insurgents, not only by supplying them with munitions of war, but by the transportation of rebel troops from Cape Haytian to Gonaives, where, it is alleged by the Haytian government, they were landed, and the same day met and defeated the government troops, after a short and decisive battle, lasting about four hours. The story of the seizure of the steamer and of the gallant conduct of her commander in refusing to lower the American flag and deliver the steamer over to the Haytians has already been published.

The owners of the Saginaw deny that their steamer carried any supplies for the insurgents and say that the ship was not consigned to Haytian ports at all, but to ports under the Dominican government where she landed her cargo, and claim that their connection and responsibility therewith entirely ceased when the goods were over the ship's side, the ultimate destination of the goods being none of their business. One of the agents of the Haytian Republic, speaking of the allegation made by the Haytian authorities that the steamer had been engaged in the transportation of rebel troops, said:

Our vessels, in the regular course of their voyages, call at several ports where they both receive and discharge cargo, and take on board or land such passengers as may be desirous of travelling by our line, the same as is done by any line of steamships in the world. We do not make it our business to inquire of such persons from whence they come, where they are going, or what is their object in travelling. In the case of the alleged rebel carried by our vessel, it was not within Captain Compton's province to inquire of these alleged rebels their business. They paid their passage in the usual way and as far as reports received by us show, behaved themselves in such a manner on our vessel as to give rise to no suspicions on the part of the ship's officers that there was any illegality or wrong-doing connected with the object of their voyage.

With reference to the alleged blockade, for violating which the Haytian authorities seized and condemned the steamer, it is claimed that it was invalid by reason of the absence of due notice to neutral powers, and because of its manifest inadequacy. Furthermore, it was, and is, nothing more than a paper blockade of the northern ports of the little republic by a provisional government which holds the southern half of the country, and asserts that the government that controls the northern half is insurrectionary in character. The appearance of our fleet in Haytian waters will doubtless result in the release of the seized steamer unless it has already been burned. If such is found to be the case the fullest compensation will be demanded and the demand will be enforced. As the Haytian government has only two small gunboats available our ships of war are not likely to have a very hard task. The officers and sailors are more likely to rejoice at the prospect before them, for the weather down there is said to be delightful at this season of the year.
