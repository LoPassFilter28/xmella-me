The Dearborn independent argued that an immediate withdrawal of American marines from Haiti would quickly lead to revolution and governmental collapse. The article contrasted the French colonial period's agricultural prosperity with the nation's condition following Toussaint L'Ouverture's uprising and Jean-Jacques Dessalines's 1804 declaration of independence. It asserted that more than a century of post-independence conflict and despotic leadership had ruined the country's plantations and civil institutions.

El Dearborn independent sostuvo que una retirada inmediata de los infantes de marina estadounidenses de Haití conduciría rápidamente a la revolución y al colapso gubernamental. El artículo contrastó la prosperidad agrícola del período colonial francés con la situación de la nación tras la sublevación de Toussaint L'Ouverture y la declaración de independencia de 1804 de Jean-Jacques Dessalines. Aseguró que más de un siglo de conflictos y caudillismos despóticos posteriores a la independencia habían arruinado las plantaciones y las instituciones civiles del país.

It is frequently said that a revolution would follow the departure of our occupational forces by a few days and the Marines would scarcely be away long enough to settle down on their battleships before trouble would necessitate their return. For more than a century the Haytian mass have followed the African instinct back to barbarism. Previous to 1804 by fifty years their land under the French had become the richest area in the West Indies. Great plantations of sugar and coffee covered the land. Beautiful cities, built after the French plan, graced the seaports, which were constantly filled with craft carrying the products to Europe. Napoleon looked upon his West Indian possession as the richest under the flag of France. When Toussaint L'Ouverture, an ex-slave, led a revolt against the French land and slave owners in 1798, there started a period of terror which ended in 1804 when Dessalines, the successor of L'Ouverture stood on the seashore at Gonaives, tore the French tricolor into three pieces, cast away the white and ordered the red and blue bars to be fastened together as the flag of independent Hayti. The whites had been expelled. Generations of despotism, revolution and strife have followed. The great plantations of the French, their rich architectural dwellings both in city and country have gone the way of ruin. The ruling class one finds today in Hayti is gathered in the cities.
