A correspondent describes his ship's arrival at Samana, sighting land on the morning of the 24th, tracing the mountain range westward from Cape Cabron, and entering the bay past hills, palm groves and small settlements to Santa Barbara de Samana. The ship anchored in the outer harbor about two miles from the village, where the American flag floated over the property leased by the United States and the steamer Nantasket lay, and the commissioners landed to visit the local authorities.

Un corresponsal describe la llegada de su barco a Samaná, el avistamiento de tierra en la mañana del 24, el seguimiento de la cordillera hacia el oeste desde el cabo Cabrón, y la entrada a la bahía pasando colinas, palmerales y pequeños poblados hasta Santa Bárbara de Samaná. El barco ancló en el puerto exterior, a unas dos millas del pueblo, donde la bandera americana ondeaba sobre la propiedad arrendada por los Estados Unidos y se encontraba el vapor Nantasket, y los comisionados desembarcaron para visitar a las autoridades locales.

Thick clouds hung heavily over a dark line about two points off our starboard bow, hardly discernible at first; but as we came nearer we gradually traced the outline of the mountain range which stretched westward to the mainland from Cape Cabron at the extremity of the peninsula of Samana. Behind this mountain barrier and opening to the east lies the bay to which we were bound. The morning was the hottest we had yet felt. A burning sun blistered the deck. The brass work of the frigate glowed like fire, and the waters glittered like burnished metal under the blazing heavens. There was hardly a breath of air, and as we steamed through the calm sea the sails hung useless from the yards. Long before noon the whole country around the mouth of the bay was distinctly visible in outline. There were the hills rising abruptly close to the shore, the palisade-like rocks almost surrounding the peninsula, the irregular and picturesque profile of the range stretching back as far as the eye could reach. We could discover no table-land; we saw no beach; and at first there was no sign of life except in the waters, from which a flying fish darted now and then; nor was there much appearance of vegetation. It was not until we had rounded Cape Cabron and entered the mouth of the bay that the dingy aspect of the landscape changed, and we realized something of that tropical luxuriance and freshness upon which travellers have loved to dilate.
