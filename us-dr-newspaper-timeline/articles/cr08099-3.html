The correspondent describes sailing into Samana Bay on the 24th, rounding Cape Cabron to find Santa Barbara de Samana coming into view, and anchoring in the outer harbour near the United States steamer Nantasket, with the American flag flying over the property leased by the United States. Ashore the next day, the party inspected the town's few public buildings, its Catholic church, a Methodist church being built, and schools whose pupils include descendants of the coloured colony sent here from Philadelphia, whose textbooks are all in English.

El corresponsal describe la entrada en la bahía de Samaná el día 24, doblando el cabo Cabrón hasta ver aparecer Santa Bárbara de Samaná, y el fondeo en el puerto exterior cerca del vapor americano Nantasket, con la bandera americana ondeando sobre la propiedad arrendada por los Estados Unidos. Al día siguiente en tierra, el grupo inspeccionó los pocos edificios públicos del pueblo, su iglesia católica, una iglesia metodista en construcción, y escuelas cuyos alumnos incluyen descendientes de la colonia de color enviada aquí desde Filadelfia, cuyos libros de texto están todos en inglés.

It was not until we had rounded Cape Cabron and entered the mouth of the bay that the dingy aspect of the landscape changed, and we realized something of that tropical luxuriance and freshness upon which travellers have loved to dilate. In the distance, we soon descried, peeping above the horizon, the roofs of Santa Barbara de Samana, and little by little the whole scene broke upon us in its delicious beauty. One by one appeared the spreading tops of the palms. The brown hue of the hills changed to the most brilliant shades of green, their sides showed here and there a little patch of farm-land shut in by waving forest, and over all the country was a mantle of that soft, beautiful verdure which we never see at home except after the showers of early summer. We could appreciate now the rapture with which Columbus (and we were sailing so they say in the very track of the discoverer) painted the beauties of this island paradise. Close to the shore, in a little gully through which a stream of water ran singing to the sea, stood a hut, with round thatched roof and a single window looking out upon the ocean. Waving palms shaded it in front and rear; blooming hills sheltered it from the winds, and close by was a huge ragged rock against which the lazy surf beat perpetual music. This perfect picture was succeeded by views of larger houses, with patches of cleared land and groves of cocoonuts and bananas.
