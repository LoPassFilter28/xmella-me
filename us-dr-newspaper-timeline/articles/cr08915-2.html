would [fail?] me to handle this important subject in all its details and in which the interests of the two countries are so much involved, but I will as briefly as possible present a few facts which, I hope, will go far toward explaining away a great many misrepresentations and misstatements in connection with the commerce of the two countries. San Domingo, which in my judgment is the very finest island in the Antilles or New World, is little known to the majority of our people, although it is the first island upon which Columbus, the great discoverer, founded a city in the New World; and, strange to say, it is the only Republic on this continent in the group of islands outside of Hayti which maintains its sovereignty free from all encumbrances of foreign or European dictation. Although when first discovered this beautiful island contained an inhabitance of several millions of people, yet to-day the Dominican Republic, with its vast area of territory, with its fertile and unexplored forests of valuable woods and minerals, has a population of less than four hundred thousand. Its commercial relations with the United States are little known, as I have already stated, to a majority of our people. San Domingo is often considered in the columns of our journals as a revolutionary and barbarous country. This is an error; they have their periodical political troubles, and sometimes they resort to the sword or to war to settle these difficulties; but these revolutions are less destructful to life and property than our labor strikes.
