The Springfield Weekly Republican reported on the extensive modernization of the Dominican Republic under President Rafael Trujillo over a sixteen-year span achieved without foreign loans. Improvements included a twenty percent decrease in illiteracy, hundreds of newly built schools, seventy new bridges, and irrigation projects reclaiming 85,000 acres. The article noted that the rebuilt capital, renamed Ciudad Trujillo by citizens, was growing into a major Caribbean tourist destination.

El Springfield Weekly Republican reportó las profundas transformaciones de progreso material logradas por la República Dominicana bajo el mandato de dieciséis años del presidente Rafael Trujillo, realizadas sin necesidad de préstamos extranjeros. Los adelantos abarcaban una reducción del veinte por ciento en el analfabetismo, centenares de escuelas recién levantadas, setenta puentes nuevos e infraestructuras de regadío que recuperaron 85.000 acres de tierra. El informe agregaba que la reconstruida capital, rebautizada Ciudad Trujillo por la población, se afianzaba como un emporio turístico caribeño de primer orden.

Trujillo's Accomplishments Yet the task of modernizing the birthplace of the New World was accomplished in the brief span of 16 years without once resorting to foreign loans. Illiteracy dropped 20 per cent due to the building of hundreds of new schools and the allotment of 13 per cent of the national budget to furthering education. Beautifully paved roads and 70 new bridges put formerly isolated regions into easy communication with one another and with the capital. Vast irrigation projects brought 85,000 acres of land under cultivation. Factories employ twice as many people and produce three times as many exportable items as previously. New hospitals. clothing manufacture, water works, schools and public buildings transformed an ancient city of ruins into a modern metropolis. Little wonder that a grateful people over-rode the protests of their president and gave Santo Domingo the name it bears today—Ciudad Trujillo. Home of Columbus The Dominican Republic occupies the eastern three-quarters of the island of Santo Domingo- an area slightly larger than Kentucky. Ciudad Trujillo, its capital, straddles the mouth of the Ozama river and is only four hours by air from Miami. The city threatens to rival Rio de Janiero, Havana and Miami as a year-round playground for American tourists.
