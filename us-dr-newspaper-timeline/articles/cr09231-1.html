The fact is believed to be simply that the President was authorised in 1851 to negotiate for a loan, but that such a loan would require the sanction of the Legislature, and that it is doubtful if this could be obtained for anything like the amount named. At the same time, it is impossible to comprehend the purpose for which it can be required. The depreciated paper circulation might be bought up for £30,000, and the idea of a State with only 200,000 inhabitants, and scarcely yet sure of its independence, or secure from the domestic intrigues peculiar to all races of Spanish origin, taking upon itself a debt of £760,000 simply to promote enterprise and immigration, is preposterous. The interest and sinking fund upon this would be nearly £60,000 per annum, and the entire revenue of the republic is supposed not to exceed £60,000. If the country, after a war with Haiti, and experiencing financial difficulties, should be in a condition to need an advance, it is not such an exaggeration as is likely to take place at present; and, as far as regards the promoters of enterprise, it will be enough if the English public deal with any specific project that may be brought forward, after a due examination of their merits. That many such exist there can be no doubt, and as Samana, cases will from time to time present themselves cannot be questioned. If the country were in a settled state, its situation on the highway from Europe to Nicaragua, together with its natural resources, it must be admitted, that an abundant supply of bituminous coal, and a soil of the highest fertility, would scarcely fail from this period to insure its rapid progress. Whatever may be the real prospects of its welfare, they are certainly not such as to lead to any expectation that the annual interest on £760,000 could be met, or that such a loan, although raised professedly to develope the resources of the country, could operate in any other way than to corrupt and encumber it.
