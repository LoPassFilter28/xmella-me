The steamer George W. Clyde, Captain Holmes, arrived from Quarantine early yesterday morning, bringing the latest news from Hayti and San Domingo. An interesting story of her adventures and experiences was gathered from her passengers, among whom were Thomas Clyde, the Rev. Mr. Moran, Charles Elliott, of Norfolk, Va.; Mr. Munoz, ex-Purser Nells, of the Clyde, and C. Kroncke y Rebatet, of Vera Cruz. The voyage southward was unusually severe. A cyclone struck the vessel, which dashed the seas about furiously and tossed the steamer about as though she were feather-ballasted. The waves rolled over the deck, carrying with them the starboard rail; the skylights were broken and the staterooms and saloon were flooded with water several feet deep. The gale lasted several days, and it is believed that the Samana went down during the raging storm. She weathered the tempest, however, and arrived at San Domingo City on December 8, steaming thence for Samana, where she arrived on December 15. She touched at Puerto Plata December 17, and reached Monte Cristi on December 18, where she loaded a cargo of logwood.

Shortly after anchoring at Monte Cristi two men-of-war were sighted, which upon nearer approach proved to be the Haytian gunboats Toussaint l'Ouverture and Manzel, owned by the Riviere Steamship Company, which gets a subsidy of $100,000 for their use. These were keeping strict watch of the coast, and were determined to see what the Clyde was about. To accomplish this, two smaller boats left the Toussaint, well manned with sailors and officers, who carried arms. They rowed up near the Clyde and encircled her first from one side and then from the other. During this performance, which surprised the crew of the American steamer, the heavy gun of the Toussaint was pointed in a menacing manner toward her. However, the rowboats returned to the man-of-war. On the evening of December 20 the Clyde, owing to a severe storm which had come up, shifted her position from Manzanillo Bay to President's Bay, which is the dividing line between Hayti and San Domingo. This brought the men-of-war nearer.

Here the roar of cannon and the unmistakable noise of battle coming from the coast of Hayti were distinctly heard on that day. Captain Holmes had occasion to visit a small steamer lying near the coast, thinking that she had orders for him. To do this, he was forced to pass near the Toussaint, from which boats were again sent to intercept the captain's passage. The drum was sounded calling the marines, who stood in fighting array with pointed guns as the officer passed. He was cut off from his course several times by the boats from the men-of-war, but finally reached his destination and returned without further molestation.

On the morning of December 22 a small schooner, the Aurora, flying the Dominican flag, under Captain Wilson, came from Turk's Island, and attempted to go to Cape Haytien. She was intercepted, however, by the Manzel, whose men seized her and took the captain and crew aboard and placed them in irons. They then took the crew of a little wrecked brig which was lying in Dominican waters, and forced them to man the captured Aurora. They were retained at this work only twenty-four hours and then dismissed, and it was from them that the story of the captain, whose wife was also on board, was learned. On the following day, the 23d, the two men-of-war hove anchor and steamed away with the Aurora in tow. They were afterward seen through glasses bearing off toward Cape Haytien, where it is supposed they were waiting to see if the Clyde attempted to run the blockade. But before going they seized several sailing-boats which attempted to land near Manzanillo Bay. They were carrying on a genuine system of piracy in the interest of Legitime, the Haytians having declared, too, that they would sink any vessel belonging to the Clyde line. It is supposed that the seizure of the brig Aurora will even create trouble between San Domingo and Hayti.

At present, however, contrary to other reports, one of the passengers, a merchant, who came directly from the island, where he received large orders, says that perfect harmony exists between the two. The people of San Domingo are well satisfied with their President, General Heraux, their crops are unusually good, and prosperous times are anticipated. "During my visit," continued the merchant, "the General made no reference to Hayti. The report that he did not receive the deputation sent from Hayti to protest against the landing of arms in San Domingo for the north of Hayti is false. On the contrary, they were received with all honor, and were perfectly satisfied with their success. A reception and fireworks were given in their honor. The vessel El Pacificador, which the Dominican Government recently purchased from England, is to be used as a dispatch-boat along the coast, and not for warlike purposes."

FIGHTING BATTLES WITH CHAMPAGNE

Another passenger, now at the Hotel America, who spent several weeks in San Domingo City before embarking on the Clyde, tells a somewhat different tale. He said: "In the Hotel Philipo, where I was staying, in San Domingo City, was the delegate from the north of Hayti who had been sent to get the privilege of landing arms in the Dominican territory and transmitting them thence to the region which is faithful to Hyppolite. Although he would not speak of the result of his mission, it was the general opinion that his constant good humor and haughty boast that Hyppolite would gain supreme control of Southern Hayti before the end of December were indicative of its success. A few days after his arrival a man-of-war, painted white, whose name I do not remember, appeared in the harbor and landed a delegation from Port-au-Prince, which had come to protest against unrestricted transmission of arms and other articles to the north of Hayti on the part of San Domingo. It so happened that they entered our hotel one day while we were at dinner and seated themselves at an neighboring table. Here they ordered beer and began to drink. The Ambassador from the North became excited over their presence and determined to outdo them. He ordered champagne for all who were in the room, and a general demonstration in favor of Hyppolite was made, during which a Frenchman made a toast to the General. The Ambassador from Legitime soon departed in downcast spirits. During the remainder of their sojourn their spirits did not rise, and it was the supposition that their mission was in vain. While in San Domingo, I heard that 15,000 rifles and 600 kegs of powder had been landed there for Hyppolite and his followers. Indeed, from what I learned, the sympathies of the people of San Domingo are entirely with the North, though probably not strongly enough to lead them to take up arms in their behalf. I met a man who had been on board the Haytien Republic when she was seized. He was taken to Port-au-Prince, where he was imprisoned. He escaped from that city, and reached San Domingo City in safety. He then met a schooner going north, and returned to Cape Haytien, whence he originally came. He was unable to tell much of the state of affairs in Hyppolite's country, and I failed to get from him anything of importance, further than that the insurgent leader has a large and thoroughly equipped army and plenty of funds, and is bound to win. He spoke of the devotion of Hyppolite's soldiers, who, he said, fought with the desperation of madmen, and said that nothing could stay their progress when once fairly started. He said that a letter had been received from a reliable merchant at St. Marc, stating that the Northern troops had possession of nearly all the territory to the north of St. Marc, among them being Verrettes, La Chapelle, Marchand and Dessalines. Rosa was the last of the officers under General Seide Thelemaque who surrendered to General Legitime after September 28, and he is now fighting for him. The letter announces further that the bombardment of Montrouis and St. Marc is expected at any time."

Minister Preston, who is still at the Westminster Hotel, will leave for Boston to-morrow, and cannot, therefore, be in Washington for the President's New Year's reception. "I received a letter from the commodore of the fleet," said the Minister, "in which it is stated that when the Dessalines was off Cape Haytien, on the 3d or 4th inst., Fort Picolet opened fire on her. The battering-guns of the vessel soon silenced the guns of the fort, and she steamed past. When opposite St. Joseph's Battery, which is some distance further inland, she was again shot at, but succeeded in approaching the old French Battery, which is near the Custom House. In returning the fire from this point, a shell dropped near the house of the American Consul, who is said to be responsible for some of the dispatches which have been published. The commander, however, had no orders to bombard Cape Haytien, and simply answered the fire."

C. F. Cavallo, who was reported to have left the city on Saturday by the steamer Ozama, was seen in the streets yesterday. It is said that he left the Ozama at Quarantine. Upon inquiry, it was found that his name was still on the lists at the Hotel Griffou. The reasons for changing his plans could not be learned.

A HAYTIAN PAPER'S ACCOUNT OF IT.

The following is a translation of an extract from "La Verite," of December 22, a paper published at Port-au-Prince:

On Tuesday morning the population of this city were surprised to see an American naval vessel enter the inner harbor. She entered without having a pilot on board or making communication with the land; nor did she fire the usual salute. She immediately steamed alongside of the Haytien Republic and put a garrison of men on board. At the same time another United States naval vessel lay in the outer harbor. This conduct of the Americans surpasses all possible attempts at explanation. They are acting under the doctrine of "might makes right." It is a sad thing that a friendly nation should offer to us an outrageous insult, especially that of the United States; still, we have confidence that a similar humiliation will not be visited upon us again.
