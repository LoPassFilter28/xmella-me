The White Bluffs Spokesman detailed historical evidence verifying that Christopher Columbus's remains rest in the Cathedral of Santo Domingo, citing investigations by American diplomats Thomas C. Dawson, Philander C. Knox, and Charles G. Dawes. The article also described the catastrophic hurricane of September 3, 1930, which struck the Dominican capital, destroying 4,000 homes outside the city walls, damaging 70 percent of structures inside, and killing over two thousand residents.

El White Bluffs Spokesman detalló testimonios históricos que corroboraban que los restos de Cristóbal Colón reposan en la Catedral de Santo Domingo, citando investigaciones de los diplomáticos estadounidenses Thomas C. Dawson, Philander C. Knox y Charles G. Dawes. El artículo describió también el devastador huracán del 3 de septiembre de 1930 que azotó la capital dominicana, destruyendo 4 000 casas fuera de las murallas de la ciudad, dañando el 70 por ciento de los edificios interiores y cobrando la vida de más de dos mil residentes.

was sealed, and in the afternoon the president and his cabinet, the members of the diplomatic corps, the bishops and Apostolic delegate, and many others assembled to witness the completion of the excavation and the opening of the casket. Outside and inside were found inscriptions which bear alike the name and the titles of Christopher Columbus. All present, including even the Spanish consul, joined in a notarial affidavit of the circumstances of the opening of the vault and casket and the description of their contents. The late American minister, Thomas C. Dawson, pronounced the evidence complete, and the late American secretary of state, Philander C. Knox, on his visit to Santo Domingo in 1912 declared that any impartial court would sustain the contention that all that is mortal of the Founder of the New World rests within the Cathedral at Santo Domingo. Charles G. Dawes, United States ambassador to Great Britain, while in Santo Domingo in 1929 as the head of a commission to work out a budget system for the Dominican government, made a study of the evidence and reached the same conclusion as Minister Dawson and Secretary Knox. One of the most tragic experiences in the history of Santo Domingo occurred in the afternoon of September 3, 1930, when a hurricane swept over the capital. Outside the walls of the stricken city the devastation was practically complete; inside the walls 70 per cent of the buildings were damaged and practically all of the 4,000 smaller homes were destroyed.
