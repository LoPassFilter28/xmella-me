ST. DOMINGO.

The steamer Tybee, Captain Delanoy, from St. Domingo the 16th and Porto Plata the 19th, arrived here yesterday. She brings letters and papers up to date of sailing. The compliments of the purser are acknowledged.

Political Affairs—The Position of President Baez—What Is Thought of Annexation—European Influence at Work.

ST. DOMINGO, Nov. 16, 1869.

There is a party here so deeply implicated in the interior complication of the Dominican republic and so well situated to convey false statements to the foreign press that it is nearly impossible for the simple truth to obtain a hearing. For example, it went from here, on "consular" authority and endorsement, that the important and central town of Santiago had declared against Baez and that this government was on the eve of downfall. Your correspondent happened to be in company with the very general who was named as the leader of this fictitious revolution, and at that very date he was on his way to this city with a committee of the leading men of La Vega, Santiago and Puerte Plata, who came to this city to congratulate President Baez on the advance of public industry "which had resulted from the perfect tranquillity which for two years has reigned throughout the country." "We do not count the Caco guerillas on the frontier as a serious disturbance of the public peace." General Luccano went on to say although the labors and the vigilance demanded for their repression has caused the government some inconvenience, as everywhere in the great body of our territory enterprise has taken root and the general products of the country largely increased.

Speaking of enterprises, the proposal for introducing 2,000 German emigrants in the coffee and mining regions is not yet acted upon, but it most probably will be, as it is believed that M. Landais, the French Consul, gives it his warm though secret support, and he is a strong man with the Baez Cabinet, and is also a man of liberal ideas. It is reported, or rather whispered cautiously inside the consular ring, that Mr. Martin Hood, the former British Consul here, is to be sent back "to cure the Dominicans of the annexation fever." He is and was of old very obnoxious to the whole Baez party, for various personal as well as political reasons, and some members of the Senate have advised the President in advance not to receive him officially.

Major K. H. Perry, the newly appointed United States Consul, arrived in the Tybee last week, and raises his flag to-day. He was warmly welcomed by Baez.

I have heretofore attempted to explain the curious triangular antagonism of Baez, Cabral and Pimentel, each of whom accuses the other of an "intrigue to dismember the republic by selling its most precious section"—the peninsula of Samaná—to the United States, and all of whom are, more or less, openly working and praying for annexation.

The proposition of the English Company for a free port at Manzanilla with all the public lands within ten leagues of it for colonization, is probably a movement to head off annexation. The proposal is to establish the Germans, "or other European immigrants" in parties of thirty families, in a continuous chain of settlements, giving to each man a three acre block in the village or a twenty acre farm in its vicinity. These villages, beginning at their fine seaport of Manzanilla and taking in some sites with fine water power for manufacturing purposes, would extend to the old gold mines of San Rafael, which the French and Spanish governments agreed should be closed about a century ago. The promoters of this colonization project forget that German immigrants to America are almost invariably intensely loyal to the land of their adoption and would be more likely to swell than to oppose the vote for annexation. They also forget when debating on the "unwieldy expansion" of the United States—a favorite European phrase—that the telegraph and railways have compacted our national limits into much firmer and more manageable bounds than the Union exhibited at the beginning of this century, before either Florida or the States of the lower Mississippi were added to the old thirteen. Baez himself put it rather neatly at the introduction of the new United States Consul, Major Perry, to the Ministers and consular corps, at the President's morning reception. "San Francisco is now very much nearer the government at Washington," he observed, "than Mobile or New Orleans was forty years past, and all these islands are within easier reach of the commerce (he did not say arms, but he thought it) of all its seaboard States than those of the Atlantic with those of the Gulf of Mexico or either of them with the States of the Pacific."

This remark shows that Baez has looked at the map and reflected upon the beauties of a free exchange of products.

The Tybee takes on 3,000 bales of tobacco in transit for Europe, that is if the horrible roads will allow of getting down to port in time.

The London Alta Vela Company have made their arrangements for taking out from 1,000 to 5,000 tons of guano per month. The works started by the Baltimore firm, which Spain turned adrift so unceremoniously, come in very nicely for the London company. It pays a royalty of five dollars per ton on all the guano exported, and the magnitude of its arrangements is the best proof of the value of the American discovery. There can be no mistake about it, for, as I wrote you at the time, Mr. Lawson, one of the present owners, made a careful exploration of the island, accompanied by a chemist and other guano savans, with laborers and all other appliances for a thorough examination. On this he offers a royalty of one pound per ton and takes the island.

It is hardly worth the space to say that for the last six weeks the Cacos have had an uncomfortable time on the Dominican frontier. Two of their officers—Curiel and Volla—have been captured, and their great Dominican leader, the brave but misguided General Cabral, narrowly escaped by leaving his horse and throwing himself down an almost impracticable ravine.

These border raids are amplified into undue importance by the St. Thomas and Jamaica press, out of spite for Baez's supposed intention to cede Samaná to the United States for a naval station, which, of course, is regarded as the entering wedge of annexation.

No American vessel in port except the steamer Tybee. Europe does all the business here.
