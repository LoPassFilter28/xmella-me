New-York Tribune

AS MIGHT HAVE BEEN EXPECTED.

The Samana Bay Company have succeeded as ill as President Grant in their efforts at the annexation of Santo Domingo. They have failed, however, from contrary reasons to those which caused the defeat of the Babcock treaty. An unusual conscientiousness induced the Senate to reject that instrument, and a far from unusual lack of conscience has instigated Señor Gonzales to abrogate his predecessor's concession to the American Company. An attempt will be made by the projectors to involve the Government in their quarrel, and this attempt will be met according to the logical habit of a portion of the press by efforts to defeat this scheme through abuse of the enterprise and those interested in it. We see no necessity for siding with either party in this matter. When the enterprise was set on foot, we rendered full justice to the motives and purposes of the capitalists who were endeavoring in this way to open up a new highway of commerce. But while we saw nothing objectionable in their scheme and hoped for its success, we could not refrain from the prediction that sooner or later the exigencies of Dominican politics would give rise to trouble which would call for the intervention of the United States, and that this would be refused, no matter under what pretext it was asked. The gentlemen who went into this business were not born yesterday. They knew what risks they were taking. They knew the character of those ex-officio brigands who make their living out of revolutions in that island. It was the business of the buyers to look carefully at their bargain, and not to look to the Government to avenge them in case they were cheated. They have no more right to ask the Government to defend them against the scoundrels who are trying to rob them.

It is not worth while to complicate the question by bringing in the supposed influence of the British Government in inducing Gonzales to take his recent course. It was an old maxim of art that a god should not be brought out of the flies, unless the matter on the stage were important enough to call for his assistance. In this case the cruise of the Woodlark is not a necessary explanation of Gonzales's action. We need not look any further than the dictator's cabinet for a motive. No one suspects Señor Baez of any very lofty motives in making the original concession. When he and Captain-General Gonzales arranged the recent farcical revolution by which he gave up the Government to him, there is no question that the annual rental of Samana Bay was the principal resource the new rulers looked to for furnishing their empty treasury. But it is stated that Gonzales did on arriving at his capital warn the Company not to pay their rent to anybody else, and to assure them of his friendly regard. While they were waiting for official information of his inauguration, which did not come, and were naturally hesitating about paying $150,000 to a usurper who might be driven out to-morrow, he took their silence for a refusal or for a notice, and they allowed his drafts to be protested. After this, nothing he could do ought to surprise them. In common with statesmen of his class, if he has no money, he has no quantity of any dignity. He needed no further incentive, from Consul-General St. John, the British Government, or anybody else, to resent the prudent suspicions of the Yankees, and to answer them by an act as public and as thorough as possible.

The cause of the Company's failure was inherent in this enterprise from the beginning. They had no right to expect honest and fair dealing from the Dominican Government, and it was not reasonable to hope for any permanency in the occupants of power at Santo Domingo. Their scheme was in most respects an admirable one, but it has been mismanaged from the start. It could only have succeeded by making a sufficient force of Americans on the place against the greedy and treacherous "statesmen" who made the grant. This was the only course to adopt if the enterprise was a genuine one. If it was merely intended for a piece of stock jobbing, it might as well die now as later, and in either case it has no just claim that we can see to come upon the United States for protection or indemnification.
