Writing from Santiago de los Caballeros on March 4, the correspondent describes his eight-day journey across St. Domingo from Samana, three days of it in a canoe slowly ascending the Yuna River from Santa Barbara to Almacen. On the way the party spent a night in hammocks at Bebedero and joined a marriage feast at a house near Santa Capuza, with music, dancing, tiny cups of coffee and revolver shots fired in honour of the event, before entering the small mouth of the Yuna at midday.

Escribiendo desde Santiago de los Caballeros el 4 de marzo, el corresponsal describe su viaje de ocho días a través de Santo Domingo desde Samaná, tres de ellos en una canoa que remontaba lentamente el río Yuna desde Santa Bárbara hasta Almacén. En el camino el grupo pasó una noche en hamacas en Bebedero y se unió a una fiesta de boda en una casa cerca de Santa Capuza, con música, baile, tacitas de café y disparos de revólver en honor del acontecimiento, antes de entrar al mediodía por la boca pequeña del Yuna.

It was midday When we entered the Boca [Chica?] or small mouth of the Yuna, the river on which the Samana Bay Company expect to put a steamer one of these days to drain the trade of the La Vega country. There is a bar at this mouth and a bar at the other, a short distance off the Boca Grande or large mouth, and the depth of water over each is about two feet and a half. The shores at the entrance, covered with vegetation, are exceedingly low, not more than a few inches above the water, disappearing altogether when the river is full. Here the festive alligator may be found and the mud he loves, for his pallet is spread over endless acres. A few hundred feet within the mouth of the river the depth is three feet, which is retained throughout all the tortuous windings of this stream to the village of Almasin, thirty-three miles' sailing distance, or seventeen as the bird flies.
