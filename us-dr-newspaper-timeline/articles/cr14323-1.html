The Anaconda Standard published an overview of the Dominican Republic, detailing its geography, population of 610,000, and history from colonial rule through its independence on February 27, 1844. The report described the nation's political structure, public education system, and major agricultural exports, including tobacco, sugar, coffee, and mahogany. It also observed that while rich mineral deposits of gold, copper, and iron existed, the country's mineral resources remained largely undeveloped.

There are two republics on the island, the republic of Dominica being the larger and comprising about two-thirds of the island, while the republic of Hayti occupies the western end of the island. The island was first settled by the Spanish and was called Hispaniola. Then French buccaneers gained a foothold on it. In 1697 the western part was ceded by Spain to France. In 1800 Toussaint Louverture seized the Spanish part of the island and also stirred up an insurrection in the French part. Through treachery he was enticed to France and died in jail. On February 27, 1844, the Spanish or eastern part of the island threw off allegiance to Spain and formed the republic of Dominica. The republic contains 18,045 square miles and has a population of 610,000. The president is elected for four years, chosen by universal suffrage. He is assisted by a congress of twenty-two deputies elected for two years by popular vote with restricted suffrage. The Spanish language is principally spoken. Education is free and obligatory. The schools are good. The products are mainly tobacco, sugar, rice, coffee, maize, cocoa, bananas, pine apples, sweet potatoes and cotton. Gold, silver, copper, coal, iron and tin exist on the island, but no efforts have been made to develop the mineral resources. Logwood and mahogany are among the principal exports.
