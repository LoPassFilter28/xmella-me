A Suspended Law.

Since 1861 that statute of the United States known as the "Guano act" has been a dead letter, so far as the State Department is concerned. No American citizen, we believe, when he desired to avail himself of the benefit of its provisions, has ever been allowed to invoke them against unlawful interference with his business by any other power.

In order to encourage and build up an extensive and valuable domestic trade, Congress declared that whenever a citizen of the United States should discover guano on any derelict island or key, beyond the maritime jurisdiction of any other nation, he might raise the flag of the United States over it, and proceed to dig and ship the guano to this country under the laws of the coastwise trade. Such islands became by reason of such occupation appendages of the United States, subject to its jurisdiction, and entitled to its protection. They were registered in the Treasury Department, upon certain proofs of the facts, and the Treasury certificate was a conclusive and final adjudication in favor of the parties making the discovery, and adventuring their capital and labor in the business into which they were expressly invited by the public policy of the nation.

The "Guano act" was plain. Several times its provisions were submitted to the Attorney-General, and received each time the same interpretation, both as to the municipal law and the public law of the world. In a case with Venezuela it was diplomatically enforced. In a case with Hayti it was enforced by the navy, under instructions of the State Department. But Mr. SEWARD, for reasons of his own, chose to disregard it. American citizens had discovered a rich deposit of guano upon a barren and derelict islet lying in the high seas off the coast of San Domingo. They occupied it peacefully for seven months, had complied with all the conditions of the law, and established a large business. At the end of that time an armed Dominican vessel called, tore down the flag, and carried the men away prisoners. There never was a more flagrant insult to a nation, or a more indefensible outrage upon the rights of our private citizens. But Mr. SEWARD declined to seek any redress, and from that day to this there has been no instance in which the Guano act has been successfully invoked against a foreign aggressor.
