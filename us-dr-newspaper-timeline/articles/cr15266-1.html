The Topeka State Journal reported on President Taft's speech defending his foreign policy against critics of "Dollar Diplomacy," advocating pending treaties with Nicaragua and Honduras modeled on the Dominican arrangement. Taft emphasized that American customs collection in Santo Domingo had ensured debt repayments and fostered national reconstruction. He countered Senate objections that such treaties created entangling alliances for American banking interests rather than promoting regional stability.

The Topeka State Journal informó sobre el discurso del presidente Taft en defensa de su política exterior frente a las críticas a la «diplomacia del dólar», promoviendo los tratados pendientes con Nicaragua y Honduras diseñados bajo el modelo del arreglo dominicano. Taft destacó que la recaudación aduanera estadounidense en Santo Domingo había asegurado la amortización de la deuda y favorecido la reconstrucción nacional. Replicó a las objeciones del Senado que sostenían que tales tratados forjaban alianzas comprometedoras para los intereses de la banca estadounidense en lugar de afianzar la estabilidad regional.

The president spoke in behalf of proposed treaties with Nicaragua and Honduras and vigorously defended the policy of the administration which he said "muck raking journals have labelled Dollar Diplomacy.' Broadly speaking President Taft said the proposed treaties with these countries before the senate would secure for them a financial agent in the United States who would settle their existing debts on a just basis and who would point the road to peace and prosperity to two republics, rich in natural resources but torn by strife and revolution. The president pointed out the efficacy of a similar arrangement with Santo Do mingo where this government collects the customs and sees that foreign debts are paid, exercises a benefit supervision that Mr. Taft said has accrued to the upbuilding of Santo Domingo. Objection to the treaties in the United States senate, the president said, was based largely upon the proposition that they would make entangling alliances with republics in this hemisphere for the promotion of a banking contract since the money for the payment of the debts of the two republics would be furnished by American interests. Another objection discussed by the president was that the Monroe doctrine was sufficient bond between the United States and Latin American countries. "It is objected that this is 'dollar diplomacy,' said the president, "and that we ought to have none of it. Give a dog a bad name and you know what follows.
