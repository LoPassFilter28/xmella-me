Passage summaryResumen del pasaje

The *sun* reviewed Otto Schoenrich's book on Santo Domingo, highlighting the country's lack of infrastructure, with only 144 miles of railroad and inland roads reduced to muddy trails. The excerpt also examined the historical dispute over the burial place of Christopher Columbus between Spain and the Dominican Republic. Schoenrich concluded from 1877 cathedral excavations that Spain had mistakenly removed the remains of Diego Columbus, leaving Christopher Columbus's body in Santo Domingo.

El *sun* reseñó el libro de Otto Schoenrich sobre Santo Domingo, destacando la deficiencia de obras camineras en el país, que contaba con apenas 144 millas de líneas férreas y caminos interiores reducidos a lodazales. El extracto examinó asimismo la histórica disputa entre España y la República Dominicana sobre el sitio de enterramiento de Cristóbal Colón. Schoenrich concluía, sobre la base de las excavaciones efectuadas en la catedral en 1877, que España había retirado por error los restos de Diego Colón, quedando el cuerpo de Cristóbal Colón en Santo Domingo.

"There are only 144 miles of railroad

in the republic, and the other lines of

communication overland can be called

roads only as a matter of courtesy, being

in fact little better than mere trails.

This explains why most of the important

towns arc on the sea coast, where they

can be reached by water. One day when

travelling on the high road near Ia Vega

the author encountered an old man who

was in actual danger of being drowned

in the liquid mud; he took a snapshot of

the victim while he was being extricated

by a good Samaritan who was passing by.

Santo Domingo is not only proud of

having been discovered and acquired by

Christopher Columbus; it also claims to

be his burial place. "The same honor is

claimed by Spain, but a Dominican would

consider it almost treasonable to doubt

the justice of the Dominican claim." That

the body of Columbus was brought from

Spain, where he died, and deposited in

the cathedral in Santo Domingo city,

where it remained until 1795, is unques

tionable. The doubt arises in reference to the

claim of the Spaniards that they removed

the remains to Cuba in 1795, whence they

were taken back to Spain after the Spanish-American

war of 1898. Excavations

made in the Dominican Cathedral in 1877

satisfied the Dominicans that the body

removed by the Spaniards was really that

of Diego Columbus and that the remains

of his father were still in the cathedral

vault.

Mr. Schoenrich reviews the evidence

in detail and concludes that the Domini

can contention is correct. It is sustained
