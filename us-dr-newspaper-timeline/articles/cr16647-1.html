SALE OF SAN DOMINGO.

"No, sir, the United States Government cannot buy San Domingo, no matter what dispatches from Amsterdam or any other place may say. The Republic of San Domingo can't give a clear title to the property, and there you are."

It was F. S. Samuels who spoke, and he spoke what he was talking about. Mr. Samuels is with J. D. Spreckels & Bros. His father, Captain A. Samuels of New York, is the president of the Samana Bay Company of San Domingo.

Mr. Samuels had a morning paper in his hand, and he pointed to a dispatch from Port-au-Prince stating that a treaty had been signed at Santo Domingo to the United States for $3,500,000 for a coaling station.

"Mr. Blaine knows as well as the San Domingo Government does," said Mr. Samuels, "that the United States can get no clear title to that island, or at least to the portion which would be of any practical use to her."

"You mean Samana Bay and the coaling station there?"

"Yes, sir; that is what I mean."

"Will you tell something of the history of the Samana Bay Company?" was asked.

"I will, if you desire it. You see the Samana Bay Company was formed in this country in 1872. It was composed of prominent New York capitalists who obtained a concession from the Dominican Government, making a treaty with it, which was ratified by the people of the republic at a general election. By the terms of this treaty a large portion of Dominican territory was ceded to the company—the peninsula and bay of Samana, which contains the only important harbor on the whole island of San Domingo, or Haiti, as it is called. That was a grant to the company the full control of the peninsula and bay of Samana, and, in fact, a republic of Samana was set up there, it being wholly independent of the republic of San Domingo.

"The property was to be paid for in installments of $150,000 each, to be paid in advance on the first day of each year. Soon after the concession was made and the company was given possession, negotiations and regulations were made with the United States by which for an annual grant of $150,000 the United States was to have the use of the harbor as a coaling station for its men-of-war.

"During the latter part of the first year of the company's possession of San Domingo a suspicious act on the part of an English gunboat was discovered by the representative of Americans at Samana Bay, and to this act and the persistent refusal of the company to allow the navy of Great Britain to obtain the overthrow of the Dominican Government, for the purpose of ousting the company of all its rights under the treaty.

"Part of the original Dominican treaty was that in case of any dispute between the company and the government the matter was to be left to arbitration. Thus far the Dominican Government has refused to refer the disputes to arbitration, although with nearly every administration since 1872 the company has tried to get the United States Government to compel the Dominican Government to keep its contract, but our Government has kept aloof.

"Now, however, the United States has a navy and it is necessary to provide coaling stations for it. It finds that the bay of Samana is the most available point at which to coal its vessels, because of its excellent advantages from a marine point of view. Engineers have declared it to be the best place for a coaling station in the whole West Indies, as it is easily approached without danger from reefs or shoals, while it is easily fortified, and would command all the other islands, so hemmed in are all foreign vessels in passing through the Mona passage. It is in a direct line to the Nicaragua canal and, when that great waterway is opened to commerce, there will be no better point from which it can be defended. The island of Samana is a good place for a war-vessel to lie while awaiting orders, for directly a dispatch is sent it can proceed rapidly to all the islands of the West Indian group.

"Now, under these circumstances it is more than likely that our Government will take some steps towards getting Samana, as it won't retain its coaling station there. But, as I said before, the Dominican Republic can't give a clear title to Samana Bay, for it belongs to the Samana Bay Company, unless some arrangement should now be made with American citizens our Government would be buying a lawsuit. The claims of the company for damages run into the millions—somewhere in the neighborhood of $14,000,000. It has a lobby at Washington, and it is my belief that it will force the Dominican Republic to pay.

"Mr. Blaine appreciates the situation, and I do not think that he would allow an American company to own the property at the same time the United States took possession of Samana Bay, or that he would try to make of it a naval rendezvous without some kind of title, because the republic cannot sell property under a virtually defective title and the claims of American citizens on it.

"The United States has the privilege of maintaining a coaling station there, so long as $150,000 a year is paid to the Dominican Government, and this privilege may be disposed of to our navy.

"The damages set by the Samana Bay Company against San Domingo are $14,000,000. It is out of the question, however, to suppose that the Dominican Republic can ever pay that sum.

"As you see the company has acquired rights to the territory, as well as the supreme importance of the place as a naval station. With these facts in view the company will not readily give up those rights."

"Wait a moment! Has the United States made any effort to buy San Domingo, such as is reported it has as having virtually purchased it?"

"During the past 50 years there have been repeated attempts made by the United States to obtain a naval station in the West Indies. San Domingo was the place sought for. During General Grant's administration the efforts of our Government were directed toward the acquisition. John L. Stephens conceived the notion of the acquisition of San Domingo during the Presidency of Tyler. William Hogan, an ex-Congressman, was sent to the island as secret agent, and he reported in favor of the idea of going under American rule. Hogan was sent back on a second agency, and his report was highly favorable, but political reasons were prevailing at the time and no use of the information thus received.

"Then, J. B. Cazneau, in 1854, was sent to the island by Secretary Marcy, and he made a report that the bay was an excellent place for a naval station, and he further said that it could be easily defended and could command the Mona Passage. No convention was negotiated with the Government of San Domingo for 15 years, when President Grant's administration got an adjacent land for $2,000,000 for 50 years, but the project failed for want of Senate ratification. In 1861 Spain retook the island, but the troops were driven out two years later, and she acknowledged the independence of the island.

"Secretary Seward visited the island in 1867 and took steps toward its acquisition, but his negotiations resulted in nothing.

"President Grant took up the subject in 1869, and made a direct effort toward annexation, but it was opposed by Charles Sumner and failed, Senator Morton being the only person who favored the treaty.

"It would seem, from the strong endeavor which has been made by our Government to acquire in one way or another, gain control of the island, that if it does not belong to private corporations it will have a big bill to settle before it shall finally come into possession."
