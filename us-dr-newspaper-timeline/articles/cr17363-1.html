Increase of Union Feeling—Success of the Federal Officials—Revival of Trade—The Military Governor a Great Favorite with the People—Success of the New Daily Paper—The Norfolk Herald—Change in the Provost Marshal's Office, &c.

It will afford a sincere gratification to all well wishers of the Union cause to notice the gradual, steady return to a healthy state of public sentiment that is now in progress at Norfolk. But a few weeks ago it was difficult for an officer to pass one block and not meet with some expression of disrespect from the citizens, especially the women; but now they seem to take the presence of the federal authorities as a matter of course, and to prefer the good order now reigning in the city, the military occupation has, indeed, been a source of blessing to the people. Under the rebel rule everything was stagnant, and disorders were every day occurrences, and the most arbitrary and cruel persecutions were dealt out to all such as had moral courage enough to stand up for the right, and speak the truth and truth amidst the clamor of lawlessness and traitors.

Business is now fairly opening with northern traders and purchasers; the stores, one by one, begin to show signs of life, and the people are now gradually returning to habits of trade, which may soon be expected, and a large number of the citizens are deploring the folly of the secessionists who have so long persisted and will certainly find that the days of the rebellion are numbered and gone forever.

The manner in which General Viele, the Military Governor, conducts his department, has met with the highest words of the highest praise, and even the secession citizens of the bitterest stamp frankly admit that, while he deals out the strictest justice to all, his deportment is at all times courteous and characteristic of the perfect gentleman.

The weather, which during the early part of last week was extremely hot and oppressive, took a sudden turn on Wednesday night, and, with the exception of the dust, nothing could be more delicious than the climate we are now enjoying. The health of the citizens and the military is excellent.

The new Union paper meets with general favor, and is productive of good. It is called the New Regime, and the kind tone in which the claims of the Union and the dear old flag are presented is well calculated to win over the malcontents. The Dominicans have certainly no reason to complain of her occupation. Indeed, the benefits bestowed have been thus far like the jug handle—all on one side—for poor St. Domingo has had nothing but humble acquiescence and gratitude to render on its part. The officials of her Majesty's government are free to express their avowed desire to foster enterprise, and especially to welcome and protect foreign capital and labor. There will be plenty of room for all, and the field is a wide and inviting one. The climate is not more unhealthy than that of our Southern States, and the soil is of unsurpassed fertility. The city of St. Domingo is being rapidly improved, and the health of the Spanish troops is excellent. The most perfect order and security prevail throughout the island.
