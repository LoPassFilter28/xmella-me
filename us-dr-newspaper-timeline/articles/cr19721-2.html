A special report from St. Domingo describes Samana Bay in detail, calling it a powerful and convenient naval station if the United States becomes its possessor. It traces the bay's entrance, headlands, landmarks such as Sugar Loaf Mountain, its channels and reefs, and its inner anchorage where a fleet, fortifications, docks and warehouses could be placed.

Un informe especial desde Santo Domingo describe en detalle la Bahía de Samaná, llamándola una estación naval poderosa y conveniente si los Estados Unidos llegan a poseerla. Traza la entrada de la bahía, sus cabos, puntos de referencia como la montaña Pan de Azúcar, sus canales y arrecifes, y su fondeadero interior, donde podrían colocarse una flota, fortificaciones, muelles y almacenes.

Between Cape Samana on the north and Cape Raphael on the south the mouth measures twenty-one miles north, west three-fourths north, reckoned from Cape Raphael. From this line, including the inner harbor, as we may term it, the bay extends thirty miles to the mouth of the Yuna river. This stream is one of the largest on the island of St. Domingo and enters the bay at the extreme western shore. The breadth of the bay about midway its depth from the towns of Samana on its northern to Savannah la Mar on its southern shore is eight miles. Ten miles west of Cape Raphael, near Punta Jicaco, begins a reef which stretches northwestward to a number of islets or rocks, called Cayo Pascual, Levantado and Carenero, better known as the Samana Cays. This reef protects the basin of the bay against the heavy seas which otherwise a gale from the north and east would send into it. The passage for the entrance and exit of vessels is thus contracted to one [illegible] between Punta Cacao and Cayo Pascual. In the middle of this channel is a small reef, marked on the charts and called Jean Bart, having three and three-fourths fathoms, or twenty-four feet, of water. This could be indicated by a buoy. At other points not less than six fathoms, or thirty-six feet, of water is found, while a channel exists, well defined on the charts, showing nowhere less than eight fathoms, or forty-eight feet.
