Interesting from the Dominican Republic.

SourceFuente: The New York herald, New York [N.Y.], 1848-07-16
https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83030313/1848-07-16/ed-1/?sp=1
unclassified

I have stated enough in a former letter, when briefly reviewing the history of this part of the island, to enable the reader, to infer in some measure, the character of its population, considered in reference to the races from which it sprang, and of which it is now composed; having there shown the historical reasons, if I may so speak, why it necessarily differs in this respect, from that of the other part, or republic, of Hayti. I will add here, as being in place, a few words more on this point, and then proceed to give some account of another matter, though a small one, of the population; as, in a former letter, I promised to do.

As in all far southern or tropical countries, where slavery has existed, the servile class here, where that institution was first planted on American soil, was, in former times, numerically greater than that composed of the masters; and, as in the case in all countries where the relations of master and slave—the one white and the other black—exist, there sprang up a third class, intermediate between these two, both in color and condition—and all of these classes, it will have been seen, are, by the constitution of this republic, placed on a footing of civil and political equality. Why this state of society—a state far more homogeneous and mixed than in other circumstances and under other influences, it would have become—effacing more or less the nice lines and shades of distinction, both as respects color and social position, of the different classes—it may not be very difficult to account for, nor uninteresting to briefly investigate.

The Spanish colony of St. Domingo never was an agricultural one. The mining of gold, and not agriculture, was the inducement—the object that brought hither the early colonists; and though the surpassing fertility of the soil allures some to its systematic cultivation, the great body of the land has ever remained, from the period of the first discovery to this day, in a state of primitive nature, or under a kind and degree of cultivation that hardly deserves that name. The colony, thus isolated itself by its lack of agricultural enterprise and activity—shut itself out, in a great degree, if not done, from the rest of the world, with its growing commerce and enterprise—its people not having progress—when the supposed richer mineral resources of Mexico already began to cast a shadow on the peculiar enterprise that existed, gradually dwindled away. The higher and richer classes, losing to a greater or less extent, the energy and enterprise which the pursuit of and after riches gives with the active world stimulate and promote, sank into a state of intellectual and physical sloth; agriculture became neglected, all industry stagnated, and in a succession of years this class became less wealthy as well as less elevated, gradually, in their turn of descent, to lower and less feeing; the middling classes of the whites, by the same process, degenerated, and became more assimilating in their tastes and habits, and the poor and lower still lower. The next step, as regards these latter classes, was easy and natural. The nice distinctions of caste which, in slaveholding communities, ever exist in the thoughts and moral ideas and feelings of the privileged race—whatever the association, custom, and habits—or contact permit to the contrary—became gradually obliterated in some, and quite effaced in other cases; assimilating as well as in tastes and feelings resulting mainly from them, induced a preference, a blending of the similar classes in the two castes, reducing them to the same level, and finally closing them, in their own estimation, and, in fact, on the same footing of social equality and familiar local intercourse. And this commingling of the different races, naturally—in the condition of the country, remained stationary, or rather retrograded—extended, in time, upwards in the social scale. Such, unquestionably, have been the causes and principles operating through centuries, and producing the present actual state of society as presented to the view of the observer. I wish it, however, to be distinctly understood, that I do not intend to imply that there has been a universal amalgamation of the races in all classes, or indeed in any. I speak generally of general results from general causes. Besides the remnants of poor, humble white or nearly white families that exist, the great mass of the people, there is a small proportion of the people in every profession—the descendants and representatives of the old Castilian aristocracy of the country, among whom the distinctions of caste in social rank, as well as blood, have ever been consistently adhered to and preserved with all the proverbial pride of that race. And yet, notwithstanding this rather general mingling of races to which I have referred, the general and rigid, the moral power, the active business enterprise of the nation, vastly preponderates on the side where it might naturally be expected they would, by those, at least, who, though they may believe in the civil and political equality of mankind, stop at that point. And the character of the government and the constitution of society are, in a good degree, composed and moulded accordingly.

Besides, here in the capital, and in a few of the other commercial towns, the mercantile class is mostly composed of naturalized, or at least, long resident foreigners—Europeans generally; and these, together with a small corresponding native class, give a tone and character to business and also to society; and their influence is reflected strongly on the government itself, shaping in no small degree its character and policy.

The remaining portion of the population, of which I have to speak, is considered with the subject of the political elements of the nation, are the colored Americans. To prevent, it would seem, the shock to the agricultural interests of the country, which the abolition of slavery was well known to produce even here, the Haytian government, soon after taking possession of this section of the island, invited the immigration hither of the free people of color in the United States, and provided the means for their transportation. As an inducement to their coming, the heads of families were to be furnished gratuitously with good land for agricultural purposes, and also the means of sustenance until they should have time to receive returns from the soil by their own cultivation. They were, besides, guaranteed perfect freedom of and protection in their religious worship, as well as immunity from military service. Under these inducements, many hundreds emigrated from their homes in United States, elsewhere, and, particularly, and from county adjacent to those cities, and came and settled in various places in the island, chiefly, I believe, in this part.

It is no more than justice to the Haytian government to state, as I have found to be the uniform testimony of these people in conferences that this government sacredly kept its faith and promises made to them, and that they were then, and have been, treated with paternal solicitude. They have, however, never harmonized in their feelings, with the lower classes of their creole neighbors. Utter strangers for a long time, at least, to each other's language, and forms of religious faith and worship, it is not strange that the two classes should not have coalesced. While the character and conduct of the Americans inspired the natives with respect, still the latter could not forget that a horde of heretics had been introduced and quartered, as it were, among them; they were jealous of them, as they which and its appropriate people, they have, as a nation, ever entertained a most rooted and singular aversion. On the other hand, the Americans regarded, their new neighbors and fellow citizens, with whom, for a long time at least, they were held no social intercourse, though the medium of language, with distrust and fear; and, in point of religion, looked on them as a rude sort of idolators. As used to be the case with the planter, tension of fear was, in their own minds, possessed, though certainly on very different grounds, for slowly creeping behind here, with the bugbears, the terror of all American women and children, "the black cat, and the brown man," more emphatically in this aspect, than in what the fewer regarded as matters less than fanciful of incantations on death and burial occasions in the country. But I am digressing, whither, indeed, I am strongly tempted to do. Still, with all the prejudices of the natives against the people of their class, and which are still fostered in a great degree, it is due to them, so far as the Americans are concerned, to state, that the latter always universally bear testimony to the uniform inoffensive-ness of conduct in the former toward themselves.

The present government has extended to this portion of the citizens, as has been seen, the protection and guaranties under which they came and had before lived. Their lands and their religious privileges remain secure to them. They form an exemplary portion of the population, both in town and country; and, as every thing is by comparison in this world, it may be stated, that they are excellent examples of industry, intelligence, and enterprise to their fellow-citizens. And yet, with all the intelligence, industry, and practical knowledge of what the French call savoir vivre—how to live—which this people, who it is natural to suppose were among the most enterprising of their class at home, acquired by being raised among the whites, the observing traveller would look in vain among them here for that intelligence and its fruits—that comfort and thrift, and the thousand consequent nameless home comforts and means of enjoyment which are so general among the rural and urban of our own people at home. Placed by the government on excellent land, with good facilities in a near market here for the surplus of their agricultural productions, they seem to live, in most cases—with some degree of enterprise upon the cultivation of the soil. But the allurements of the lumber country—the cutting of mahogany—in the bands of a few capitalists, once lured these away from their fields, the very luxuriant fertility of whose soil, became a curse to them, so great is the labor to keep out the weeds, though by no means severe labor, to the lazy or indolent, and the more attractive labor of mahogany cutting, with its freedom from all restraint, wages, often in advance. These temptations appear to have been too great for their degree of farm-craft to counterbalance, or, consequently, resist. Their fields were abandoned, or left to their females; in other words, to necessary neglect; while the men spent away their time, if they were not, on the coast, in the mahogany cuts. The consequence was inevitable. Nature, with her natural luxuriance and vigor, soon re-asserted her supremacy; and the fields that had been masked by return of cultivation, were speedily overgrown again with weeds, wild vines, brush and bushes. Thus drawn from their appropriate avocations, or, more properly speaking, having added to them the toils of the field and of the transportation of a few scanty products of their spoiling, on their own persons to market, to obtain a few household necessaries, and perhaps avoidable, which many of them do to this day—the women, I say, undoubtedly suffered most severely under these hard necessities, from a mode of rural life. And it would pain the heart, it seems to me, of any disinterested, to hear them recount, in their simple but graphic language, the struggles and toils and sufferings they endured, and in a measure continue to endure. "I never was used to this kind of work," said a very intelligent active woman to the traveler in the woods, as she was trudging along towards town, with a heavy load on her head; and she evidently spoke the truth in all respects, for he knew her well. "I never was used to this kind of work when I lived in Philadelphia, and owned my little house and lot, and worked out when I pleased, and got good wages in hard money for it."

The story of this is, that many of the emigrants sickened and died, as was the case; while a part of them, more fortunate, perhaps, than either class, returned home, as those left here still invariably and fondly call the United States. Meanwhile, the men, exposed—in the pursuit of the calling that had lured them from their own lands, productive of every thing that the soil of a tropical or (with few exceptions) a semi-tropical nature can yield, and the climate, the cultivation of which would have secured them an independence—to the alternate burning heats and drenching rains, or, worse than either, the combined scorching rays of a tropical sun and a humid atmosphere, which their vocation compelled them to endure, have, from these altered conditions, very generally contracted chronic diseases, pains, and ailments, which have broken down their constitutions, crippled them, and made them prematurely old and inefficient for bodily labor. Thus, the heads of families, and themselves, in the decline of life, instead of being surrounded with the comforts and conveniences which faithfully pursued agriculture is sure to repay the tiller of the soil, destitute of a good deal of them; without the force left (for the sons have very generally followed the avocation of the fathers,) to again reclaim their re-wooded fields from the encroachments of nature, driven to restrain their labor within the narrow circuit of a small plat of open ground, for the sustenance of their now scantily furnished cabins, seems to grudge them their unwonted possessions, and yearns to separate them from these limited precincts in the center of her realms. This is no fancy picture. It is not highly colored. It is drawn from the life—from careful and frequent observation, made by the writer, of the general—not universal—condition of the "American" rural population here; and the causes that have produced it. "Did you know Mr. ——, of Baltimore?" was asked the writer, a few days since, by an old looking, decrepit black man. On being replied to that he had known him well, though he was now dead. "Well, he was my first master; I waited on his table till I was grown up." And the reminiscence that his present condition and that which had evidently been his lot for years, contrasted so widely and sadly with that he had under that master, struck the writer with a force not easily forgotten.

The writer has entered into these details, and will yet pursue them a little further, on account of the subject of emigration hither being now again agitated, among a portion of the American colored population. He has not done so to deter them from the step. Far from it. He has desired to extend, to hold up a beacon-warning to caution them against the seductive nature of most of these undertakings. If any of the free American colored population, of whatever shades and complexions, represented, are inclined to exchange the comforts of their American homes, with their domestic and other means of happiness, (I have not used the words unheedingly,) for a life and career of uncertain promise, let them bear well in mind that these latter advantages are not to be preserved without a degree of thrift they now possess; they have their inevitable price—labor, energy and self-denial, and that too under circumstances with which they are not now familiar. What inducements this government may hold out in the shape of guaranties, I am very confident will be religiously observed and fulfilled. Let them come, but with all the habits of industry their pursuits, and all the mechanical means and tools they can command. There is no country where they will find them more, or where their happiness, and none, surely, where, with the practice of the one and the exercise of the other, the agriculturist may more certainly secure an independence and with it the happiness which, under such circumstances, so far as it goes, naturally secures.

It is an error, very prevalent, I believe, among all classes in northern climes—and in such I include, of course, the northern states of our own union—that clothing and shelter, especially the former, are matters of little moment in tropical climates; that, so far as comfort is concerned, the requirement of them and their value to existence is not a matter of personal covering. As regards the West Indies, at least, this is a fatal error, mistake, and the emigrant hither, from whatever country or latitude, who should come with this idea, and in consequence contemplated the notion that he will have to labor very little, indeed, to sustain life and be comfortable, and with the fixed and settled intention of acting accordingly, could not commit a more egregious, and, in his case, more fatal error. This is emphatically true, when applied to any intended settlement or residence away from this city. True—the lower rural classes of creoles here, and the free negroes also, live in thatched, or rather unthatched hovels, destitute of almost either of the referred to departments of civilized life. And they may live in their rude, unfloored, or floorless cabins, and sleep on the ground, with nothing but a blanket to protect and cover them over, as many really do, and think themselves very comfortable, for they have never known, nor aspired to any thing better. But the American immigrant has not been accustomed to such a life, whether in Maryland or Alabama; and coming here from either of these extremes, he cannot endure, without much physical suffering, such destitution of the household comforts to which he has been accustomed. Again, the creole can, and does frequently live almost exclusively on the spontaneous productions of a prolific soil; but the American colored man, accustomed to a different diet, would pine away, and, in a short time, in many cases, probably, die, by such a course of living. All these comforts, and in him necessaries, must be procured by labor here as well as anywhere else. Many articles, which to the creole may be luxuries, to the immigrant are necessaries. What is more, as the greater part of the luxuries, are, can only be procured at a high price, being imported; and if the latter does not, by his industry, furnish himself with the means of procuring them, he is continually exposed, not merely to the same state of suffering and disease to which this destitution even now, but he would become liable to new and aggravated ones to which a change of climate, and an exposure to it, would naturally expose him. Besides, the force of example, constantly before him, of the indolent habits and customs that prevail here, and the ease with which, when he shall fall into the ways and habits of the native classes of the country, he can obtain a sustenance, must, in a great measure, tend to the same result. And if, without knowing how or why, in the midst of a people whose manners and habits he must adopt, without considering that the cause and fault lie solely with himself, and not the country or its government, he bitterly rues the day he left his native soil; preferring, perchance, as has been, on one occasion, declared to the writer, to have rather been a slave in Virginia than a citizen here. And yet it must be remarked that, as it would be natural to expect, there is a decided difference in the intelligence and character of those immigrants who were born free and those who were emancipated slaves, though young. And the writer is led, though no friend to slavery, to say, that, from what he has observed, he regards it as a calamity to the latter class to receive their freedom at home, if the conditions of that emancipation are emigration, even to such a surpassingly fruitful and genial a clime as that of this country. There might, and probably would be, exceptions, but that this is the rule, his limited observation, and the deductions of reason, lead him equally to believe.

The writer has stepped somewhat aside from the limits of his letter, as he had sketched it; but he has done so, upon a further topic, out of pure kindness, to furnish some suggestions to any portion of his less favored countrymen who may have an intention of coming out to this country; and, in doing this, to guard them against entertaining any unreasonable and illusory expectations—which could only, and necessarily, dissipate. And should these lines fall under the eyes of those of our people who are not entertaining such intentions, he believes they will still read and appreciate what has been written as indicative of quite as much genuine kindness and feeling towards their class, as that entertained or manifested by those who, so freely, under the guise and perhaps with the feelings of philanthropists, assume to take them and their interests under their peculiar care and protection.

And now, as it is on Sunday that I am writing, and am, moreover, in the way and humor of gossipping, and shall not, because I have no occasion particularly directed against that class which has occupied my pen, a portion of my remarks will close by another view they present. As a class, they are not only upright in their character and conduct as citizens, but as far as an observer can judge, sincere, devout, and in fact, christians. The unpretending place of worship of their little flock, occasionally—for they do not yet possess a place of worship in this city—is a room in an antique looking, and, for the rest, vacant, old mansion; at the hour that custom convenes the little congregation, all calmness and quiet reigns around, the sea breeze, imparting that delicious coolness to the air, so grateful in a tropical summer, after the hot stagnation which occurs just before that hour, on the approach of the land breezes of the night; while the eternal roar of the breakers over the reef, formed shore just below, mingles its majestic and solemn voices in tune with the simple chaunt of the neighboring worshippers. Scattered about, by vicinity of the city as those worshippers are, the number of the weekly attendants is small, some nine or ten—perhaps ten or twelve, two or three children being seen there. It can hardly fail to awaken the soft sympathies of the American visitor here—even though he may not be very devoutly inclined—to witness the simple, though sincere worship of these humble countrymen of his, in this out of the way corner of the island; and accustomed as his ears are generally, here, to the sound of a foreign tongue, to hear in an intelligible language, whether it be in the routine of the common prayer book, or the fervent responses of the Methodists, so unlike the pomp and ceremonies of the Catholic worship, in the spacious cathedral. Still more to hear the effect of some old familiar church melody fall on his ear, which, though certainly not executed in the style of the accords of our metropolitan choirs, yet comes from the heart, and cannot but awaken an echo in his; and with that peculiar, pleasing, melancholy melody which the colored race of our country always infuse into their music—all these turn his yearnings towards his home—the land of his birth and his affections—to the scenes, it may be, of his childhood—the neat village church, and those amidst whom he there used to sit, now, perhaps, departed, or far distant, as the case may be. And, to look back, to the, perhaps, scarce less impressive, and far more familiar and accustomed scenes of his after years—the stir and life of the city, and then relief to a still, and recess from the jar and turmoil of business—the soothing stillness and peace of the sacred Sabbath hours—while the friends there usually clustered round him, gathered from the varied tracks of life, pass vividly in memory by ties more or less intimate and dear, that are now broken and gone, or those still entwined with the light garland of hope and promise; and he, in a few minutes, falls into a reverie, during which he has traversed the expanse of ocean that separates him from all these, and is surprised to find that all this phantasmagoria of pleasing and of painful memories has been evoked, by an enchantment no more potent than the humble religious devotions in this far off spot, of a few aged representatives of a race, whom prejudices at home, at least, have placed in a grade so far below his own.
