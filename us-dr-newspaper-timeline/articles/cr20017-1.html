The Evening Star reported that Dominican President Heureaux attributed escalating border disputes to Haiti's default on the commercial and boundary treaties of 1874–1875. Under the agreement, Haiti leased Dominican territory for $150,000 annually and granted tariff exemptions to Dominican imports, accumulating over $2,000,000 in unpaid dues while complaining of Dominican rum smuggling. Dominican consular officials in New York acknowledged simmering diplomatic frictions but had received no official confirmation of armed border clashes.

El Evening Star informó que el presidente dominicano Heureaux atribuyó la escalada de disputas fronterizas al incumplimiento de Haití de los tratados comerciales y de límites de 1874–1875. En virtud del acuerdo, Haití arrendaba territorio dominicano por 150.000 dólares anuales y otorgaba exenciones arancelarias a las importaciones dominicanas, acumulando más de 2.000.000 de dólares en cuotas impagadas mientras se quejaba del contrabando de ron dominicano. Funcionarios consulares dominicanos en Nueva York reconocieron las latentes fricciones diplomáticas, pero no habían recibido confirmación oficial de enfrentamientos fronterizos armados.

"President Huerreaux said today that the real cause of the trouble was that in 1876 and 1876 a treaty for commercial purposes was entered into between the Haytian and Dominican governments, which at the same time defined the boundaries of the two countries. A special stipulation of this treaty was that Hayti was to make use of a portion of Dominican territory, which she needed, and which Santo Domingo government was to allow to be so used, on condition that Hayti should pay annually to the Dominican $150,000 as long as she retained possession of the loaned territory. Hayti was, moreover, to permit all imports from Santo Domingo to enter into Hayti free of duty. Hayti has not paid the $150,000 for a number of years, and now owes something over $2,000,000. "Hayti, however, alleges that there is so much rum imported from Santo Domingo that the Haytian revenues are greatly suffering. Hayti wishes to make an end of her bargain and at the same time retain possession of the loaned land." Senors Francisco Leonte Vasquez and Manuel Do J. Galvan, the consul general and consul, respectively, of the Dominican government in New York, state that they have received no official intimation from their government that any outbreak or invasion had occurred on the island. They knew that there was some sort of trouble brewing between Hayti and Santo Domingo, but they could not say to what extent it had developed.
