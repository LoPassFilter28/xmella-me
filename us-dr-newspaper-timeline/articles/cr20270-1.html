SAMANA BAY.

Further Details of the Sale to the American Company—Creation of a Sovereign Power—Speculations and Surmises Regarding the Future Action of the New Nation.

Special Despatch to The Chicago Tribune.

NEW YORK, Jan. 18.—The full text of the Samana purchase convention confirms the fact that the Company has obtained absolute sovereignty over the peninsula, with its inhabitants and private interests. The sovereignty conceded to the Company is greater than that of the State of New York, or any other State in the Union, for it includes the right to levy and collect custom duties, and to do many other things which can only be done by a sovereign nation. Baez not only sells them these extraordinary privileges, but he sells them the flag of the Republic of St. Domingo to use as they may see proper. Besides all this, the Company can build railroads and telegraphs in any part of the island, obtaining grants as extraordinary as those accorded to the Union Pacific and other railroads when Congress was giving away the public domain. Every league of railroad constructed by the Company brings its square league of land, and every three leagues of telegraph, a square league grant. The Company promised Baez to use all their influence with Congress to bring Dominican products into the ports of the United States, free of duty, which would be a singular discrimination against other tropical countries.

A most extraordinary feature of the treaty is that it frees the men composing the Company from the operation of our Register and Navigation laws. Under the acts of Congress, American merchants cannot buy ships abroad. This Company, though composed mostly of Americans, can buy ships where it can buy them cheapest, for it has bought a flag under which to run them.

The Herald says on this head: "A few judicious alliances with American railway monopolies would make the Company as powerful in the United States as in St. Domingo, and in the end it might rule us as effectually as our tan-colored brethren in the West Indies."

[To the Associated Press.]

NEW YORK, Jan. 18.—A great deal of speculation is rife in this city as to the Samana Bay Company seeking aid from the United States Government in furthering its objects. It is thought that the Company will endeavor first to obtain the formal protection of our Government, and, if possible, to lease it a naval station in the bay, and, further, to agitate the idea of annexation. Already it is stated that powerful lobby interests are to be created with these ends in view, and that Congress will be asked soon to pass exceptional tariff laws applying to the trade from San Domingo.
