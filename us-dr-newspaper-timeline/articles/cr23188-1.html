The San Domingo Property.

While Mr Sumner's committee is considering the San Domingo annexation treaty, a hundred editors and correspondents are exploring for all the facts that can be got at about the undivided half of a great island which will come to us if the treaty is ratified. The extent of the Dominican part of Hayti is uncertain, but cannot well be less than 20,000 square miles. The population is a mixture of Spanish, Indian, negro and French, and the whole number is not more than 150,000. They are now, as usual, in a state of inactive revolution against the nominal government, which is made up of President Baez, a Castilian, his secretary of state, M. M. Gautier, who was his commissioner in conducting the negotiations, Attorney-General Felix Del Monte, Secretary of War and Marine Hungria, and Secretary of the Treasury Curiel. These officers are all white, being either of Castilian or French descent. There is also a supreme court, of which Señor Tejera, a pure Castilian, is chief justice. The republic is divided into seven provinces, each of which has a representative in the Senate, which is the only legislative body, and meets regularly once a year for legislative business. The Senate is understood to be with Baez in his wish to transfer the country to us, but there is a party headed by two generals, Cabral and Luperol, who are bitter against annexation.

A document protesting against it has found signers in Curacoa, St Thomas, Venezuela, Turks Island, and those few parts of the Dominican republic which are in the hands of the revolutionary party. The Dominicans who have been expelled from the country, and the partisans of Cabral and Luperol have signed it, wherever they may happen to be. They call themselves the "constitutional party," and denounce Baez for his cruelties toward them. His success, they say, is owing to American intervention, which alone could have prevented the triumph of Cabral and Luperol, and that, as a consequence of this intervention, there are more than 200 prisoners in the jails, some of whom are dying of hunger, and others are being destroyed by reason of terrible sufferings. Moreover, Baez is engaged in shooting a large number of friends of the constitution, because they are opposed to the projects of an unfaithful executive. Sustained by the government at Washington, which has remitted him arms, and sent a steamer to cruise in Dominican waters for his aid and support in case of emergency, Baez has thus been kept in power against the will of the people, and now proposes to trade them off to the United States. They declare that they will resist annexation to the death, etc., etc. Their leader, Cabral, is on the Haytian frontier, with a few hundred men, principally Cacos from Hayti. The town of Jabon, captured some time back by these insurgents, had been retaken by the government troops, of which a force sufficiently numerous to keep Cabral and his adherents effectually in check, holds possession of that part of the country. Luperol is at Turks Island, awaiting the issue of the Haytian war. A perfect understanding is said to exist between him and the revolutionary leaders in Hayti, who have promised that as soon as they succeed in overthrowing Salnave, they will place all their resources, including their war vessels, at the service of Cabral and Luperol, to be employed against President Baez. The latter, nothing daunted, has issued a proclamation, directing the commandants of the several districts to make arrangements for submitting the treaty to a popular vote. The feeling in the city of San Domingo, in Porto Platte, in Santiago, and in all the towns, is strongly in favor of annexation; but not in the country districts, the rural population being generally under the impression that the Americans are coming to take possession of all the good things of the land, and make serfs of the natives.

This feeling among the Dominicans is probably due in part to the fact that they are not to come into the Union as a state, but under territorial government. Their first proposition was for admission as a state, and the admission of Texas was cited as a precedent, but while Gen Babcock did not then have power to fix any conditions, he was invested with a general discretion which authorized him to inform Baez that this was out of the question. Knowing this, and hearing, perhaps, of the combinations forming in New York and Washington to make money out of the material riches of the island, it is no wonder that the Dominicans feel jealous about it. Moreover, they are mostly very ignorant, and have an unreasonable jealousy of all foreigners. The more intelligent, however, know the advantage of being under the protection of a strong government, and probably the vote will show a majority for annexation. There is little distinction of caste, color or religion among the people. Baez's chief of staff, Guielermo, is a colored man, and so is the collector of the port of San Domingo. The Catholic church prevails, though other denominations are tolerated. At San Domingo there has been a Methodist chapel for twenty-four years, and on Samana bay among the blacks, there is a Methodist church of several hundred members. There is little either of religion or morals among the population, of whatever church they profess to be. The condition of their paper currency is as bad as their morals. About two millions were issued some years since, but it soon became uncurrent, and the merchants and dealers repudiated it by fixing its rate of redemption at $400 of paper for one in gold. Subsequently this rate was ratified by legislative action, and a large amount of the money, which was printed on ordinary printing presses, was burned in the public square of San Domingo City. The revenues of the republic have been wholly derived from export and import duties. The export duties, however, are very small and are levied on sugar, coffee, rice, salt, hides, mahogany, logwood, "lignum vitæ," tobacco, etc. They have no system of internal or property taxation. Their import duties are also very light, and our government will have several blessings to confer upon their new children in the shape of internal taxation, high tariffs, and a small army of office-holders. The few soldiers of the Dominican army have just been furnished with new uniforms and rifles, both secured from the United States as part of the consideration for the lease of the bay of Samana. Col J, W. Fabins is in Samana in charge of the American flag there, which is duly raised every morning; another American is there looking after railroad matters, and ready to begin building a line into the interior as soon as the treaty is ratified. We trust there will be no haste about this, for there is no reason why we should spring a trap on anybody in San Domingo.
