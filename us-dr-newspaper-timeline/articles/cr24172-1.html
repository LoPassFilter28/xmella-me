THE SAN DOMINGO TREATY.

The Senate was engaged in executive session to-day, from 1 o'clock until near 5, on the San Domingo treaty. Mr. Sumner occupied the floor during the whole time, but he has not yet finished his speech. It will be continued on Monday's session. It must be disposed of on Tuesday, as that day is fixed for the final vote. Mr. Williams, of Oregon, called up the subject, and Mr. Morton spoke for about three hours in favor of the treaty. After Mr. Sumner took the floor, both speakers treated the subject involved in President Grant's message as one of immense importance to the nation, and as calculated to shape its future policy.

It is impossible to over-estimate the power of Mr. Sumner's speech. He spoke for about three hours, giving one of the strongest points presented by him. He said he did not want to speak on the subject, but duty compelled him to do so. One of the strong points presented by him was in reference to the argument that if we do not take the country it will produce a foreign war. He said it would create an aggressive power, and that it does not become us to announce any foreign government or a protectorate over the Dominican Republic. He said if we take it, it would show that we might enter into an alliance with any foreign government. The people of the Dominican Republic do not want annexation. Such a course as proposed will be an humiliating and an energetic republican government of our own. In his view, annexation would not be for the well-being of the people there any more than for our national advantage. After treating the subject in this way, he took up the position of the treaty, titles, advantages, and resources of the island, and Mr. Sumner answered the favor of all that was said on that side. There was some talk about the strategic and commercial position, but most of the speech was on the moral side of the question, and was conducted on higher grounds than this. Mr. Sumner argued that San Domingo is the place to which the colored race of the South will eventually step, without taking the others into our nationality, and Mr. Sumner avowed the desire to see them all incorporated into our body politic as at present. He said annexation had a direct tendency to destroy the black Republic of Hayti; not a war are of a peaceful character, but it will end in nothing less than the extirpation of the race from the constant state of warfare which would be produced.

Returning to the treaty terms brought under consideration, he called attention to the fact that little is even now known about it print, except from rumors of what it contains, that the $1,500,000 paid to Baez would go to pay San Domingo bonds, or to whom Baez may distribute them as presents. The public lands of San Domingo are of less value than those of Alaska. There is not a foot of them, except possibly the mountain peaks, which has not been private property, and the titles are to be traced in the records of murders, confiscations, evictions, plunder and rapine. President Baez is a cowardly and brutal adventurer, who, having run his career of assassination and rapine in Hayti, was driven out by Geffrard, and then went to San Domingo to repeat his work. Geffrard offered $10,000 for his head, and if Baez ever goes back there he will be executed as a pirate. He called attention to the fact that Baez now only stays in power by the presence of our gunboats. One of Mr. Sumner's strongest points was that Baez's power was a usurped power, and he spoke particularly of the fact that it was obtained by fraud. He said that if those who favor this measure were in our position, they would see that it very ill becomes us to attempt to annex a country in this way. He referred to the fact that Baez had made the strongest arguments that can be presented in favor of the treaty. General Schurz has the same views on this subject as Mr. Sumner. He expressed confidence in the defeat of the treaty by an overwhelming vote, and advised his friends not to believe in any other result.

The question of adjourning on Monday was made a special order for 1 o'clock. A vote was taken, which resulted, in an attendance of about fifty, by five majority.
