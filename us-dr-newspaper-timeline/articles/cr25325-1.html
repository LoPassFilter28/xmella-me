GRANT—SUMNER.

Maj. Ben Perley Poore Rises to an Explanation.

[Washington Cor. Chicago Tribune.]

Maj. Ben Perley Poore has at last given to the world an authentic statement of the causes of the estrangement between Sumner and President Grant. This explanation has been called out by the recent statements of Gen. Grant, Wendell Phillips, ex-Secretary Fish, and John W. Forney. Maj. Poore was the guest of Sumner at the time of the estrangement, and heard all of the conversation between President Grant and Sumner. Maj. Poore’s story, in substance, is this: The removal of James M. Ashley, ex-Congressman from Ohio, from the Governorship of Montana, and the appointment in his place of Col. Potts, which had just happened, was the topic of table conversation. A letter just received from Ashley, protesting against the change, had been read at table, when the servant announced that the President was at the door. He was received, and in the course of the conversation the letter from Ashley was handed to the President. The latter became immediately excited, and denounced Ashley as a mischief-maker and a worthless fellow.

The next topic discussed was the San Domingo treaty. Sumner, Maj. Poore says, dwelt especially upon the expenditure by Gen. Babcock on San Domingo of a large sum taken from a secret-service fund for promoting intercourse with the West India islands, which Seward had prevailed on Thad Stevens to have inserted in an appropriation bill during the war, and the President impressed Sumner with the idea that he feared an attack in Congress for that expenditure. Poore adds the opinion that Sumner thought that the President had come to enlist his services in defending this expenditure by Gen. Babcock, but the President meant the treaty for the acquisition of the Dominican republic. The President and the Senator misunderstood each other. After a while the President promised to send Gen. Babcock to call on the Senator the next day, with copies of the papers, and left. As Mr. Sumner escorted him to the door he told him that he was a Republican, a supporter of the Republican administration, and that he should sustain the administration in this case if he possibly could, after he had examined the papers. He meant, sustain the expenditure of $100,000 in cash, $50,000 in a light battery and muskets, purchased at New York, but the President meant the treaty.

Maj. Poore’s statement thus removes a grave historical doubt, and seems to set at rest the stories recently published of the cause of the difficulty between Grant and Sumner.
