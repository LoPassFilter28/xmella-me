Our San Domingo City Correspondence.

SAN DOMINGO CITY, Jan. 12, 1862.

Termination of the Republic and Commencement of the Provinciature of the Country—Non-Recognition of the New Government by the Great Powers—What Spain Has Accomplished and Promises—A Splendid Location for the Negroes and the Outcasts of the United States—How the City Has Improved in Dashing "Turnouts," Toilets, Crinoline and House Decorations—War News from America—Theatricals, &c.

We are mulling along here in a kind of "cucumber time" between hay and grass.

The poor old Dominican republic has breathed its last. It died, too, without even the usual amount of howling; but Spain still sits awkwardly in the new saddle. She finds it not a triumphal opportunity to invest the reals she got from Morocco, and those she expects to get from time to time from Hayti and Mexico (D. V.) She does not think she has got such an almighty bargain in San Domingo after all. Still her old Castilian pride will not permit her to back out without a first rate excuse.

As yet none of the great Powers have recognized this island as a Spanish province, and the several consulates of France, England and the United States present a singularly modest and "attendant" aspect.

Spain, however, has begun well—she is on her good behavior.

A new tariff is proposed, reducing duties on merchandise in foreign bottoms from 30 to 20 per cent.

A decree grants permission to whalers to make Samana a free port for all nations, and it is darkly hinted that President Lincoln will be invited by his august cousin, Isabella II., to send his "contrabands" to our fertile (not sacred) soil.

The most entire religious toleration will be exercised.

Emigration from every land under the sun, including coolies and the "rest lying east of Chinese Tartary," will be encouraged.

Our coasting trade is thrown open to all flags, and last, but by no means least, that nation whose name for centuries has been the synonym of tyranny and oppression, has begun a march with the progressive people of the earth; and here, on the spot where African slavery was first planted, and whence it spread its poisonous branches like a blighting curse over the surrounding countries; here her first act after annexation has been to declare the chains of the slave broken, and broken forever.

Materially the city at least has been benefitted by the change of government. Carriages that have seen service in the continental or insular world in the days of the puff and toupee of Havana, begin to show themselves in our unaccustomed streets. Spanish thoroughbreds, English, American and other high breds do their duty, whilst "Jack-ass-dom" is scorned and disgusted. Several fine horses from Cuba and Porto Rico have been imported, and gayly caparisoned, bear their creole masters right proudly.

Toilets are more dashing, if not more distingue than formerly. Less of the eternal and dreary black mantilla, but pretentious crinolines flaunt their voluptuous curves in our breeze.

Houses, too, are catching a fresh coat of plaster or paint, and shops and tiendas innumerable meet the eye in our streets.

Meantime traders are not frequent, but loafers abound.

I have little of importance to send you, being, like yourselves in political circles, confused in our notions of things. There are big stories afloat, of course.

One report was, that in consequence of the blunder made by John Bull upon the arrest of Mason and Slidell, the French Emperor has called upon John to account for the disposition made of his uncle, who placed himself as a refugee (not in act of war) on board a British craft; also that the Southern confederacy had declared war against Spain, and that a French and Spanish fleet had been seen off Charleston.

Nature, too, has not yet owned the culture of the husbandman. The island is the same wilderness as when Columbus gazed with rapture upon its green clad hills and magnificent prairies. Vast herds roam at will over the savannas, but the herdsmen are few. Its mines, that once yielded so fast to the conqueror, and, rich as the pockets of Spain, are unworked; its teeming valleys are unbroken by the plough, and its woods contain towering timbers to shelter navies. What a glorious field to try the experiment of your new freed or discarded black population.

General Santana is still Captain General. He is an exemplary gentleman, but slow. Affairs are managed pretty much by his Excellency, the Superintendente and the Governor General, who are both men of a high order of intelligence and of extremely liberal views. The Dominican party, though shorn of all their power, are reconciled, and Spaniards occupy their places and pocket their emoluments.

Mr. Jaeger, our new Consul, is at his post, but, like his predecessors, holds no official intercourse with the government.

We have at the theatre a prestigitateur. The feats, however, do not astonish the natives, since Santana changed a republic into a monarchical province by a dash of his pen.

The bark Harvest Queen, which sails to-morrow for New York, carries a sample of native cotton which grows here abundantly, and can doubtless be cultivated to advantage.
