The Sun commented on Spanish apprehensions regarding the Dominican Republic, asserting that Spain could neither re-establish colonial control nor prevent voluntary annexation by the United States. The editorial noted that under the Monroe Doctrine, European powers—including Spain and France—were strictly barred from acquiring territory or interfering in the political destinies of New World republics.

The Sun comentó sobre los temores españoles respecto a la República Dominicana, aseverando que España no podía restablecer el control colonial ni impedir una anexión voluntaria por parte de los Estados Unidos. El editorial señalaba que, en virtud de la Doctrina Monroe, a las potencias europeas —incluidas España y Francia— se les prohibía terminantemente adquirir territorios o intervenir en los destinos políticos de las repúblicas del Nuevo Mundo.

The annexation of San Domingo is nowhere talked of or dreamed of here at the present time, but does Spain imagine that if the people of the United States and the people of San Domingo wished to join fortunes she would have the right or power to prevent it? On the other hand, if the secret of this otherwise inexplicable [illegible] in Madrid is the [illegible] on the part of Spain to regain her old control in San Domingo, she may as well abandon it. She could not be suffered to acquire as her own either the eastern or the western part of the Island. That would conflict with American policy as embodied in the Monroe doctrine. This policy and doctrine, however, may relieve Spain from her latest source of anxiety — the supposed designs of France upon San Domingo. For it does not even accord to France the right to transfer to herself Cuba, which is now not free but under European domination. Secretary Clay's letter of Oct. 25, [illegible], is clear on that point, while as to the republics of the New World, Monroe had declared that any interposition for the purpose of oppressing them, or controlling in any other manner their destiny, by any European power, would be regarded as the manifestation of an unfriendly disposition toward the United States. This view is not essentially altered by the fact that France, then a monarchy, is now a republic.
