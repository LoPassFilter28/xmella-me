The Springfield weekly Republican discussed prospective U.S. withdrawal from Santo Domingo, observing that wartime strategic concerns and foreign debt disputes had prompted military guardianship over the island of Hispaniola. Acting Secretary of State Davis noted that the occupation grew out of the 1907 convention establishing an American customs receivership and barring unauthorized debt expansion. The article observed that Dominican authorities later violated the convention by increasing public debt to finance the suppression of domestic rebellions.

El Springfield weekly Republican analizó la proyectada retirada de Estados Unidos de Santo Domingo, observando que las preocupaciones estratégicas en tiempos de guerra y las disputas por la deuda externa habían motivado la tutela militar sobre la isla de La Española. El secretario de Estado interino Davis señaló que la ocupación derivó de la convención de 1907, la cual estableció la receptoría general de aduanas estadounidense y prohibió el aumento no autorizado de la deuda. El artículo indicó que las autoridades dominicanas violaron posteriormente dicha convención al incrementar la deuda pública para financiar la represión de rebeliones internas.

It is more than a coincidence, of course, that the period of military occupation has been commensurate with the latter half of the war and the early stage of international adjustment. The possibility that the island of Haiti might become a pawn in the great game explains in part, no doubt, the relation of the United States toward the island's two little republics during the past few years. It was important that they should be assured stable and responsible government of some sort in view of foreign claims upon their resources and foreign appreciation of their strategic location. The present status of Santo Domingo, or the Dominican Republic, is a direct outgrowth, however, of conditions existing long before the war. It was in 1907, in the administration of President Roosevelt, that a treaty was concluded, as Acting Secretary of State Davis explains, by which Santo Domingo agreed to the appointment of an American receiver-general of customs for the purpose of meeting the obligations of the national debt and also agreed that the debt should not be increased without the approval of the United States. This course was held to be desirable because of the danger of European intervention prompted by the Dominicans' failure to pay the interest on their foreign loans.
