This history of Samana Bay recounts how the natives called the land Samana, how their cacique Cayacoa visited Columbus's ship, and how Columbus christened the bay De las Flechas after a skirmish there. It also tells how the English pirate Jack Banister, at the close of the seventeenth century, placed his guns on one of the cays and with 200 men beat off two English frigates, killing more than 120 of the assailants, an engagement that gave the islets the name Banister's Cays.

Esta historia de la bahía de Samaná cuenta cómo los naturales llamaban Samaná a la tierra, cómo su cacique Cayacoa visitó la nave de Colón y cómo Colón bautizó la bahía De las Flechas tras una escaramuza en el lugar. Cuenta también cómo el pirata inglés Jack Banister, a fines del siglo XVII, colocó sus cañones en uno de los cayos y con 200 hombres rechazó a dos fragatas inglesas, matando a más de 120 de los asaltantes, un combate que dio a los islotes el nombre de Banister's Cays.

After his death his widow became a Christian, and was baptized under the name of Donna Inez Cayacoa. The natives called the land Samana, and Columbus christened the bay De las Flechas, in consequence of the skirmish. After remaining four days the Spaniards sailed upon their homeward voyage to Portugal, which they reached in nine more than six weeks. Reefs, islets, and rocks form natural defenses to the entrance of the bay, and are so situated that, if properly fortified, any hostile vessel that attempted to enter might be placed under a destructive cross fire. The favorable situation of some of these islets or cays for preventing the approach of an enemy was signally proved at the close of the seventeenth century, by that noted English pirate, Jack Banister, who, with his bold buccaneers, at that time infested the neighborhood. He had just arrived at [Samana?], accompanied by another vessel commanded by a Frenchman named Lagarde, when the commanders of two English frigates, hearing that Banister was anchored there, prepared to enter the bay. The pirate immediately caused the guns of his vessel to be placed ashore upon one of the cays, and defended the entrance with his own crew and that of the French vessel, consisting of 200 men. so effectively that more than 120 of the assailants were killed and the frigates forced to retire.
