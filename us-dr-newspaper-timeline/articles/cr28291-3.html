The then recent experiences of the people, however, under foreign domination rendered the proposition inopportune. Although, as the Assistant Secretary reported, the measure commended itself to the better judgment of the leading minds of the Dominion republic, it would require time to satisfy the minds of the people that the occupation of Samana by the United States would be a far different matter from its recent temporary control by Spain, and that our presence and friendship would save them from the very evils which they dreaded, and bring peace and prosperity to take the place of their unhappy discords. The negotiations were, however, broken off; but on the 24th of November in the same year our commercial agent again wrote that an informal proposal had been made to him for a lease of Samana and the following month President Baez, after consulting the magnates of the republic, made arrangements for submitting a proposition for the protection of the United States to a popular vote.

Such was the condition of these negotiations when the administrtion of Mr. Johnson ceased and General Grant was elected President. General Babcock was now sent as a commissioner to act in conjunction with Mr. Perry, the commercial agent of the United States, to negotiate a treaty for the annexation of the whole territory of the republic to the United States, or for a lease of the peninsula and bay of Samana.

In February, 1870, the proposition for annexation was submitted to the people and was approved by an almost unanimous vote. Mr. Perry in his dispatches said: "The whole people desire annexation to the United States."

The treaty, however, failed to receive the requisite two-thirds of the United States Senate, and General Grant again called attention to it in his annual message at the next session of Congress. He said: "I now firmly believe that the moment it is known that the United States have entirely abandoned the project of accepting as a part of its territory the island of Santo Domingo a free port will be negotiated for by European nations. In the bay of Samana a large commercial city will spring up to which we will be tributary without receiving corresponding benefits, and then will be seen the folly of our rejecting so great a prize."

After referring to the fact that the Government and people of St. Domingo had voluntarily sought this annexation—that they were a weak power, yet possessing one of the richest territories under the sun, capable of suporting in luxury 10,000,000 of people—he presented at great length the commercial advantages to be secured, and finally argued that "the acquisition of St. Domingo is an adherence to the Monroe doctrine; that it is a measure of national protection; that it is asserting our just claim to a controlling influence over the great commercial traffic soon to flow from east and west by way of the isthmus—that it is to build up our merchant marine; that it is to furnish new markets for the products of our farms, shops, and manufactories; that it is to extend the area of freedom and to furnish our citizens with the necessaries of life at a cheaper rate than ever before." In fine, he concluded: "So convinced am I of the advantages to flow from the acquisition of St. Domingo and of the great disadvantage—I might say calamities—to flow from non-acquisition that I believe the subject has only to be investigated to be approved."

But this eloquent and patriotic appeal fell unheeded upon the dull ears of an unappreciative Senate, and the matter has strangely remained in abeyance through successive administrations from that day to this, until so disheartened had the authorities and the people of the little republic finally become that they plaintively referred to it in recently declining to be represented at the World's Fair in 1893.

It is true that President Baez and others prophesied that it would some day be renewed. And this has now come to pass. But the renewal now proceeds from the side of the United States, and not until our humble supplications at the other end of the island have been peremptorily rejected. The vigorous increase of our navy, our rapidly augmenting commerce with the southern republics, and the new impetus to be given to isthmian transit by the Nicaragua Canal, and soon, perhaps, by a ship railway at Tehuantepec, all so forcibly urged by General Grant in his repeated appeals to Congress, contribute to impress upon the mind as a matter of imminent importance.—Chicago Times.
