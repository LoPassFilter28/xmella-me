SAMANA OURS.

The Money Sent to Baez by New York Merchants—Trade With Santo Domingo Secured.

From the New York World, Dec. 7.

It is now three and a half years since President Grant took up the subject of the annexation of Santo Domingo. The main idea urged in favor of the plan was that it was indispensible to the prosperity of American commerce for the United States to have in their possession a substantial territory in the West Indies. In June, 1869, the President appointed an agent to obtain information about the Dominican Republic, and General Babcock was finally sent out there. In September, 1869, he negotiated a preliminary treaty, which was approved by President Grant, and in November a regular treaty with President Baez was concluded. The matter was debated in Congress, a commission was sent to the island in January, 1871, and finally in April of that year President Grant relinquished the plan.

But it has since been taken up by prominent business men of this country, who were persuaded that it would be profitable to them to secure what the Government had rejected. Some of them had visited Santo Domingo, and the result is that the steamer Tybee has just taken out, with other valuable cargo, the money required by the Baez Government for a certain specified amount of territory, including the Bay of Samana and for which Congress refused an appropriation. It is reported in business circles that one gain that will accrue to the new company will be in the shape of the port dues.

The following coloquy with Mr. C. K. Garrison gives all the particulars upon the matter that are now obtainable:

Mr. Garrison, when found at his office at the Bowling Green, was at first unwilling to communicate what he knew upon this important subject, but finding that the main facts were already known to his visitor became more communicative.

When asked, "Is it really true, that the Tybee has gone out with important lading connected with the new enterprise of the mercantile association that proposes tofollow up the Santo Domingo project?" he replied in substance, "Yes, a number of us, merchants and others, have concluded that we will accept the bargain that the government has refused. This is apart from all political considerations; is a mere matter of business."

"What shape has your action taken?"

"We have raised the money demanded and sent it out in the Tybee, and will thus gain control of the Port of Samana, the finest in the West Indies, and capable of holding the navies of the world."

"You will have special rights and privileges, I suppose—be a sort of western 'East India Company?'"

"Well, we are paying out a great deal of money, and of course we are not giving it for nothing, but expect ample returns."

"Will not this first outlay need to be followed by other large expenditures for warehouses, etc.?"

"We don't know much about what will come after. We shall move cautiously, and be governed by circumstances."

"Are only New York capitalists in the enterprise?"

"No; there are capitalists of Boston, Philadelphia and other towns in the movement."

"It appears to be a legitimate transaction. I suppose that many of you have long had mercantile dealings with those regions?"

"Yes; I, for one, have been running steamers to those islands, as far down as Brazil. I have no connection with the administration, and it is a matter of business with us all."

"This is certainly a good way to settle a vexed question, and prevent further political controversy on the subject. But what is the condition of the island? Is there much industrial activity there?"

"There is a large export trade of coffee and other tropical products; but the point of the greatest importance will be the possession by Americans of such a fine port in the West Indies?
