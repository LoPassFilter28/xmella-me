The Sun reported on the appointment of Walter W. Vick as general receiver of Dominican customs, emphasizing the requirement that he maintain strict political neutrality. The article credited the American receivership with securing revenues from aspiring revolutionists and providing seven years of uninterrupted domestic peace until disturbances resumed in 1912. Vick's mandate was to preserve the integrity and security of customs collections across all contending political factions.

The Sun informó sobre la designación de Walter W. Vick como receptor general de las aduanas dominicanas, enfatizando la exigencia de que mantuviera una estricta neutralidad política. El artículo atribuía a la receptoría estadounidense el mérito de haber puesto los caudales a salvo de las ambiciones revolucionarias y de brindar siete años de ininterrumpida paz interna hasta el resurgimiento de los disturbios en 1912. La misión de Vick consistía en preservar la integridad y seguridad de la recaudación de aduanas frente a todas las facciones políticas en pugna.

After the convention at Baltimore he was made assistant secretary of the Democratic National Committee and had charge of the disbursements. More than $3,000,000 passed through his hands In three months. In December he was designated as secretary of the Inauguration committee and opened headquarters in Washington, from which he directed the arrangements as the personal representative of the President elect. His new appointment involves a radical change. Instead of being in politics he must maintain absolute neutrality In such matters in the Dominican republic. One of the advantages supposed to lie in the American receivership is that the customs revenues are by it removed to neutral hands and are no longer to be preyed upon by aspiring revolutionists or grafting officials. From [illegible], when the first temporary arrangement went into effect, until [illegible], Santo Domingo had its first long period of freedom from revolution and internal strife. The neutralization of the customs receipts has been regarded as the cause of this peace in the turbulent republic. In 1912 there was continued political disturbance, but even during the revolution the custom houses remained unmolested by either faction In the struggle. It will be Mr. Vick's task to continue this absolute security of the customs receipts and maintain perfect neutrality toward all the contending political factions in the Island.
