In a letter to Benjamin F. Wade at Azua, Captain William G. Temple begs him to withhold his consent to a proposed expedition and to ask President Baez to forbid the passage of the party through his lines. However remote the chances of their capture may seem, Temple argues, the chances of deriving any benefit from their journey are more remote still, and he adds that there are other points of view he does not care to commit to writing.

Respectfully and faithfully yours, in great haste. Wm.G. Temple. Hon. B. F. Wade, Azua. P. S.—There are other points of view from which this question can be seen, which I do not care to commit to writing. The Troubles in Mississippi. About ten days ago, our telegraphic dispatches gave an incoherent account of riots and murders in Meridian, Mississippi. The statement was in substance that a large fire had occurred there some days before, that a negro was on trial in the city court, charged with being the incendiary, and that while the trial was in progress, a negro shot the presiding judge, Bramlette, upon which a riot ensued, eight or ten negroes were shot, Mr. Sturges, the mayor of the city, a native of Connecticut, left the place at midnight by order of the mob, with a promise never to return, and the series of crimes was wound up by the burning of the house of another negro and the African church. This story, while it left no doubt that the town had been the scene of riot and outrage, in which the negroes and their friends were the principal sufferers, gave a very obscure account of the origin of the disturbances, with the implication that the colored people were responsible for them.
