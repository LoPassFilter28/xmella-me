The Dolores star reviewed the 1861 annexation of the Dominican Republic to Spain, which took place while the United States was absorbed in the Civil War. Dominican citizens revolted two years later, leading Spain to formally recognize Dominican independence in 1865. The piece noted that President Grant later sought to annex the republic to the United States, but the negotiated treaty failed to pass the U.S. Senate on a tie vote after Dominican voters ratified it.

El Dolores star rememoró la anexión de la República Dominicana a España ocurrida en 1861, mientras los Estados Unidos se hallaban absortos en la Guerra de Secesión. El pueblo dominicano se sublevó dos años más tarde, compeliendo a España a reconocer formalmente la independencia dominicana en 1865. La nota observó que con posterioridad el presidente Grant intentó anexionar el territorio a los Estados Unidos, fracasando el tratado en el Senado de los EE. Spain. The United States was entering upon the Civil war and had neither the time nor the strength for the prompt and effectual enforcement of the Monroe doctrine. One result was that Napoleon III, counting on the success of the Confederacy, constituted the Republic of Mexico an empire and put Archduke Maximilian of Austria on the throne. Two years after the Dominican Republic was annexed to Spain its people revolted and restored the republic. In 1863 Spain acknowledged its independence. Incidentally President Grant desired to annex the republic to the United States. A treaty for this purpose was ratified by the Dominican people, but was rejected by the American senate by a tie vote. At the close of the Civil war in 1865 the United States had both the time and the strength to enforce the Monroe doctrine in Mexico and the French troops withdrew, leaving Maximilian to his fate — execution in 1867 at the hands of the revolutionists. In 1867 Benito Juarez became the first president of the restored Republic of Mexico. Why French Is Now the Language of Diplomancy FRENCH is the official language at the peace conference, and the world is so accustomed to think of French as the language of diplomacy that it is something of a surprise to be reminded that it has not always been so accepted. As recently as 1730 the treaty of Belgrade was written in Latin. Since that time, however, nearly all international negotiations have been carried on in French.
