The Perth Amboy evening news reported on U.S. intervention in Santo Domingo, stating that severe unrest in 1914 and 1915 forced the United States to deploy marines and warships to restore quiet. The article asserted that Dominicans reluctantly accepted external peace enforced under the Monroe Doctrine after customs administration alone failed to prevent renewed revolutions. It characterized the military interventions in Santo Domingo, Haiti, and Nicaragua as costly, altruistic sacrifices made for regional security rather than territorial gain.

El Perth Amboy evening news informó sobre la intervención de los Estados Unidos en Santo Domingo, indicando que los graves disturbios acaecidos en 1914 y 1915 obligaron a Washington a movilizar infantes de marina y escuadras navales para restaurar la calma. El artículo aseveraba que la población dominicana aceptaba a regañadientes una paz exterior impuesta al amparo de la Doctrina Monroe, luego de que la sola administración aduanera resultara ineficaz para evitar la reaparición de revueltas. Calificaba los desembarcos militares en Santo Domingo, Haití y Nicaragua como sacrificios onerosos y altruistas en favor de la seguridad continental antes que en búsqueda de engrandecimiento territorial.

"For a long time it was thought that, deprived of the opportunity of securing customs house revenues through the seizure of ports, revolution could not support themselves. But after six or seven years of peace trouble broke out again, and during 1914 and 1915 it became so serious that the United States was forced to Intervene in behalf of peace and to demand, with marines on shore and naval guns trained and pointed on the ports, that the country return to a state of quiet. And so today the Dominicans, realizing that the Monroe doctrine is determined to afford them protection, from their own excesses, their own bitter passions and blind purposes, have accepted the inevitable and have secured the blessings of peace from without when they could not attain that end themselves. It is a reluctant acquiescence they yield, but a wholesome one, none the less. 'It has not been without effort or without expense, nor yet without the actual sacrifice of blood and life that our country has stepped in to play the role of Good Samaritan to the people of Santo Domingo, Haiti and Nicaragua, who had lost the blessings of peace and were unable to regain them. In Haiti alone we lost one officer and six marines and had a number wounded. But out of this altruistic effort are growing results of which a nation that covets no territory, that seeks only its own security and the welfare of its unfortunate neighbors may well be proud.
