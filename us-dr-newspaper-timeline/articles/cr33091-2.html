The Spanish government, it would appear, are at last becoming convinced of the impossibility of resubjugating St. Domingo, or rather of the uselessness of any further effort to accomplish that object. The Spanish press, which is usually the reflex of public opinion, if not of the government, seems now to be unanimous in advocating the abandonment of the struggle. It is urged that the island is of no real value to Spain; that its possession, so far from adding to her strength, is a source of weakness, and that it is better to withdraw her troops at once than to continue a war which is both costly and disastrous. The Diario Español, an influential organ, says that Spain has done enough to prove her power and to satisfy her honor, and that she can now abandon the enterprise without any loss of prestige. But the truth is that Spanish prestige in America has received a blow from which it will not easily recover. It has been shown that Spain is no longer capable of sustaining any but the very feeblest struggle on this side the Atlantic. This has been shown in the St. Domingo war. However we may rate Spanish power in Europe, Spanish power in America has been proved, by the power of the Dominicans, to be contemptible. Spain, after much effort, has not been able to overcome the resistance of these islanders to her dominion, and her inability to achieve that conquest is admitted in Spain. A Madrid paper urges the Spanish government to make peace at once with the Dominicans on the best terms they can obtain, and to withdraw their troops without delay. This advice will probably be followed, as the Spanish government must be glad of any excuse for putting an end to a war which has cost so many lives and so much money, with no prospect of any beneficial result.
