The Raymer Enterprise reviewed the history of the Dominican customs receivership, noting that removing customs revenues from political grasp had ended chronic revolutions and established domestic peace between 1905 and 1912. The article recalled that President Roosevelt intervened in 1905 to prevent European creditor nations from seizing Dominican customs to collect $20,000,000 in defaulted debts. Newly appointed Receiver Walter W. Vick was charged with maintaining strict administrative neutrality and safeguarding revenue security.

The Raymer Enterprise repasó la trayectoria de la receptoría de aduanas dominicana, destacando que sustraer los caudales aduaneros de la codicia política había suprimido las revoluciones crónicas e instaurado la paz interna entre 1905 y 1912. El artículo recordó que el presidente Roosevelt intervino en 1905 para evitar que las potencias acreedoras europeas se apoderaran de las aduanas dominicanas para cobrar 20.000.000 de dólares en deudas morosas. El recién designado receptor Walter W. Vick tenía por misión preservar una rigurosa neutralidad administrativa y resguardar los fondos fiscales.

supposed to lie in the American receivership is that the customs revenues are by it removed to neutral hands and are no longer to be preyed upon by aspiring revolutionists or grafting officials. From 1905, when the first temporary arrangement went into effect, until 1912 Santo Domingo had its first long period of freedom from revolution and internal strife. The neutralization of the customs receipts has been regarded as the cause of this peace in the turbulent republic. In 1912 there was continued political disturbance, but even during the revolution the custom houses remained unmolested by either faction in the struggle. It will be Mr. Vick's task to continue this absolute security of the customs receipts and maintain perfect neutrality toward all the contending political factions in the Island. European Intervention in Santo Domingo seemed a certainty when President Roosevelt intervened in behalf of the republic. The Dominican government was saddled with a foreign and domestic indebtedness amounting, with interest, to about [illegible]. Far from paying the interest, the Dominican government was increasing the debt by about a million dollars a year. As a result of centuries of misrule the revenues were being dishonestly dissipated. A succession of revolutions had reduced the government simply to a source of graft for which many factions were contending.
