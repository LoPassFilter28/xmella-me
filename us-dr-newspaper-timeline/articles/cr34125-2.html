The Evening star published a historical overview of the Dominican Republic, detailing its initial settlement by Spain, the eradication of indigenous inhabitants, and the importation of enslaved Africans. The article described how an eighteenth-century uprising abolished slavery and expelled colonizers, followed by shifts between Spanish and Haitian rule until the Dominican Republic secured independence in 1844. It observed that despite preserving Spanish language and institutions, the nation experienced frequent political coups and revolutions throughout its history.

El Evening star publicó una reseña histórica de la República Dominicana, detallando su colonización inicial por parte de España, la desaparición de los pobladores indígenas y la importación de africanos esclavizados. El artículo describió cómo un levantamiento en el siglo XVIII abolió la esclavitud y expulsó a los colonizadores, seguido de sucesivas alternancias entre el dominio español y el haitiano hasta que la República Dominicana consolidó su independencia en 1844. Observó que, a pesar de conservar la lengua y las instituciones españolas, la nación experimentó frecuentes golpes políticos y revoluciones a lo largo de su historia.

"Santo Domingo seemed destined to become the bustling metropolis of a western empire. But it became the victim of exploitation. After a turbulent history, during which the native Indians were exterminated and thousands of African slaves were imported, the latter, assisted by mulatto freemen, rose, in the last years of the eighteenth century, abolished slavery, and drove their oppressors from the island. Though the land of the Dominican Republic reverted for a while to Spanish control, and later was conquered by the Republic of Haiti, with which it shares the island, it established its independence in 1844 and has retained its individuality since. "The territory which now constitutes the Dominican Republic has been subject at different periods to a number of nations. It has been twice under the rule of Spain and has been controlled at other times by French, English and Haitians. The Spanish influence has been the strongest, and the language of Spain and many of its customs and institutions are those of the Dominicans of today. "The Dominican Republic is peopled largely by mulattoes. While it has not been as badly torn by revolution as the 'black republic' to the west, its history has by no means been one of peaceful development, and assassinations and coups have played their part in determining the succession of its rulers.
