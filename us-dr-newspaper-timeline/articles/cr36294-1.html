The Dominican republic committed suicide yesterday. I was one of the witnesses of the act, and can testify that it was a very discreditable affair. Never was there seen a more grotesque corpse or a more ridiculous funeral. Santana, the President, has sold the republic to the dead republic, in an honest old fool, and accepted the Spanish knife to cut his country's throat, under the delusion that he was performing a specially religious operation. He is no coward, but as stubborn as a mule, and about as well learned in the grammar of nations as the average of those useful animals. It is impossible to explain his astounding stupidities without this insight into his mental calibre. The Haytiens were always threatening to reconquer the Dominican territory, and he was as mad as those well informed people in seeking Spain to assert a protectorate over this, her former colony of St. Domingo. His best counsellors advised him to form a treaty with the United States, and, by opening the magnificent resources of the island to American enterprise, create in a few years the capital and the interests which would force the Union to protect its own. Unfortunately England and France would not permit this, and as the republic was a necessity to her existence, they compelled this government to cancel a treaty it had just concluded with the United States. Ever since then Spain has been coquetting with the Dominican government for this surrender, body and soul, to the service of Spain, from whence she expects for a cheap penny her own she has it.

The Santana's Cabinet are pure Spaniards, and every one of them would rather wear a gold coat to-day—with a neat pension tacked to it—from the chaste hand of Isabella Segunda, than live the plain citizen, or even first minister, of a simple republic. They are dogs for the chase, and they have scented the Spanish bounty to run down the prey, and bring their country to the feet of Spain. The prime movers—outside of the Cabinet—were the brothers Felipe and Juan Alfau, one a Spanish General, and the other the Vice President of the republic. Santana himself was born in the Spanish portion of the Dominican republic, and was at first treated with the most cutting disdain by the grandees of the court; but when he asserted his power the cooling luxury prevailed; but, then, what to do with the Dominican people? Three members of the Cabinet took charge of that. The transfer was to be effected by a coup de main, and after that, the resistance of the people could be crushed out by Spanish artillery in legal form, and, as General Scott would say, 'According to the science of war.' The war steamer Don Juan de Austria was despatched from Cadiz early last summer to begin the game by landing at St. Domingo no less than ninety secret agents to hunt up proselytes, help set them at work, and report progress to the Spanish government. Spanish steamers of war were continually coming and going between Cuba, St. Domingo and Porto Rico, and their many visits and unusual movements the Cabinet manifested an increased taste and means for the costly luxuries of life. Priests from Spain came as agents with Spanish subjects, their passage and three months' provisions being paid by government. This set the Dominicans wondering, and the local authorities ventured to inquire, but they were told that the Spanish officers and soldiers landed by their war ships, were a few friends from the coast of Spain. Half the time, indeed, they—the officers and soldiers had come to instruct the Dominicans and assist them to repel Haytien invasions, and the other half—why, they were simple visitors to till the ground and develop the stagnant resources of the country! As soon as these visits were without any further movement, the people began to believe that their Cabinet told the truth, and that after all there was no fear of a Spanish protectorate, and for a while there was a general repose throughout the land.

It was during this interval that the Dominicans asked what was done for a treaty with the United States, and had it been accorded to them the destruction of this American State, and all the horrors of a bloody war of races, would have been avoided. Why was it refused? That no one understands.

When all was ready for the treaty with the United States, then, given up, Santana became as anxious as the most Spanish of his friends to be accepted a person beyond redemption. Nothing short of annexation would satisfy him any more. Many times, during this, were emissaries from Cuba to buy up the willing, and plenty of wine and aguardiente were dispensed with the doubloons to silence the refractory; but it was managed so cunningly that everything was kept secret, and when the people made inquiry of, they were even led to suspect they were actually sold to Spain.

A small party of troops was sent, under disguised Spanish officers, to each leading town—at least, thirty, and between five and ten thousand men in all—to shout with them at the same time, 'Long live our mother country!' and plant the Spanish flag in a conspicuous place. When the people assembled, as they naturally would, to inquire into the meaning of this proceeding, a speech was made to the effect that the Queen of Spain, having learned the ferocious determination of the Haytiens to invade their villages with fire and sword, she resolved to protect them from all these evils and all the horrors of war; that it wasn't their liberties, but, on the contrary, she yearned to shower upon them all the blessings of peace and prosperity, and as a first evidence of it she proposed to exchange their depreciated paper money for gold, gold and silver. Some of the local magistrates were hesitant to have tested this last promise, and found it true in their own cases. They touched Spanish gold for their paper, but on condition that they added their official signature to the request written for them at St. Domingo, that their districts had pronounced for annexation to Spain.

A powerful and fast steam fleet of vessels came in together about the 18th and 20th of March, and on them it was reported to everyone that 'Dominican republic' is province of Spain, by the act of the people; but the presence of the war vessels was so many symbols of the Spanish power that it was useless to attempt to protest. Instead of waiting until the 25th inst., as originally intended, Santana hastily threw up the Spanish flag on the 18th, in order to employ the Spanish troops in crushing out whatever resistance was shown by the pride of their 'tender mother country.' Santana made short work of it. He told the people that their country was now 'the province of St. Domingo and a part of the kingdom of Spain,' and on the 18th he showed himself on the balcony of the national palace and raised the Spanish flag amid the roar of Spanish artillery and the music of the Spanish bands, without even a cheer. Even the Dominican traitors, who have made their own fortunes by selling their country, could not hide the grim grief of the citizens present by lending their voices to this shameful act of treason—not even! The citizens were ordered to assemble in the Plaza to hear the decree of the sale of the republic to Spain, but hardly one went that was not specially ordered to be present under pain of imprisonment. Such was poor Santo Domingo, while Santana gave in his allegiance to Queen Isabella, and Dominican gold—no, the gold that was offered by Spaniards—and, each of the double traitors was to have his reward. Is there no hope for traitors? Yes; even that will not keep down the people.

The following is the

Protest of the Foreign Consuls—Feeling in the Country—Dominican Currency, &c., &c.

Of course it will be made to appear that this conquest is the will of the nation; that, tired of internal dissensions and dreading an invasion from Hayti, the young republic, like a wayward and weary child, has sought to repose again in the arms of its old mother. This is all false. Fear alone of the power of the sword prevents a [illegible] speak openly denouncing the treason, and in the province, I am told, there is a determination not to submit to the annexation without a struggle. All the people's eyes are turned anxiously to the United States. There they believe they may expect some sympathy. It is true, the French and English Consuls have protested, struck their flags, &c., but the people here have no faith in these gentry. They say it is not in the order of things for monarchies to protect republics, except with the ulterior design of swallowing them. Gen. Cazneau, our diplomatic agent here, has been strongly appealed to to protest against this usurpation of Old Spain but, although I believe the Texan hero has no particular affection for the flag which his friend Quitman styled 'the Pirate Flag of Blood and Gold,' I have not heard that he has acted in the matter as yet.
