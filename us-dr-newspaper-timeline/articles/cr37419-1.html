The Anaconda Standard recounted the political fallout from President Grant’s attempt to annex Santo Domingo, highlighting the favorable report by commissioners Benjamin Wade, Samuel Gridley Howe, and Andrew D. White. The proposal provoked a fierce Senate battle led by Charles Sumner, causing the treaty’s defeat, a lasting personal breach between Grant and Sumner, and Sumner's removal as chairman of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee.

The Anaconda Standard relató las repercusiones políticas del intento del presidente Grant de anexionar Santo Domingo, destacando el dictamen favorable de los comisionados Benjamin Wade, Samuel Gridley Howe y Andrew D. White. La propuesta provocó una encarnizada batalla en el Senado liderada por Charles Sumner, lo que ocasionó el fracaso del tratado, un distanciamiento personal duradero entre Grant y Sumner y la destitución de este último como presidente del Comité de Relaciones Exteriores del Senado.

a salary for the commissioners. The president named Benjamin F. Wade, Samuel G. Howe and Andrew D. White — the last named is one of the Venezuelan commissioners. These gentlemen visited San Domingo. They studied the situation thoroughly, became convinced that President Grant was right and so reported. Then the trouble began. There followed in the senate one of the most ill-tempered debates ever heard in congress. Charles Sumner led in the opposition — he made a terrific attack on the president's conduct; he went farther than most of the senators felt was right. The annexation scheme failed, and the smoke of the battle hung heavily and long. Thereafter General Grant and Senator Sumner had no personal intercourse. A few months afterward, by action of the senate, Sumner lost the chairmanship of the committee on foreign relations, and he refused committee appointments to which he was assigned. From that time until the close of his life Senator Sumner, brilliant as had been his career and splendid his ability, was in an uncomfortable place, although, just as the end was approaching, he appeared to be treated by the republican senators with renewed friendliness. He died three years and a day after his chairmanship was taken from him; meanwhile, the San Domingo annexation scheme had passed out of the minds of the people.
