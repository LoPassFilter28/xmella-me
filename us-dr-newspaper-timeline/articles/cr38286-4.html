WE SCARED HAYTI NOBLY.

A War of Etiquette, Too, In Which Admiral Luce Came Out Strong.

ON BOARD THE UNITED STATES STEAMSHIP GALENA, OFF PORT-AU-PRINCE, Dec. 21.—

The United States steamships Galena and Yantic, which left New York Dec. 12, steamed into the harbor of Port-au-Prince Dec. 20, at 9 A. M., with shotted guns, torpedo booms, ships cleared for action, and crews standing at quarters, to demand of the alleged Haytian Government the instant release of the steamer Haytian Republic. On their arrival in the outer harbor Rear Admiral S. B. Luce transferred his flag to the Yantic, which has a draught of but fourteen feet. The Yantic was then run slowly into the inner harbor to within 100 feet of the docks where the Haytien Republic was at anchor. Anchor was dropped, and a stern line was at once attached to the Haytien Republic, the guns of the Yantic being trained on the city. The Galena remained in the outer harbor with a broadside and pivot guns trained on the Haytian man-of-war Dessalines, and prepared to fire at the first hostile demonstration. The Dessalines' crew also stood at their guns until the result of the interview of Lieut. Griffin with Gen. Légitime was made known.

Lieut. Griffin went ashore shortly after 9 o'clock with communications from the State Department and Admiral Luce demanding the release within four hours of the detained steamer and the withdrawal of her guard. Admiral Luce's message was to this effect, only more polite:

"I want the Haytien Republic. You will turn her over to me by 1 P. M. to-day or I will send men on board and tow her out. Resistance by force will necessitate my using force and shelling your town in return."

While this was doing we could see ammunition put on board the Dessalines and other gunboats, and could see them load their guns. The reply to the Admiral's message was to this purport:

"We will give her up if the United States Government demands her, but we do it under formal protest, and reserve our right to bring our claims against your Government to arbitration. She was aiding and abetting revolutionists."

A point in etiquette international was raised in that Admiral Luce had not saluted on entering the harbor. His reply was that the Galena had saluted the Haytian flag in this harbor within six months, and it was not our habit to do it more frequently. Admiral Luce made the point that the commander of the Dessalines, a Commodore, had failed to call on him, Luce. The reply made in person by the Commodore, in a very dignified and courteous manner, was that it was so unusual for foreign men-of-war to go so far in as the Yantic had done, and to do it with men at quarters, that he, the Commodore, thought possibly our visit was not friendly in its motive.

This exchange of lessons in politeness was followed by the surrender of the Haytien Republic some hours before the time set by Admiral Luce. Gen. Légitime commanded one of his gunboats to tow the Haytien Republic to the position in the harbor occupied when captured, and to have such slight damage as had been done to her repaired by his mechanics. Night fell before her cable could be severed, as had been planned.
