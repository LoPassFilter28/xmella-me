The Daily Exchange

The Daily Exchange cites a letter from Porto Plata dated November 10

January 1, 18611 de enero de 1861

The Daily Exchange cites a letter from Porto Plata dated November 10 reporting that the Dominican Republic (referred to as "the little Republic there known as Dominica") has been sold to Spain for half a million dollars. According to the letter, the bargain was made in March but kept secret for three months to smooth matters with the population and to see whether the people of the United States would object to this violation of the Monroe Doctrine. The report adds that the Dominican commissioner who negotiated the sale took his family to Spain and sold his property on the island as soon as the business was settled, and that Minister of Foreign Relations Don Miguel Lavastida and Minister of War and Marine Don Felipe de Castro have done the same and stand ready to flee at a moment's notice.

The Daily Exchange cita una carta de Puerto Plata fechada el 10 de noviembre que informa que la República Dominicana (denominada «la pequeña República allí conocida como Dominica») ha sido vendida a España por medio millón de dólares. Según la carta, el trato se cerró en el mes de marzo pero se mantuvo en secreto durante tres meses para suavizar la situación con el pueblo y ver si el pueblo de los Estados Unidos objetaría esta violación de la doctrina Monroe. El informe añade que el comisionado dominicano que negoció la venta llevó a su familia a España y vendió sus propiedades en la isla tan pronto como se concretó el negocio, y que el ministro de Relaciones Exteriores, don Miguel Lavastida, y el de Guerra y Marina, don Felipe de Castro, han hecho lo mismo y están listos para huir en cualquier momento.

Daily National Intelligencer

The Daily National Intelligencer describes the geography of the island of Haiti

April 3, 18613 de abril de 1861

The Daily National Intelligencer describes the geography of the island of Haiti, noting that the western third forms the Republic of Haiti with its capital at Port-au-Prince, while the more mountainous eastern division constitutes the Dominican Republic (also referred to as the "Republic of Dominica" and "San Domingo"), with San Domingo as its capital. Pointing out that the island is separated from Cuba by the twenty-five-mile Windward Passage and from Puerto Rico by the twenty-five-mile Mona Passage, the paper observes that any power holding all three islands would command the ocean gateways to the Gulf of Mexico, the Caribbean Sea, and Central America. The editors explain that they detail this geography because intelligence from Havana states that Spain has seized the Dominican Republic and is about to re-annex it to the Spanish Crown, a report they consider not at all improbable.

El Daily National Intelligencer describe la geografía de la isla de Haití, señalando que el tercio occidental forma la República de Haití con su capital en Puerto Príncipe, mientras que la división oriental, mucho más montañosa, constituye la República Dominicana (también denominada «República de Dominica» y «Santo Domingo»), con Santo Domingo como capital. Al destacar que la isla está separada de Cuba por el Paso de los Vientos, de veinticinco millas, y de Puerto Rico por el Canal de la Mona, también de veinticinco millas, el periódico observa que cualquier potencia que posea las tres islas dominaría las vías marítimas de acceso al Golfo de México, el Mar Caribe y Centroamérica. Los editores explican que detallan esta situación geográfica porque informes procedentes de La Habana indican que España se ha apoderado de la República Dominicana y está a punto de reanexarla a la Corona española, noticia que consideran nada improbable.

New-York Dispatch

The New-York Dispatch contrasts the two nationalities of the island

April 6, 18616 de abril de 1861

The New-York Dispatch contrasts the two nationalities of the island, identifying the eastern part as the Dominican Republic—also called St. Domingo or the Spanish part—and the western part as the Republic of Haiti. According to the article, the Dominican Republic has a population of 120,000, chiefly Spanish-speaking mulattoes who were formerly subjects of Spain, and is ruled by President Santana, whom it describes as a nearly white leader whose sympathies have always favored reuniting his country with Spain. By contrast, Haiti has a French-speaking population estimated at 600,000 to 1,200,000 and is ruled by President Geffrard, whose aim is to establish an independent nationality composed exclusively of men of African descent. The paper concludes that there is no probability either that the Dominicans will submit to Spanish domination or that the Haitians will allow French whites to become their masters.

El New-York Dispatch contrasta las dos nacionalidades que ocupan la isla, identificando la parte oriental como la República Dominicana —también llamada Santo Domingo o la parte española— y la parte occidental como la República de Haití. Según el artículo, la República Dominicana tiene una población de 120,000 habitantes, principalmente mulatos hispanohablantes que fueron súbditos de España, y está gobernada por el presidente Santana, a quien describe como un gobernante casi blanco cuyas simpatías siempre han favorecido la reunión de su país con España. En contraste, Haití tiene una población francófona estimada entre 600,000 y 1,200,000 habitantes y está gobernada por el presidente Geffrard, cuyo objetivo es establecer una nacionalidad independiente compuesta exclusivamente por hombres de ascendencia africana. El periódico concluye que no hay probabilidad alguna de que los dominicanos se sometan a la dominación española ni de que los haitianos permitan que los blancos franceses se conviertan en sus amos.

Springfield Weekly Republican

The Springfield Weekly Republican remarks on a sensational report of the week

April 6, 18616 de abril de 1861

The Springfield Weekly Republican remarks on a sensational report of the week, which claims a Spanish colony has instigated a revolution in St. Domingo and that a Spanish fleet has sailed from Havana to subjugate and re-annex the whole island to Spain with France's consent. The paper argues that any attempt to subjugate the Haitian Republic is improbable and would be opposed by both France and England, suggesting instead that the affair likely stems from an internal outbreak against Dominican President Santana, who is heavily indebted to Spain, has previously tried to sell out to the Spanish government, and probably obtained aid from Spanish forces in Cuba against domestic resistance. The editorial concludes that such a situation hardly warrants anxiety over an infraction of the Monroe Doctrine, adding that rumors of a united British and French fleet sailing for American waters under sealed orders likely refer only to a routine fleet of observation.

El Springfield Weekly Republican señala que una noticia sensacionalista de la semana afirma que una colonia española ha promovido una revolución en Santo Domingo y que una flota española ha zarpado de La Habana para subyugar y reanexar toda la isla a España con el consentimiento de Francia. El periódico sostiene que cualquier intento de subyugar a la República de Haití es improbable y contaría con la oposición de Francia e Inglaterra, sugiriendo en cambio que el asunto probablemente se debe a un levantamiento interno contra el presidente dominicano Santana, quien está fuertemente endeudado con España, ha intentado anteriormente vender el país al gobierno español y probablemente obtuvo ayuda de las fuerzas españolas en Cuba frente a la resistencia interna. El editorial concluye que tal situación difícilmente justifica inquietud por una infracción de la doctrina Monroe, y añade que los rumores sobre una flota conjunta británica y francesa rumbo a aguas americanas bajo órdenes selladas probablemente se refieren solo a una habitual flota de observación.

April 8, 18618 de abril de 1861

Under the headline "Spain and the Dominican Republic," the Daily Whig of Richmond observes that rumors concerning Spain's designs against the Dominican Republic (referred to as "the Republic of Dominica") draw attention to the military and naval strength of the Madrid government. Citing a statement from the New York World, the article notes that in 1860 the Spanish navy numbered 137 vessels—including 49 ships-of-war, war steamers, and other war vessels—in addition to smaller coast-service craft, three ships-of-the-line with 1,000-horse auxiliary steam-power on the stocks, and five steam frigates. The piece also points to the size of Spain's regular army organization, including 10 captain-generals and 148 major-generals, and reports that in Cuba Spain maintains eight regiments of veterans, one regiment of artillery, a brigade of five batteries, one company of workmen, two regiments of cavalry, one battalion of engineers, and a large militia force across the principal ports and towns.

Bajo el titular «España y la República Dominicana», el Richmond Daily Whig observa que los rumores sobre los designios de España contra la República Dominicana (denominada «la República de Dominica») llaman la atención sobre la fuerza militar y naval del gobierno de Madrid. Citando un informe del New York World, el artículo señala que en 1860 la armada española contaba con 137 buques —incluidos 49 buques de guerra, vapores de guerra y otras embarcaciones bélicas—, además de embarcaciones menores para el servicio costero, tres navíos de línea con potencia auxiliar de vapor de 1,000 caballos en astillero y cinco fragatas de vapor. Wilmington Journal

The Wilmington Journal comments on the reported intention of Spain

April 11, 186111 de abril de 1861

The Wilmington Journal comments on the reported intention of Spain to reconquer the Dominican Republic, noting that it is a former Spanish colony whose independence the Spanish government has never formally acknowledged. The paper mocks a Dominican call to arms declaring that the inhabitants would "make a fight of giants which will astonish the whole world, and make the earth tremble under our feet," contrasting the boast with the republic's total population of 126,500. Despite denials by the Spanish minister, the article states that Spain genuinely appears to intend to assert authority over San Domingo, predicting that Spain will take the eastern part of the island, known as the Dominican Republic, while France will take the western part, known as the Republic of Haiti.

El Wilmington Journal comenta la anunciada intención de España de reconquistar la República Dominicana, señalando que se trata de una antigua colonia española cuya independencia el gobierno español nunca ha reconocido formalmente. El periódico ironiza sobre un llamado a las armas dominicano que declara que sus habitantes harían «una lucha de gigantes que asombrará al mundo entero y hará temblar la tierra bajo nuestros pies», contrastando la proclama con la población total de la república de 126,500 habitantes. A pesar de los desmentidos del ministro español, el artículo afirma que España realmente parece tener la intención de imponer su autoridad sobre Santo Domingo, y predice que España tomará la parte oriental de la isla, conocida como la República Dominicana, mientras que Francia tomará la parte occidental, conocida como la República de Haití.

Anti-Slavery Bugle

The Anti-Slavery Bugle reprints "The Dominican Republic," an article

April 13, 186113 de abril de 1861

The Anti-Slavery Bugle reprints "The Dominican Republic," an article from the New-York Tribune, noting that for nearly seventy years the island of Haiti—formerly called Hispaniola and later St. Domingo—has been a theater of revolutions since the first African slave was brought there. Situated fifty-four miles from Cuba and once known as the "Garden of the Antilles," the island was divided almost equally at the outbreak of the French Revolution into a French and a Spanish colony. According to the article, the wealthy white slaveholding proprietors of the French colony initially embraced principles of liberty and equality for themselves, before those principles spread downward through society. The account adds that in 1791 the French National Assembly decreed civil freedom for persons of color born of free parents, that universal freedom was proclaimed to the slaves in 1793, and that the Spanish colony was subsequently ceded to France.

Reproduciendo un artículo titulado «La República Dominicana» del New-York Tribune, el Anti-Slavery Bugle señala que durante casi setenta años la isla de Haití —antes llamada La Española y luego Santo Domingo— ha sido escenario de revoluciones desde que fue llevado allí el primer esclavo africano. Situada a cincuenta y cuatro millas de Cuba y conocida en otro tiempo como el «Jardín de las Antillas», la isla estaba dividida casi en partes iguales al estallar la Revolución Francesa en una colonia francesa y otra española. Según el artículo, los ricos propietarios blancos de esclavos de la colonia francesa abrazaron inicialmente los principios de libertad e igualdad para sí mismos, antes de que dichos principios penetraran hacia los estratos inferiores de la sociedad. El relato añade que en 1791 la Asamblea Nacional Francesa decretó la libertad civil para las personas de color nacidas de padres libres, que en 1793 se proclamó la libertad universal para los esclavos y que posteriormente la colonia española fue cedida a Francia.

Following the closing lines of a proclamation dated Santo Domingo

April 16, 186116 de abril de 1861

Following the closing lines of a proclamation dated Santo Domingo, March 10, 1861, and signed by Santana with cries of long live Doña Isabel II and liberty, The Daily Green Mountain Freeman presents an account of the Dominican portion of the island in view of intelligence that it has been re-annexed to Spain. The paper describes the island of Haiti as the largest, richest, and most important of the West India islands next to Cuba, noting that since its independence it has been divided into Haiti, a former French colony occupying about two-fifths of its area, and the "republic of Dominica" (the Dominican Republic), a former Spanish colony holding the remaining three-fifths. According to the report, the Dominican Republic has a population of about 105,000, roughly one-tenth of whom are white, and maintains an army of 20,000 men and a navy of seven small vessels. Holmes County Republican

A correspondent in the Holmes County Republican reports that since

April 18, 186118 de abril de 1861

A correspondent in the Holmes County Republican reports that since the independence of the Dominican Republic, two parties led respectively by Santana and Baez have divided and alternately governed the country under policies represented by the words "reunion" and "independence." Some years earlier, Santana borrowed a million dollars from Spain and, failing to repay the loan according to its terms, offered a mortgage on the custom houses, resulting in the arrival the previous summer of no more than eighty Spaniards at St. Domingo, including three officials who immediately assumed duties as port collectors while the rest were mechanics. According to the writer, that group constituted all the "emigration" Spain had sent to the Dominican Republic, and Santana's policy united the opposition against his government with accusations that he was selling the country to the whites. Although revolutionary circulars and proclamations were in type in St. Domingo as early as December and proofs were shown to the ambassador at Paris, the project was nipped in the bud, Baez remains in exile, and Santana has called for aid as opposition intrigues continue and increase.

Un corresponsal del Holmes County Republican informa que, desde la independencia de la República Dominicana, dos partidos encabezados respectivamente por Santana y Báez han dividido y gobernado alternativamente el país bajo políticas representadas por las palabras «reunión» e «independencia». Algunos años atrás, Santana tomó prestado un millón de dólares de España y, al no poder pagarlo según los términos del préstamo, ofreció una hipoteca sobre las aduanas, lo que dio lugar a la llegada el verano anterior de no más de ochenta españoles a Santo Domingo, entre ellos tres funcionarios que asumieron de inmediato sus cargos como recaudadores en los puertos mientras que el resto eran mecánicos. Según el autor, ese grupo constituía toda la «emigración» enviada por España a la República Dominicana, y la política de Santana unió a la oposición contra su gobierno bajo la acusación de que estaba vendiendo el país a los blancos. Aunque ya en diciembre circulaban en imprenta proclamas y circulares revolucionarias en Santo Domingo cuyas pruebas fueron mostradas al embajador en París, el proyecto fue sofocado en ciernes, Báez permanece en el exilio y Santana ha solicitado ayuda ante el continuo aumento de las intrigas opositoras.

A correspondent in the New-York Dispatch laments what he calls the funeral obsequies of the Dominican Republic, asserting that Spain has bought up and intimidated President Santana and the heads of the Dominican forces so that the Spanish flag of "blood and gold" now floats from the bastions of Santo Domingo. According to the writer, the only free and constitutional state in the West Indies was stifled on March 18, 1861, by a select corps of traitors backed by a force of Spanish soldiers and Spanish bribe-money. The correspondent states that the late President of the United States had been informed of the plan to end the Dominican Republic by high-ranking Cubans and must also have been apprised of it by his own envoys at Madrid and Santo Domingo, yet took no notice of it. Two years earlier, La Presse and La Patrie of Paris had published articles on suppressing the Dominican Republic as semi-official feelers to gauge international reaction to converting a free state on the borders of the American Union into a Spanish coolie colony, while the Spanish press began patronizing its former colony as rich in natural wealth but lacking the political strength required of a nation.

American newspapers covered the events of 1861 to 1865 through the lens of their own Civil War. When President Pedro Santana annexed the Dominican Republic to Spain in 1861, and when Dominicans rose against Spanish rule in 1863, American editors weighed the story in terms of Union and Confederate strategy, the future of slavery in the Caribbean, and old arguments about European empires in the Americas. Many condemned Spain's return; few doubted the island mattered mainly for what it meant to larger powers.Los periódicos estadounidenses cubrieron los hechos de 1861 a 1865 con el lente de su propia Guerra Civil. Cuando el presidente Pedro Santana anexó la República Dominicana a España en 1861, y cuando los dominicanos se levantaron contra el dominio español en 1863, los editores estadounidenses pesaron la historia en términos de la estrategia de la Unión y la Confederación, el futuro de la esclavitud en el Caribe y viejos argumentos sobre los imperios europeos en las Américas. Muchos condenaron el regreso de España; pocos dudaron de que la isla importaba sobre todo por lo que significaba para las potencias mayores.

Dominicans lived those years as betrayal and then as a second founding. Santana's handover was answered from the countryside: the Grito de Capotillo of August 16, 1863, opened the Restoration War, and the fighters who waged it — Gregorio Luperón among them — drove Spain out by 1865. What American papers filed as foreign news is remembered in the Dominican Republic as the war that won independence a second time.Los dominicanos vivieron esos años como una traición y luego como una segunda fundación. La entrega de Santana fue respondida desde el campo: el Grito de Capotillo del 16 de agosto de 1863 abrió la Guerra de la Restauración, y quienes la pelearon — Gregorio Luperón entre ellos — expulsaron a España en 1865. Lo que los periódicos estadounidenses archivaron como noticia extranjera se recuerda en la República Dominicana como la guerra que ganó la independencia por segunda vez.
