The Milwaukee leader asserted that the Dominican Republic was peaceful and paying foreign debts ahead of schedule when American naval forces seized Santo Domingo City in 1916. Evening Star

Eugene H. Hathaway, director general of posts and telegraphs of the Dominican Republic

March 24, 192224 de marzo de 1922

Eugene H. Hathaway, director general of posts and telegraphs of the Dominican Republic and former supervisor of the U.S. Post Office Department's rural free delivery service, died in Santo Domingo City on March 3 after an illness of several months. Born in Wellesley, Massachusetts, on March 21, 1849, Hathaway previously worked as a gold mine superintendent in Sonora, Mexico, a Chicago newspaper correspondent during the Spanish-American War, and head of the postal service in Porto Rico for nine years before accepting his Dominican post in 1917. He was the father of Eugene P. D. Hathaway, and his funeral services took place in Wellesley on Sunday.

Eugene H. Hathaway, director general de correos y telégrafos de la República Dominicana y exsupervisor del servicio de entrega rural gratuita del Departamento de Correos de los Estados Unidos, falleció en la ciudad de Santo Domingo el 3 de marzo tras una enfermedad de varios meses. Nacido en Wellesley, Massachusetts, el 21 de marzo de 1849, Hathaway trabajó anteriormente como superintendente de una mina de oro en Sonora, México, corresponsal periodístico de Chicago durante la guerra hispano-estadounidense y jefe del servicio postal en Puerto Rico durante nueve años antes de aceptar su cargo dominicano en 1917. Era padre de Eugene P. D. Hathaway, y sus servicios funerarios se celebraron en Wellesley el domingo.

The Maryland women's news published Horace G. Knowles's account of the 1916 American intervention in the Dominican Republic, alleging that President Wilson secretly deployed naval forces to seize the nation without congressional approval or a war declaration. Buques de guerra desembarcaron a infantes de marina bajo el fuego de cobertura de su artillería, tras lo cual las autoridades estadounidenses presentaron al presidente Francisco Henríquez y Carvajal un tratado que subordinaba la soberanía nacional. Al rechazar Henríquez las condiciones, el comandante naval estadounidense inició el pleno sometimiento militar de la república.
