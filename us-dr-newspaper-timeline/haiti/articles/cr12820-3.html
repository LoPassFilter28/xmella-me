If you are awake at a late hour of the

evening at Port-au-Prince you will hear

a dull, monotonous sound wafted down

the heavy air from the direction of that

portion of the town which lies near the

plain of the Cul-de-Sac. It is the noise

of the bamboula or Congo drum—weird,

uncanny and suggestive. The beats

are low and at regular intervals.

Black men and women dance the un­

couth measures they witnessed in their

childhood, the same as their fathers

and mothers beheld in their childhood

in Africa. To dance the bamboula is

one of the few apparent pastimes the

Haytian negro enjoys. The drum is

also an invariable associate to a vau­

daux meeting. Without it the black

man would be bereft of his closes*

intimate friend.

St. John relates in great detail the
