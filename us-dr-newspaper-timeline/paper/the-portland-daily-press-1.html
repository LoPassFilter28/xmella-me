The naval station made over to the United States by the Dominican Republic

August 29, 186729 de agosto de 1867

The naval station made over to the United States by the Dominican Republic is described by the Evening Post, in a description the Press reprints under the heading 'Our Newest Possessions.' It is situated on the south side of the peninsula of Samana, in the island of Hayti. The bay of Samana is almost completely land-locked, forty-three miles long by eight in width, with an entrance but three miles wide; it is capacious enough to contain any fleet that might be collected, and its shape affords a harbor where vessels can anchor with perfect safety. The Yuna, the largest river of Hayti, empties into its western end, and the small town of Santa Barbara stands on its northern shore.

La estación naval cedida a los Estados Unidos por la República Dominicana es descrita por el Evening Post, en una descripción que la Press reproduce bajo el título «Our Newest Possessions». Está situada en el lado sur de la península de Samaná, en la isla de Haití. La bahía de Samaná está casi completamente cerrada por tierra, con cuarenta y tres millas de largo por ocho de ancho y una entrada de solo tres millas; es lo bastante capaz para contener cualquier flota que pudiera reunirse, y su forma ofrece un puerto donde los buques pueden anclar con perfecta seguridad. El Yuna, el río más grande de Haití, desemboca en su extremo occidental, y el pequeño pueblo de Santa Bárbara se encuentra en su costa norte.

It emphasizes that no regular telegraphic or shipping channels connect Dominican ports to points in contact with the United States at this season, meaning news cannot be expected until the steamer Tybee arrives in New York in about ten days. The Press prints part of a feature on Samana bay in the West Indies

November 28, 190328 de noviembre de 1903

The Press prints part of a feature on Samana bay in the West Indies: the bay, one of the fine and valuable bays in the West Indies, thirty-one miles long, lies at the eastern extremity of the island, just across from Porto Rico; for almost one hundred years the United States has sought possession of this bay as a strategic point in the Caribbean sea; General Jackson first recommended the purchase in a message to Congress, President Buchanan made two or three recommendations, and Presidents Lincoln and Johnson followed with like suggestions; able engineers were sent to report on the waters and various conditions, and the project finally culminated in President Grant's open effort to secure the bay for the United States, which was defeated by Sumner in the famous and bitter fight along about 1871; the report of the commission sent to Santo Domingo at that time is an elaborate and interesting document, and while no President has openly taken any stand since Grant's day the statesmen have all recognized the value of the possession, and it is an open secret in Washington that negotiations have been opened several times through the State Department for the purchase of the bay; the failure of the project was something General Grant never forgot, it was the most bitter defeat he ever received, and every message, including his last, referred to the mistake which had been made in not acquiring the bay of Samana; and under the head 'Efforts of the Powers' the feature adds that rumor reaches this country occasionally of the attempted purchase of ports in Hayti and Santo Domingo by European powers.
