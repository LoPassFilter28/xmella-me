The Southern Herald

President Ramon Caceres of the Dominican Republic and his career

January 5, 19125 de enero de 1912

President Ramon Caceres of the Dominican Republic and his career are detailed in an article in The Southern Herald, following his assassination by political malcontents in San Domingo city. His life was shaped by political tragedies dating back to 1884, when his father was executed by order of President Heureaux on suspicion of sympathizing with dissenters. Caceres studied in the United States, graduated from the Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute in Troy, New York, and married Lillian, the adopted daughter of Mr. and Mrs. James Hakes. His political career commenced when he avenged his father by assassinating President Heureaux on July 28, 1899.

El presidente Ramón Cáceres de la República Dominicana y su trayectoria son detallados en un artículo en The Southern Herald, tras su asesinato a manos de descontentos políticos en la ciudad de Santo Domingo. Su vida estuvo marcada por tragedias políticas que se remontaban a 1884, cuando su padre fue ejecutado por orden del presidente Heureaux bajo sospecha de simpatizar con disidentes. Cáceres estudió en los Estados Unidos, se graduó en el Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute en Troy, Nueva York, y contrajo matrimonio con Lillian, hija adoptiva de los señores James Hakes. Su carrera política comenzó formalmente cuando vengó a su padre asesinando al presidente Heureaux el 28 de julio de 1899.

September 25, 191225 de septiembre de 1912

The Washington Herald reported that U.S. Commissioners Doyle and McIntyre were sent with marines to re-establish Dominican border custom houses and protect personnel under the 1907 treaty. The report reviewed the convention's financial terms, which scaled public debt to $17,000,000 and issued $20,000,000 in bonds through New York trusts. Increased customs revenues under American administration had enabled annual principal payments to reach $400,000, well above required minimums.

El informe repasó las estipulaciones financieras de la convención, que reajustó la deuda pública a 17.000.000 de dólares y emitió 20.000.000 de dólares en bonos a través de fideicomisos neoyorquinos. El incremento de las rentas aduaneras bajo la gestión estadounidense había permitido que las amortizaciones anuales de capital alcanzaran los 400.000 dólares, muy por encima de los mínimos requeridos.

October 19, 191219 de octubre de 1912

The Daily Banner reports that the Taft administration has devised a plan for virtually annexing Santo Domingo, formulated by Secretary of State Knox, Assistant Secretary Wilson, General McIntyre, Mr. Doyle, and Dominican minister Francisco J. Peynado. The dispatch explains that the ongoing revolution was instigated by political factions in Santo Domingo seeking more money after growing dissatisfied with the revenues allocated to the Dominican government by the American supervisor of customs. While the uprising initially disrupted the State Department's designs, the disturbance provided the justification to land marines and open a general investigation, advancing a broader plan to absorb Santo Domingo as a colonial possession pending congressional approval.

The Daily Banner informa que el gobierno de Taft ha elaborado un plan para prácticamente anexionar Santo Domingo, formulado por el secretario de Estado Knox, el subsecretario Wilson, el general McIntyre, el señor Doyle y el ministro dominicano Francisco J. Peynado. El despacho explica que la revolución en curso fue promovida por facciones políticas en Santo Domingo que buscaban mayores fondos tras descontentarse con las rentas asignadas al gobierno dominicano por el supervisor de aduanas estadounidense. Si bien la sublevación perturbó en un principio los planes del Departamento de Estado, el conflicto brindó la justificación necesaria para desembarcar infantes de marina y abrir una investigación general, promoviendo un proyecto más amplio para absorber a Santo Domingo como posesión colonial, sujeto a la aprobación del Congreso.

The Catholic Bulletin

The Bulletin prints a letter signed S. L. Theobald: he noticed

August 16, 191316 de agosto de 1913

The Bulletin prints a letter signed S. L. Theobald: he noticed, in its issue of August 9, a suggestion, attributed to Assistant Secretary of State Osborne, 'that the bones of Christopher Columbus which are at Santo Domingo City be placed aboard the first ship to go through the Panama Canal.' If this is correct, and, as stated, the result of the secretary's visit to the Dominican Republic and Hayti, he is afraid he did not profit by his visit to the islands so far as historical accuracy is concerned. Not since Hispaniola passed into the hands of the French in 1796 have Columbus' bones lain on San Domingan soil, nor have they found a resting place anywhere in the New World since the close of the Spanish-American War, for in 1898 they were transferred from Havana to Seville, where, in the cathedral, they are to be found at the present time.

The Bulletin publica una carta de S. L. Theobald: en su ejemplar del 9 de agosto vio una sugerencia, atribuida al subsecretario de Estado Osborne, 'de que los huesos de Cristóbal Colón que están en la ciudad de Santo Domingo sean colocados a bordo del primer barco que pase por el Canal de Panamá'. Si esto es correcto y, como se afirma, el resultado de la visita del secretario a la República Dominicana y a Haití, teme no haber aprovechado su visita a las islas en lo que a exactitud histórica se refiere. Desde que La Española pasó a manos de los franceses en 1796, los huesos de Colón no han reposado en suelo dominicano, ni han encontrado lugar de descanso en ninguna parte del Nuevo Mundo desde el fin de la guerra hispanoamericana, pues en 1898 fueron trasladados de La Habana a Sevilla, donde, en la catedral, se encuentran en la actualidad.

The Union prints a Washington dispatch: the cruiser at Guantanamo

September 10, 191310 de septiembre de 1913

The Union prints a Washington dispatch: the cruiser at Guantanamo was expected to sail tonight secretly for Puerto Plata. The Evening Star prints an item: a new State has been formed in the rebellious northern territory by the secessionists, creating an unprecedented situation in the West Indian republic, according to advices received by Dominicans in this city today. The news which they consider of the greatest importance is that Ricardo Limardo, minister of public works and communications in the Bordas government, has resigned and joined the forces of the secession movement in the north. The secessionists in Puerto Plata have formed a provisional government with Horacio Vasquez at the head. According to a cable despatch received by F. Deetjen, the consul general of Santo Domingo in New York, the Dominican war vessel Jacaqua has left Santo Domingo for Puerto Plata with between five and six hundred of the best trained soldiers in the country on board.

The Evening Star publica una nota: formado por los secesionistas un nuevo Estado en el territorio rebelde del norte, se ha creado una situación sin precedentes en la república antillana, según informes recibidos por los dominicanos de esta ciudad hoy. La noticia que consideran de la mayor importancia es que Ricardo Limardo, ministro de Obras Públicas y Comunicaciones del gobierno de Bordas, ha renunciado y se ha unido a las fuerzas del movimiento secesionista del norte. Los secesionistas de Puerto Plata han formado un gobierno provisional con Horacio Vásquez a la cabeza. Según un despacho por cable recibido por F. Deetjen, cónsul general de Santo Domingo en Nueva York, el buque de guerra dominicano Jacaqua ha salido de Santo Domingo hacia Puerto Plata con entre quinientos y seiscientos de los soldados mejor entrenados del país a bordo.

The Montgomery Advertiser

The Advertiser prints a dispatch: an officer from one of the gunboats

September 17, 191317 de septiembre de 1913

The Advertiser prints a dispatch: an officer from one of the gunboats boarded the steamer Seminole as she was nearing port and told her she could not enter the harbor. The captain of the Seminole replied he would enter anyway. Upon being asked what he would do if he were ordered to remain outside, the captain replied that he would go in and take the boarding officer with him if he did not leave the ship. The officer hurriedly returned to the gunboat and the Seminole entered the port. The blockading boats steamed away. The Seminole had cleared from New York before the blockade of Puerto Plata had been declared and having no munitions of war aboard and relying upon the presence of the Des Moines, she entered the port without fear of molestation. The captain of the Clyde Line steamer Algonquin, on arriving here today, reported everything quiet in the southern ports which are ostensibly in the hands of the government. With the exception of Monte Cristi and Santiago, the northern part of the island is in the hands of rebels under General Horacio Vasquez, at one time president of the republic. The rebel towns of Sanches and Samana are barricaded and the inhabitants are ready for a fight. Monte Cristi is reported quiet, though the people there are much excited. It is reported that the revolution has spread to Seybo, and it is expected that that town and La Romana, both in the southeastern part of the island, will be attacked soon. An officer of the Des Moines came ashore today to take the United States consul out to the ship for a conference with the captain, and was warmly greeted.

September 19, 191319 de septiembre de 1913

The Sun reported that President Wilson adopted a firm policy against Dominican revolutionists in Puerto Plata, viewing their rebellion against President Bordas as driven merely by a quest for government offices and revenue. Backed by the cruiser Des Moines, Minister Sullivan warned insurgent leaders that Washington would refuse to recognize insurgent regimes or disburse customs funds to them. U.S. officials believed that withholding customs revenue served as a decisive weapon to end Caribbean insurrections.

The Sun informó que el presidente Wilson adoptó una enérgica postura frente a los revolucionarios dominicanos en Puerto Plata, considerando que su alzamiento contra el presidente Bordas respondía meramente a un afán de cargos públicos y rentas. Con el respaldo del crucero Des Moines, el ministro Sullivan advirtió a los jefes insurgentes que Washington se negaría a reconocer regímenes rebeldes o a entregarles caudales de las aduanas. Los diplomáticos estadounidenses consideraban que la retención de los ingresos aduaneros constituía un arma decisiva para sofocar las rebeliones en el Caribe.

The New Haven Union

The New Haven Union prints a dispatch from Santo Domingo of September 24

September 24, 191324 de septiembre de 1913

The New Haven Union prints a dispatch from Santo Domingo of September 24: the revolution has been limited to Puerto Plata, Moca and Samana Bay in the northern part of the republic. All other parts of the country are reported to be quiet. The foreign residents are in no danger, conducting their various businesses and travelling without fear of being molested. The blockade, however, continues at Puerto Plata and Samana.

The New Haven Union publica un despacho fechado en Santo Domingo, el 24 de septiembre: la revolución se ha limitado a Puerto Plata, Moca y la bahía de Samaná en el norte de la república. Se informa que todas las demás partes del país están tranquilas. Los residentes extranjeros no corren peligro, atienden sus diversos negocios y viajan sin temor a ser molestados. El bloqueo, sin embargo, continúa en Puerto Plata y Samaná.
