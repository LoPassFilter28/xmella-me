October 16, 185516 de octubre de 1855

Under the headline "Instructions to Mr. Buchanan," the Daily Comet of Baton Rouge reports that the Cabinet in Washington has instructed Mr. Buchanan, the United States Minister to the government of Great Britain, to intimate that the Monroe Doctrine will be strictly enforced by the United States. According to the notice, this enforcement applies particularly with reference to the Dominican Republic on the island of St. Domingo, as well as to Greytown, Cuba, and the Bay Islands.

Bajo el titular «Instrucciones al señor Buchanan», el Daily Comet de Baton Rouge informa que el gabinete en Washington ha instruido al señor Buchanan, ministro de los Estados Unidos ante el gobierno de Gran Bretaña, para que comunique que la doctrina Monroe será aplicada estrictamente por los Estados Unidos. Según la nota, dicha aplicación se refiere de manera particular a la República Dominicana en la isla de Santo Domingo, así como a Greytown, Cuba y las Islas de la Bahía.

The New-York Daily Tribune publishes an official notice from British Consul Robert H. Schomburgk

February 8, 18568 de febrero de 1856

The New-York Daily Tribune publishes an official notice from British Consul Robert H. Schomburgk, dated at the British Consulate in Santo Domingo on December 17, 1855. Schomburgk informs the authorities of the Turks Islands Presidency that the Dominican government has declared the Dominican Republic in a state of siege and closed Samaná and other ports to commercial intercourse. Noting the existing trade between the Turks Islands and Samaná, he requests that this measure be made known to the inhabitants to prevent disappointment and commercial losses, adding that only two Dominican ports, including Santo Domingo, remain open at present to foreign trade.

El New-York Daily Tribune publica una comunicación oficial del cónsul británico Robert H. Schomburgk, fechada en el Consulado Británico en Santo Domingo el 17 de diciembre de 1855. Schomburgk informa a las autoridades de la Presidencia de las Islas Turcas que el gobierno dominicano ha declarado a la República Dominicana en estado de sitio y ha cerrado Samaná y otros puertos al tráfico comercial. Señalando la existencia de comercio entre las Islas Turcas y Samaná, solicita que esta medida se dé a conocer a los habitantes para evitar contratiempos y pérdidas comerciales, y añade que solo dos puertos dominicanos, incluido Santo Domingo, permanecen abiertos actualmente al comercio exterior.

The Daily Exchange

A report brought by the U.S. frigate Colorado details the political crisis

June 29, 185829 de junio de 1858

A report brought by the U.S. frigate Colorado details the political crisis in the Dominican Republic following the warship's arrival at Santo Domingo on May 24. The dispatch states that President Baez held the capital with 250 soldiers through French and Spanish consular intrigues, while General Santana besieged the city with 5,000 to 10,000 native troops, reducing the inhabitants to near starvation. Commodore McIntosh secured assurances for the protection of American citizens and received the official records regarding the case of Charles Hill. The presence of the Colorado reportedly hastened the capitulation of Baez, who agreed to surrender his naval squadron and prepare for departure to Curaçao on June 13.

Un informe traído por la fragata estadounidense Colorado detalla la crisis política en la República Dominicana tras la llegada del buque de guerra a Santo Domingo el 24 de mayo. El despacho afirma que el presidente Báez sostenía la capital con 250 soldados gracias a las intrigas consulares francesas y españolas, mientras el general Santana sitiaba la ciudad con 5,000 a 10,000 soldados nativos, reduciendo a los habitantes a un estado cercano a la inanición. El comodoro McIntosh obtuvo seguridades para la protección de los ciudadanos estadounidenses y recibió los expedientes oficiales sobre el caso de Charles Hill. La presencia del Colorado presuntamente aceleró la capitulación de Báez, quien acordó entregar su escuadra naval y prepararse para partir hacia Curazao el 13 de junio.

The Herald publishes the text of a convention negotiated by the consuls of France

July 16, 185816 de julio de 1858

The Herald publishes the text of a convention negotiated by the consuls of France, England, and Spain between President Buenaventura Baez and General Pedro Santana to terminate the Dominican civil war. Under the agreement, Baez consents to vacate the presidency and leave the country immediately upon signing. General Santana solemnly pledges that no individual will be persecuted or molested for having sustained Baez's government, and promises passports to all officers wishing to depart after the surrender. Santana also undertakes to maintain order in and around the city of Santo Domingo, which is to be delivered to his authority along with its forts, arsenals, and naval squadron.

El Herald publica el texto de una convención negociada por los cónsules de Francia, Inglaterra y España entre el presidente Buenaventura Báez y el general Pedro Santana para poner fin a la guerra civil dominicana. En virtud del acuerdo, Báez acepta abandonar la presidencia y salir del país inmediatamente después de la firma. El general Santana se compromete solemnemente a no perseguir ni molestar a ninguna persona por haber respaldado al gobierno de Báez, y promete pasaportes a todos los oficiales que deseen marcharse tras la capitulación. Santana se compromete asimismo a mantener el orden dentro y fuera de la ciudad de Santo Domingo, la cual será entregada a su autoridad junto con sus fuertes, arsenales y escuadrilla naval.

Writing from St. Domingo on May 17, 1859, the New York Herald's correspondent

June 18, 185918 de junio de 1859

Writing from St. Domingo on May 17, 1859, the New York Herald's correspondent reports under the heading "News from Dominica" that the Dominican government's refusal to redeem its obligations to resident foreigners has compelled the foreign consuls to protest, strike their flags, and request their passports. According to the correspondent, the United States consul is the only one who has not struck his flag, supposedly because his government does not sustain him in protecting the interests of U.S. citizens. Taking issue with other accounts of the Danish question, the correspondent asserts that the recent boarding and robbery of Danish vessels on the open sea—carried out with the republic's Secretary of War as commander-in-chief of the operation—was an outrageous and piratical insult to the Danish flag.

Escribiendo desde Santo Domingo el 17 de mayo de 1859, el corresponsal del New York Herald informa bajo el título «Noticias de Dominica» que la negativa del gobierno dominicano a redimir sus obligaciones con los extranjeros residentes ha obligado a los cónsules extranjeros a protestar, arriar sus banderas y solicitar sus pasaportes. Según el corresponsal, el cónsul de los Estados Unidos es el único que no ha arriado su bandera, supuestamente porque su gobierno no lo respalda en la protección de los intereses de los ciudadanos estadounidenses. Discrepando de otras versiones sobre la cuestión danesa, el corresponsal sostiene que el reciente abordaje y robo de buques daneses en alta mar —llevado a cabo con el Secretario de Guerra de la república como comandante en jefe de la operación— fue un insulto pirático e indignante a la bandera danesa.

An article in the Memphis Daily Appeal reports that foreign consuls

November 19, 185919 de noviembre de 1859

An article in the Memphis Daily Appeal reports that foreign consuls brought produce down the coast under the consular flag and sold it to the starving inhabitants of St. Domingo at exorbitant prices, accumulating millions in spurious paper money issued by Báez. After President Santana entered the city and Báez fled, the consuls demanded that Santana redeem this currency at fifteen times what they had paid for it, and when Santana refused—offering instead to appoint commissioners to ascertain and pay what had actually been allowed—the consuls rejected the offer out of anger over his opposition to their scheme of annexing the Dominican Republic to Haiti, struck their flags, and left the country together. The writer adds that British Consul Hood is reportedly returning to present the alternative of irretrievable financial ruin or immediate acceptance of the Haitian constitution, which encourages colored immigration while excluding whites, and asks whether the twenty-five million white inhabitants of the United States and their government will remain passive and see Samaná lost.

Un artículo en el Memphis Daily Appeal informa que los cónsules extranjeros transportaron productos por la costa bajo bandera consular y los vendieron a los hambrientos habitantes de Santo Domingo a precios exorbitantes, acumulando millones en papel moneda espurio emitido por Báez. Después de que el presidente Santana entró en la ciudad y Báez huyó, los cónsules exigieron que Santana redimiera dicha moneda a quince veces el precio que habían pagado por ella, y cuando Santana se negó —ofreciendo en cambio nombrar comisionados para determinar y pagar lo que realmente se había entregado—, los cónsules rechazaron la oferta molestos por su oposición a su plan de anexar la República Dominicana a Haití, arriaron sus banderas y abandonaron el país juntos. The New-York Daily Tribune, commenting on United States relations

June 4, 18614 de junio de 1861

The New-York Daily Tribune, commenting on United States relations with Spain, remarks that there remains the chance of a collision, or rather of a diplomatic coolness and alienation, resulting from Spain's recent "reannexation" of the Dominican Republic (referred to in the passage as "Dominica"). The editorial characterizes the Spanish move as a rapacious and foolish act, though not nearly so unjustifiable as the United States' own kindred appropriation of Texas. The paper argues that had the United States ever treated the Dominican Republic with common decency or formally recognized its existence, Washington might now with reason interpose an energetic protest against the new Spanish blunder. As matters stand, the Tribune asks how the United States can protest when "Dominica," so far as appears on the surface, has chosen to merge its independent existence into a return to colonial dependence on Spain.

Al comentar las relaciones de los Estados Unidos con España, el New-York Daily Tribune señala que subsiste la posibilidad de un choque, o más bien de un enfriamiento y distanciamiento diplomático, como resultado de la reciente «reanexión» española de la República Dominicana (denominada en el pasaje «Dominica»). Presenting a Spanish account of the annexation of St. Domingo

June 30, 186130 de junio de 1861

Presenting a Spanish account of the annexation of St. Domingo, The New York Herald states that the United States had always been a powerful obstacle to Spain opening her arms to her former colony, as Spain knew that the North Americans wanted St. Domingo for themselves just as they wanted Cuba, and delayed acquiring the former so as not to imperil the latter. Meanwhile, the Dominicans endeavored to counteract North American influence by becoming increasingly Spanish, opposing the continuous drafts of workmen sent by New York filibusters by encouraging the immigration of Spanish colonists and artisans at great sacrifice and appointing to their reduced army all Spanish officers who wished to enter the republic's service. Perceiving this, the North Americans redoubled their efforts, with one of Walker's former lieutenants operating in St. Domingo in a semi-official character to organize and receive the workmen sent from New York and establishing supplementary operations in Samana and Puerto Plata. According to the writer, two events rapidly changed the situation, including the war sustained by Spain in Africa, which gave Spain a consideration in the eyes of the Dominicans and Haitians that it had formerly lacked when they supposed Spain too weak to counteract North American influence.
