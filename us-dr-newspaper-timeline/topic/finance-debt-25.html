New Hampshire Farmer and Weekly Union

Doubtless take steps to protect their interests is they deem it

July 29, 191429 de julio de 1914

The New Hampshire Farmer and Weekly Union contrasted unrest in the Caribbean, noting that while Dominican disturbances were likely to prompt U.S. armed intervention, the country presented fewer military and diplomatic difficulties than neighboring Haiti. European nations maintained smaller financial interests in Santo Domingo, whereas Haitian instability threatened foreign defaults. The Wilson administration sought guarantees that Haitian customs revenues would be managed to satisfy foreign debts in a manner analogous to the Dominican receivership.

The New Hampshire Farmer and Weekly Union contrastó las revueltas en el Caribe, observando que, si bien era probable que las perturbaciones dominicanas provocaran una intervención militar estadounidense, dicho país planteaba menores dificultades bélicas y diplomáticas que el vecino Haití. Las naciones europeas conservaban intereses financieros más reducidos en Santo Domingo, al tiempo que la inestabilidad haitiana ponía en riesgo moratorias con el exterior. La administración Wilson demandaba garantías de que los ingresos de las aduanas de Haití fueran administrados para cancelar deudas foráneas de modo análogo a la receptoría dominicana.

The Bridgeport Evening Farmer

The Bridgeport Evening Farmer prints a dispatch: Secretary Daniels decided

July 29, 191429 de julio de 1914

The Bridgeport Evening Farmer prints a dispatch: Secretary Daniels decided that the additional 400 marines to be sent to Guantanamo, Cuba, for service at Hayti would be embarked from Norfolk on the transport Hancock, now on the way to Hampton Roads from Guantanamo. Whether force is to be used by the American government has been deferred. Reports from Cape Haytien of further fighting, with added danger to foreigners, were regarded as forecasting prompt action by the marines now concentrating at Guantanamo. Not only have the military forces in Hayti refused to heed the warnings of the American government that fighting should cease and property be protected, but dispatches from Captain Eberle of the cruiser Washington, at Puerto Plata, Santo Domingo, also reported the failure of attempts to settle the Dominican revolution by diplomacy. There are now more than 500 marines at Guantanamo or on American warships in Hayti and Dominican waters.

The Bridgeport Evening Farmer publica: El capitán Eberle reporta también el fracaso de los intentos de resolver la revolución dominicana por la diplomacia, desde el crucero Washington en Puerto Plata, Santo Domingo. El secretario Daniels decidió que los 400 infantes de marina adicionales que serán enviados a Guantánamo, Cuba, para el servicio en Haití sean embarcados desde Norfolk en el transporte Hancock, ahora en camino a Hampton Roads desde Guantánamo. Se ha diferido si la fuerza será usada por el gobierno americano. Los informes de Cabo Haitiano de más lucha, con peligro añadido para los extranjeros, fueron vistos como anuncio de una acción pronta por los infantes de marina que ahora se concentran en Guantánamo. No solo las fuerzas militares en Haití se han negado a atender las advertencias del gobierno americano de que la lucha cesara y la propiedad fuera protegida. Hay ahora más de 500 infantes de marina en Guantánamo o en buques de guerra americanos en aguas haitianas y dominicanas.

The Guthrie Daily Leader

The Guthrie Daily Leader prints a dispatch: An appeal for aid

November 6, 19146 de noviembre de 1914

The Guthrie Daily Leader prints a dispatch: An appeal for aid for the tobacco interests of the Dominican Republic was made to the State Department today by Doctor Soler, the Dominican minister. He said there was fear that the entire crop, the principal product of the northern part of the island, might be lost, as it had been sold in advance and was to have gone to Hamburg, Germany. No ships can be found to carry it, as it had always been sent in German vessels. The financial stringency already is being felt, the minister explained, and the tobacco cannot be kept long without spoiling. An effort will be made to secure American or other neutral vessels to handle the crop.

The Guthrie Daily Leader publica un despacho: Una apelación de ayuda para los intereses tabacaleros de la República Dominicana se hizo hoy al Departamento de Estado por el doctor Soler, el ministro dominicano. Dijo que había temor de que se perdiera la cosecha entera, el principal producto de la parte norte de la isla, pues se había vendido por adelantado y debía haber ido a Hamburgo, Alemania. No se encuentran buques que la lleven, pues siempre se había enviado en buques alemanes. La estrechez financiera ya se está sintiendo, explicó el ministro, y el tabaco no puede guardarse mucho tiempo sin echarse a perder. Se hará un esfuerzo por conseguir buques americanos u otros neutrales para manejar la cosecha.

The Montgomery Advertiser

The Montgomery Advertiser prints a dispatch: Juan Isidro Jimenez

December 6, 19146 de diciembre de 1914

The Montgomery Advertiser prints a dispatch: Juan Isidro Jimenez, who was proclaimed president of the Dominican Republic yesterday by the National Congress, took the oath of office today. Quiet prevails throughout the country. Jimenez, former president of San Domingo, was driven by a revolution into exile in Porto Rico. A recent election was held under the supervision of an American commission selected by the State Department. On November 25 Secretary of the Navy Daniels, fearing another revolution, sent back to San Domingo 800 marines who shortly before had been relieved of the duty of preserving order there. Opponents of Jimenez, in control of the Dominican Congress, for a time declined to hold a session, and the new president, who was to have assumed office in November, was therefore unable to appear before that body to take the oath.

The Montgomery Advertiser publica un despacho: Juan Isidro Jimenez, que fue proclamado presidente de la República Dominicana ayer por el Congreso Nacional, prestó hoy el juramento del cargo. Reina la tranquilidad en todo el país. Jimenez, ex presidente de San Domingo, fue expulsado por una revolución al exilio en Porto Rico. Una elección reciente se celebró bajo la supervisión de una comisión americana seleccionada por el Departamento de Estado. El 25 de noviembre, el secretario de Marina Daniels, temiendo otra revolución, envió de vuelta a San Domingo 800 infantes de marina que poco antes habían sido relevados del deber de preservar el orden allí. Los oponentes de Jimenez, en control del Congreso dominicano, por un tiempo se negaron a celebrar sesión, y el nuevo presidente, que debía haber tomado posesión del cargo en noviembre, no pudo por tanto comparecer ante ese cuerpo para prestar el juramento.

The Montgomery Advertiser prints an item: New York, Jan. 27. John G. Gray of Wilmington, Del., who was influential in obtaining the appointment of James M. Sullivan as minister to the Dominican republic, testified today at the investigation into the fitness of the Minister to hold his post. Mr. Gray said he became attorney for the Banco Nacional of Santo Domingo long after Sullivan was appointed. He 'slipped in,' he said, because the Banco Nacional had been placed in an awkward position through inability to secure payment of loans from the Dominican government. He declared he had not gone to Secretary Bryan in the matter to 'obtain a personal favor,' but that it was a matter of legal right. 'I considered it an honorable matter to take up,' he said, adding that advocacy of Sullivan was an entirely separate matter from his subsequently representing the bank. He said 'unfair imputations' had got into the newspapers to the effect that he had originally sought Sullivan's appointment in behalf of the Banco Nacional's interests.

Sullivan como ministro en la República Dominicana, dijo que se convirtió en abogado del Banco Nacional de Santo Domingo mucho después de que Sullivan fue nombrado. «Se coló», dijo, porque el Banco Nacional había sido puesto en una posición incómoda por la incapacidad de asegurar el pago de préstamos del gobierno dominicano. The Sun commented on chronic instability in Haiti and Santo Domingo, asserting that recurring revolutions over twenty years had cost the United States more in naval patrols and interventions than direct administrative control would have required. A special feature in The Washington Herald details Dominican trade

August 28, 191528 de agosto de 1915

A special feature in The Washington Herald details Dominican trade statistics and agricultural prospects for 1914, noting that the United States handled roughly 75 percent of the nation's commerce, with Germany second. The report illustrates the disruptive impact of the European war: while sugar exports reached $4,948,452 and cocoa $3,896,489, tobacco shipments plummeted from $1,121,775 in 1913 to $394,224 following the closing of the German market, alongside exports of coffee, bananas, and timber. The dispatch emphasizes untapped potential in goat and cattle ranching, observing that the island is free from the fever tick and bovine diseases, and quotes a survey by Karl W. Woodward of the American Forestry Service calculating that forested tracts cover 9,500,000 acres of the republic's total land surface.

Un reportaje especial en The Washington Herald detalla las estadísticas comerciales y las perspectivas agrícolas dominicanas para 1914, señalando que los Estados Unidos controlaban cerca del 75 por ciento del comercio nacional, con Alemania en segundo término. El informe ilustra el impacto perturbador de la guerra europea: mientras que las exportaciones de azúcar alcanzaron 4,948,452 dólares y las de cacao 3,896,489 dólares, los envíos de tabaco cayeron de 1,121,775 dólares en 1913 a 394,224 dólares tras el cierre del mercado alemán, junto con exportaciones de café, plátanos y madera. Woodward, del Servicio Forestal estadounidense, que calcula que las zonas boscosas abarcan 9,500,000 acres de la superficie territorial total de la república.
