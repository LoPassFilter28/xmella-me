A Herald survey of the island's intended road and railway lines

August 14, 186914 de agosto de 1869

A Herald survey of the island's intended road and railway lines recalls that, on the first inception of the idea, a correspondent of the paper traversed one of the great lines in question, from St. Domingo westward to the bay of Ocoa, and crossed the entire island from sea to sea to determine where the truth lay between the extremely contradictory reports sent to the Northern press. An English company, the survey reports, has secured a charter for a line of railway from Monte Cristi up through the length of the great tobacco region, by La Vega and Santiago, to the foot of the first spurs of the Abao, the "Gold Mountains" of Indian name — about 150 miles, or half the entire distance to the city of St. Domingo on the south coast; the second 150 miles cross some rugged mountain spurs, but offer no such difficulties as were conquered on more than one Pennsylvania railroad. This first great trunk line of 300 miles crosses the island from the best port on the northwest to St. Domingo, and from Santiago it is to send down lines to Puerto Plata, a short but terrible road of sixty miles, and to Samaná, where the window breaks off.

Un estudio de un corresponsal del Herald sobre las líneas de caminos y ferrocarriles proyectadas en la isla recuerda que, al concebirse la idea por primera vez, un corresponsal del periódico recorrió una de las grandes líneas en cuestión, desde Santo Domingo hacia el oeste hasta la bahía de Ocoa, y cruzó la isla entera de mar a mar para determinar dónde estaba la verdad entre los informes extremadamente contradictorios enviados a la prensa del Norte. Una compañía inglesa, informa el estudio, ha obtenido la concesión de una línea férrea desde Monte Cristi a lo largo de la gran región tabacalera, por La Vega y Santiago, hasta el pie de las primeras estribaciones del Abao, las «Montañas de Oro» de nombre indígena: unas 150 millas, o la mitad de la distancia total hasta la ciudad de Santo Domingo en la costa sur; las segundas 150 millas cruzan algunas ásperas estribaciones montañosas, pero no ofrecen dificultades como las vencidas en más de un ferrocarril de Pensilvania. Esta primera gran línea troncal de 300 millas cruza la isla desde el mejor puerto del noroeste hasta Santo Domingo, y desde Santiago ha de enviar ramales a Puerto Plata, un camino corto pero terrible de sesenta millas, y a Samaná, donde la ventana se interrumpe.

The New York Herald

In a historical essay on the island's two republics, the Herald explains

November 4, 18694 de noviembre de 1869

In a historical essay on the island's two republics, the Herald explains that the republic of Hayti, which comprises the western third of the island, that being the area of the ancient French colony, displaced the name of St. Domingo for the more distinctive and historic appellation of Hayti, by which the natives introduced their island to Columbus. This uniformity of name between the island as a whole and the separate government of the western portion, the essay observes, often leads careless inquirers into confounding the Dominican republic with that of Hayti, though there is a wide difference between them: the Dominicans are Spanish in language, customs and habits of thought, though more democratic in sentiment and more tolerant in religion than the corresponding social levels usually found in the mother country. The Dominican republic, it adds, was the first American state which made the absolute equality of all races before the law a fundamental principle of its constitution, where the window ends. Of Hayti itself the essay recounts that its French section was the scene of the first successful struggle of the black race for political equality with the whites, and that the newly emancipated slaves discarded the colonial name of St. Domingo and restored the one known to the red race.

En un ensayo histórico sobre las dos repúblicas de la isla, el Herald explica que la república de Haití, que comprende el tercio occidental de la isla, siendo esa el área de la antigua colonia francesa, desplazó el nombre de Santo Domingo por el apelativo más distintivo e histórico de Haití, con el que los nativos presentaron su isla a Colón. Esta uniformidad de nombre entre la isla en su conjunto y el gobierno separado de la porción occidental, observa el ensayo, lleva a menudo a los indagadores descuidados a confundir la República Dominicana con la de Haití, aunque hay una amplia diferencia entre ellas: los dominicanos son españoles en lengua, costumbres y hábitos de pensamiento, aunque más democráticos en sentimiento y más tolerantes en religión que los niveles sociales correspondientes que suelen hallarse en la madre patria. La República Dominicana, añade, fue el primer estado americano que hizo de la igualdad absoluta de todas las razas ante la ley un principio fundamental de su constitución, donde termina la ventana. De Haití mismo el ensayo relata que su sección francesa fue el escenario de la primera lucha exitosa de la raza negra por la igualdad política con los blancos, y que los esclavos recién emancipados desecharon el nombre colonial de Santo Domingo y restauraron el conocido por la raza indígena.

Marshall County Republican

A descriptive item on the island in the Marshall County Republican reports

November 18, 186918 de noviembre de 1869

A descriptive item on the island in the Marshall County Republican reports that the imports to the United States for 1868 were valued at 760,000 from Hayti and $33,000 from San Domingo, the principal articles being raw cotton, sugar, coffee, dye woods, gum arabic, hides and cocoa. San Domingo is divided into five provinces; there is an army of 20,000 troops of all arms, and a small navy; the language of the people is Spanish, and the religion Roman Catholic; the government is that of a president, with a Congress consisting of two branches. Hayti and Dominica were several times united, the item recounts, but were divided by revolution, and the country has been for twenty years in a state of chronic revolution, the Dominicans having long since concluded that there can be no peace until they are taken under the flag of the United States; for several years they have proposed to cede the Bay of Samana to the United States, and now offer the whole republic. The other part of the island, the Republic of Hayti, contains 10,000 square miles and a population of 600,000; in considering the question of the acquisition of Dominica, the item concludes, it is necessary to consider also the probable acquisition of Hayti, where the window ends.

Una nota descriptiva sobre la isla en el Marshall County Republican informa de que las importaciones a los Estados Unidos en 1868 se valoraron en 760.000 procedentes de Haití y $33.000 de Santo Domingo, siendo los artículos principales algodón en rama, azúcar, café, palos de tinte, goma arábiga, cueros y cacao. Santo Domingo está dividido en cinco provincias; hay un ejército de 20.000 soldados de todas las armas, y una pequeña marina; la lengua del pueblo es el español, y la religión la católica romana; el gobierno es el de un presidente, con un Congreso compuesto de dos cámaras. Haití y Dominica estuvieron varias veces unidas, relata la nota, pero se dividieron por revolución, y el país ha estado durante veinte años en un estado de revolución crónica, habiendo concluido los dominicanos desde hace tiempo que no puede haber paz hasta ser tomados bajo la bandera de los Estados Unidos; durante varios años han propuesto ceder la bahía de Samaná a los Estados Unidos, y ahora ofrecen la república entera. La otra parte de la isla, la República de Haití, contiene 10.000 millas cuadradas y una población de 600.000; al considerar la cuestión de la adquisición de Dominica, concluye la nota, es necesario considerar también la probable adquisición de Haití, donde termina la ventana.

The New York Herald

In an editorial on the annexation of St. Domingo, the Herald argues

November 18, 186918 de noviembre de 1869

In an editorial on the annexation of St. Domingo, the Herald argues from the letter it published yesterday from its Washington correspondent that the St. Domingo question, both in the hands of the United States government and in the administration of Baez, at the head of affairs in the Dominican republic, is in a fair way towards a speedy and practical solution. The project of annexation, the editorial continues, has long been entertained by the two contending parties in that republic, and the small faction of Cabral revolutionists who oppose it would doubtless favor it, as they favored it when in power a few years ago, if they were now in power. The people generally are reported to be well disposed to the step: President Baez was assured during last summer that the inhabitants of Porto Plata, Santiago, Lavega and Cotuy are agreed on this point. Baez, of course, could not with propriety initiate this annexation scheme any more than he could consent to the leasing of the Bay of Samana or to the plan of a protectorate to be exercised by the United States government, unless that government, as well as his own people, were unquestionably willing and ready to accomplish it; and without entering into the details of the preliminary negotiations which have from time to time been informally conducted between the two independent governments, the editorial expresses the opinion that it is now highly probable — where the window breaks off in mid-sentence, the editorial continuing beyond it.

En un editorial sobre la anexión de Santo Domingo, el Herald argumenta a partir de la carta que publicó ayer de su corresponsal en Washington que la cuestión de Santo Domingo, tanto en manos del gobierno de los Estados Unidos como en la administración de Báez, a la cabeza de los asuntos en la República Dominicana, está en vías de una solución rápida y práctica. El proyecto de anexión, continúa el editorial, ha sido acariciado durante mucho tiempo por los dos partidos contendientes en esa república, y la pequeña facción de revolucionarios de Cabral que se le opone sin duda lo favorecería, como lo favoreció cuando estuvo en el poder hace unos años, si estuviera ahora en el poder. Se informa que el pueblo en general está bien dispuesto hacia la medida: el presidente Báez recibió seguridades el verano pasado de que los habitantes de Puerto Plata, Santiago, La Vega y Cotuy están de acuerdo en este punto. Báez, por supuesto, no podría con propiedad iniciar este esquema de anexión como tampoco podría consentir el arrendamiento de la bahía de Samaná o el plan de un protectorado a ejercer por el gobierno de los Estados Unidos, a menos que ese gobierno, así como su propio pueblo, estuvieran incuestionablemente dispuestos y listos para llevarlo a cabo; y sin entrar en los detalles de las negociaciones preliminares que de cuando en cuando se han conducido informalmente entre los dos gobiernos independientes, el editorial expresa la opinión de que ahora es altamente probable — donde la ventana se interrumpe a mitad de frase, continuando el editorial más allá.

The Evening Telegraph

The Evening Telegraph's second window of its Herald reprint

November 20, 186920 de noviembre de 1869

The Evening Telegraph's second window of its Herald reprint carries the argument that the establishment of civil and political equality has removed certain objections which used to stand in the way of annexation, and there will be no difficulty on this score in governing Dominica and Hayti as territories until they shall have ripened into States. It will not be long before American capital, which has already turned its eye towards the incalculable, undeveloped resources of this beautiful and fertile island, and American enterprise, which has established Dominican banking, railway and steamship companies — in fact, actually laying the egg out of which annexation seems about to be hatched — and, in due time, emigration from the United States, from Europe, and perhaps from China, will redeem the island of St. Domingo from revolutionary chaos and open multiplied markets to its increased productions. Moreover, not the least important consideration involved in the annexation of St. Domingo is the fact that Cuba is within but a few hours' sail of the waters in which a really formidable American fleet is now being gathered; whatever may be the upshot of the Cuban insurrectionary movement, this fact is eminently noteworthy and significant, and the reprint asks how long it will be before all the West India islands shall form the encircling belt of the Gulf of Mexico as an inland lake of the United States — this is merely a question of time.

En una segunda ventana de su reimpresión del editorial del Herald sobre la anexión, el Evening Telegraph lleva el argumento de que el establecimiento de la igualdad civil y política ha eliminado ciertas objeciones que solían interponerse a la anexión, y no habrá dificultad en este punto para gobernar a Dominica y Haití como territorios hasta que hayan madurado como estados. No pasará mucho tiempo antes de que el capital estadounidense, que ya ha vuelto su mirada hacia los incalculables y no explotados recursos de esta hermosa y fértil isla, y la empresa estadounidense, que ha establecido compañías dominicanas de banca, ferrocarriles y vapores — de hecho, poniendo realmente el huevo del que la anexión parece a punto de salir — y, a su debido tiempo, la emigración desde los Estados Unidos, desde Europa y, tal vez, desde China, rediman a la isla de Santo Domingo del caos revolucionario y abran mercados multiplicados a sus aumentadas producciones. Además, no la consideración menos importante envuelta en la anexión de Santo Domingo es el hecho de que Cuba está a solo unas horas de navegación de las aguas en las que ahora se está reuniendo una flota estadounidense realmente formidable; cualquiera que sea el desenlace del movimiento insurreccional cubano, este hecho es eminentemente notable y significativo, y la reimpresión pregunta cuánto pasará antes de que todas las islas de las Indias Occidentales formen el cinturón que rodea el golfo de México como un lago interior de los Estados Unidos: es meramente una cuestión de tiempo.

November 30, 186930 de noviembre de 1869

The Chicago Tribune's descriptive feature continues: Puerto Plata is the chief place of resort for American vessels, for we do the majority of the trade both for Hayti and Dominica. The cattle of Dominica are celebrated; its mahogany and sandal wood are the best in the tropics; many American colored men have settled in this republic, and, but for the civil war, none would ever have quitted it. The bay of Samana, the feature continues, is the outlet of the Yuna River, and is fifty miles long and from fifteen to twenty miles wide; the ocean entrance to it is only a mile wide, and is commanded by an old fort, while high mountains enclose both shores. Twenty-five miles up the bay, upon an inlet to the north, is the town of Samana, the inlet very deep and almost land-locked; the bay has been compared both to the Bay of Naples and to the Chesapeake, to the former for its dazzling tropical and mountain scenery, to the latter for its innumerable wild fowl. This bay has been pronounced the key to the Gulf of Mexico; fine beds of coal surround it, and navigators say that the future route to Darien and Mexico will be by the Mona Passage, just outside of Samana. Samana town, the passage adds, is no town at all, a mere semi-detached peninsula with town vestiges upon it, and the site is swampy; Bonaparte planned a city near Samana, to be called Napoleon, and had the site surveyed. Chicago Tribune

An unnamed speaker tells the Chicago Tribune, in its final window

November 30, 186930 de noviembre de 1869

An unnamed speaker tells the Chicago Tribune, in its final window, that "we have been besieged from the Spanish end of the island these four or five years, to annex Domingo, bodily, and twice or thrice during the same period we have been asked to extend our protectorate over Hayti." Baez has offered to use the whole influence of his party to make the United States proprietors of Dominica, accepting it as a territory of the United States, to be, in course of time, a candidate for admission into the Union as a State, and for no other price than the assumption of its small debt; this offer is open to us at present. With a foothold established there, the Haytian end of the island would be soon anxious for annexation, and Salnave would be only too glad to be the instrument of consigning Hayti to a place among the United States. Asked whether there is any party in either end of St. Domingo violently opposed to annexation, the speaker answers: none; the Jacmel party of Hayti have been, at times, incensed with us for permitting our Consuls and Agents to take sides with Salnave, and we have reason to believe that the enemies of Baez are the enemies of the United States; but everybody in St. Domingo who owns a foot of land wants us to come. The offer, the speaker adds, is not entirely from the natives: there are many Americans, with large interests, in both republics — merchants, planters, and lumber-sawyers — whose property would appreciate immensely if the United States shielded the land, where the window ends.

«Hemos sido asediados desde el extremo español de la isla estos cuatro o cinco años, para anexar a Domingo corporalmente, y dos o tres veces durante el mismo período se nos ha pedido extender nuestro protectorado sobre Haití», dice un orador sin nombre al Chicago Tribune en la ventana de cierre de su reportaje descriptivo. Báez ha ofrecido usar toda la influencia de su partido para hacer a los Estados Unidos propietarios de Dominica, aceptándola como territorio de los Estados Unidos, para ser, con el tiempo, candidata a la admisión en la Unión como estado, y por ningún otro precio que la asunción de su pequeña deuda; esta oferta nos está abierta al presente. Con un punto de apoyo establecido allí, el extremo haitiano de la isla pronto estaría ansioso por la anexión, y Salnave estaría más que contento de ser el instrumento de consignar a Haití a un lugar entre los Estados Unidos. Preguntado si hay algún partido en cualquiera de los extremos de Santo Domingo violentamente opuesto a la anexión, el orador responde: ninguno; el partido de Jacmel, en Haití, ha estado, en ocasiones, indignado con nosotros por permitir que nuestros cónsules y agentes tomen partido con Salnave, y tenemos razones para creer que los enemigos de Báez son los enemigos de los Estados Unidos; pero todo el que en Santo Domingo posee un pie de tierra quiere que vengamos. La oferta, añade el orador, no procede enteramente de los nativos: hay muchos estadounidenses, con grandes intereses, en ambas repúblicas — comerciantes, plantadores y aserradores de madera — cuya propiedad se apreciaría inmensamente si los Estados Unidos protegieran la tierra, donde termina la ventana.

The New York Herald

The closing window of the Herald's long essay on the island

December 22, 186922 de diciembre de 1869

The closing window of the Herald's long essay on the island gives the terms on which, it says, the United States is to assume the possession of the Dominican republic: a Territorial government identical with the other Territorial governments within the jurisdiction of the United States, the Dominican people first deciding by vote to allow such possession, the United States assuming the public debt and other obligations of the Dominican government, amounting to one million dollars, and paying for their State property one million dollars more. The essay recalls that our government sent out commissioners in July last, who returned in October expressing their belief that the Dominicans are desirous of annexation and that the island is worth possessing, and predicts that, 'Dominica once a Territory of the United States, Hayti will soon seek our protection,' Salnave already looking with favor upon annexation and having several times sought the protection of the American flag, while the newly accredited Minister from Hayti, General Alexander Tate, is said to favor annexation — the man who, in 1861, offered his services to Mr. Lincoln to aid in the organization of colored troops. The window ends in mid-sentence, on the peace that possession will bring 'to a people long distracted.'

A description of the Bay of Samaná, whose opening lines are cut

January 5, 18705 de enero de 1870

A description of the Bay of Samaná, whose opening lines are cut in the window, gives the bay as eight miles broad and receiving at its west end the Yuna, the largest river in the Dominican Republic; it forms one of the finest harbors in the world, and may be regarded as a most important maritime position in reference to the trade of the Gulf of Mexico and the inter-oceanic routes across Central America, in both a commercial and a military point of view. On the north shore is the small town of Santa Bárbara, which lies in a land-locked bay and has natural facilities for repairing vessels, a dilapidated Roman Catholic church, a neat Wesleyan chapel and a custom house; the peninsula of Samaná is thirty-two miles long, its highest peak, Sugar Loaf Hill, 1,936 feet, and Devil's Hill 1,309 feet above the level of the sea, its soil extremely fertile and to a great extent covered with timber suited both for ship-building and cabinet-work, and containing gold, copper and bituminous coal, its population at the last census 1,721. The news of the occupation of Samaná by the United States, the description closes, created great excitement among the people of San Domingo, and the revolutionary party were making redoubled efforts to drive out Báez, who had effected the negotiations and declares that he will annex the whole republic to the United States if he is forced to that alternative.

The Tribune's narrative of Báez's career: Pimentel elected President

February 14, 187014 de febrero de 1870

The Tribune's narrative of Báez's career: Pimentel elected President, then forced to give way to Cabral when a pronunciamiento broke out in San Domingo City on the 4th of August, Cabral being commissioned Protector of the Republic; Báez presented himself as a candidate in the following December and, though not a military hero and notwithstanding that his sympathies and support were given to the Spaniards during their invasion of the island, by his tact and consummate management defeated Cabral and became President; Pimentel and Cabral entered his Cabinet, but in 1866 a revolution headed by Pimentel drove him out of San Domingo, and another revolution the same year brought him back into power; Cabral succeeded him, but in the latter part of 1867 the office was again vacated, and after Cabral and his Cabinet fled at night from the capital, Báez was formally proclaimed President in 1868; Cabral remained in the field, with Polanco, Luperón, Moreno, Castillo, Ogando and Adó, opposed to the President with arms or otherwise, and Salnave lent Báez a helping hand in those trying times, without which he could never have maintained his position — the window closing mid-sentence, on the President's notoriety for shrewdness.
