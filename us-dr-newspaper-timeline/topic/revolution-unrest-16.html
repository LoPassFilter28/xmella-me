Evening Times-Republican

The Times-Republican prints a Washington item of March 1: President Morales of the Dominican republic

March 1, 19041 de marzo de 1904

The Times-Republican prints a Washington item of March 1: President Morales of the Dominican republic has been refused permission to blockade his own ports to keep out the revolutionists, as shown by the following report to the Navy Department by Captain James E. Miller, commanding the cruiser Columbia: 'This morning (February 8), the American schooner W. R. Perkins came in from the eastward. The captain had informed me he had been warned off Macoris and not allowed to enter by the gunboat Presidente, and was told that he must proceed to this port. Immediately upon receipt of this information I communicated with the minister, requesting him to communicate with President Morales and inform him I would not recognize his right to blockade Macoris and refuse entrance to vessels flying the United States flag. The minister informs me the president demurred at first, but finally agreed to the fact.' The item appears under the head 'Morales Called Down'.

El Times-Republican publica una nota de Washington del 1 de marzo: al presidente Morales de la República Dominicana se le ha negado permiso para bloquear sus propios puertos para mantener fuera a los revolucionarios, según muestra el siguiente informe al Departamento de Marina del capitán James E. Miller, al mando del crucero Columbia: «Esta mañana (8 de febrero), la goleta estadounidense W. R. Perkins vino del este. El capitán me había informado que había sido advertido de que se alejara de Macorís y no se le había permitido entrar por la cañonera Presidente, y se le había dicho que debía proceder a este puerto. Inmediatamente al recibo de esta información me comuniqué con el ministro, pidiéndole que se comunicara con el presidente Morales y le informara que no reconocería su derecho a bloquear Macorís y negar la entrada a buques que enarbolan la bandera de los Estados Unidos. El ministro me informa que el presidente vaciló al principio, pero finalmente aceptó el hecho». La nota aparece bajo el titular «Morales Called Down».

The News-Herald

The News-Herald prints a magazine excerpt on Santo Domingo

March 3, 19043 de marzo de 1904

The News-Herald prints a magazine excerpt on Santo Domingo, drawn from 'Santo Domingo, A Turbulent Republic', by Charles S. Solomon, in the American Monthly Review of Reviews for March: the absence of cold weather, the excerpt says, makes it possible for the insurrectionists to fight season in and season out, sleep in the open, and subsist, if necessary, on the fruit which grows in great variety and abundance in their country. In the Dominican republic, revolutions take the place of elections: not because the people are too impetuous to permit a president to complete his tenure of four years, and not because the conditions have ever been improved as the result of a victorious campaign by some ultra 'patriotic' leader. Solomon, who says he has watched for ten years with political disinterestedness every step made in the politics of Santo Domingo, puts the reasons for the many uprisings that have taken place there in that period in two words, 'political ambition': some supposed misconduct on the part of the government is made the pretext for fighting, he says, done to mislead the people, who blindly follow the standard of the ambitious politician and the manifesto he spreads over the country setting forth the faults of the president whom he wishes to depose and incidentally succeed in office.

El News-Herald publica un extracto de revista sobre Santo Domingo, tomado de «Santo Domingo, A Turbulent Republic», de Charles S. Solomon, en la American Monthly Review of Reviews de marzo: la ausencia de frío, dice el extracto, hace posible que los insurrectos peleen en toda estación, duerman al aire libre y subsistan, si es necesario, de la fruta que crece en gran variedad y abundancia en su país. En la República Dominicana, las revoluciones ocupan el lugar de las elecciones: no porque el pueblo sea demasiado impetuoso para permitir que un presidente complete su período de cuatro años, ni porque las condiciones hayan mejorado alguna vez como resultado de una campaña victoriosa de algún caudillo ultra «patriótico». Solomon, que dice haber observado durante diez años con desinterés político cada paso dado en la política de Santo Domingo, resume en dos palabras las razones de los muchos levantamientos ocurridos allí en ese período, «ambición política»: alguna supuesta mala conducta del gobierno se convierte en pretexto para pelear, dice, hecho para engañar al pueblo, que sigue ciegamente la bandera del político ambicioso y el manifiesto que difunde por el país exponiendo las faltas del presidente a quien desea deponer y, de paso, suceder en el cargo.

The Republic prints an official letter on a bombardment: the letter, received from San Pedro de Macoris, Santo Domingo, and dated February 29, gives details of the bombardment of San Pedro de Macoris on February 27 by the Dominican warships Presidente and Estrella: the captain of the Presidente, a Spaniard, was killed, and of those on board her only six of the crew escaped without injuries; in all, 74 men were killed and 23 were wounded on the two ships, and the insurgents lost 36 men killed or wounded. Deseret Evening News

The News prints a signed description of Santo Domingo, its harbors

March 12, 190412 de marzo de 1904

The News prints a signed description of Santo Domingo, its harbors, towns and past: Santo Domingo is one of the beautiful islands of the West Indies, lying between Cuba and Porto Rico; in Samana Bay it possesses one of the finest natural harbors in the world, the port President Grant was especially anxious to secure for a coaling station, at present little used. There are a number of small cities in the republic: Santo Domingo the capital, and Puerto Plata the principal port, at about the same number of inhabitants; La Vega about 10,000, Santiago 8,000 and Samana 2,000. The oldest settlement made in the New World was on this island, Columbus himself having founded it, and here the great discoverer was buried for a number of years; it was the capital of the Spanish possessions in the New World. At the end of the eighteenth century Spain ceded the eastern half of the island to France, which held the western half; soon after, the description says, the whole country under Toussaint Louverture gained its independence, and there followed the rebellion of the eastern portion of the island, resulting in the formation of the present Dominican Republic. Outside of the commercial element, there is said to be much opposition to the idea of annexation to the United States.

El News publica una descripción firmada de Santo Domingo, sus puertos, sus poblaciones y su pasado: Santo Domingo es una de las hermosas islas de las Antillas, situada entre Cuba y Puerto Rico; en la bahía de Samaná posee uno de los mejores puertos naturales del mundo, el puerto que el presidente Grant estaba especialmente ansioso por asegurar para una estación carbonera, en la actualidad poco usado. Existen varias poblaciones pequeñas en la república: Santo Domingo, la capital, y Puerto Plata, el puerto principal, con más o menos el mismo número de habitantes; La Vega, unos 10,000; Santiago, 8,000, y Samaná, 2,000. El asentamiento más antiguo del Nuevo Mundo estuvo en esta isla, fundado por el propio Colón, y aquí estuvo enterrado el gran descubridor durante varios años; fue la capital de las posesiones españolas en el Nuevo Mundo. A fines del siglo XVIII, España cedió la mitad oriental de la isla a Francia, que poseía la mitad occidental; poco después, dice la descripción, todo el país bajo Toussaint Louverture obtuvo su independencia, y siguió la rebelión de la porción oriental de la isla, que dio por resultado la formación de la actual República Dominicana. Fuera del elemento comercial, se dice que existe mucha oposición a la idea de la anexión a los Estados Unidos.

The Times prints an item on the Mayflower's return voyage, with Admiral Dewey and Assistant Secretary Loomis aboard: Admiral Taylor and the other members of the general board of the navy which went to Guantanamo returned with them to Washington this morning on the Mayflower; after visiting the scene of the maneuvers the Mayflower went to Jamaica, Santo Domingo and Cuba before returning to the United States.
