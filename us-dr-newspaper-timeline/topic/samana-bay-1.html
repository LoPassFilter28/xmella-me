An editorial in the Daily Union discusses the reported surrender

December 15, 185215 de diciembre de 1852

An editorial in the Daily Union discusses the reported surrender, by the Dominican president, of his country's independence to French domination under the pretext of protection against Haitian Emperor Faustin Soulouque. The paper contends that ceding Samaná and establishing a French naval depot would give France supreme control over the Dominican Republic. While expressing skepticism that France intends to conquer Haiti or the whole island, the editorial warns that Samaná possesses one of the world's finest natural harbors. Stationed there, the paper asserts, a French fleet would command the Mona Passage, threatening American trade routes and coasts.

Un editorial en el Daily Union analiza los informes que indican que el presidente dominicano ha entregado la independencia de su país a la dominación francesa bajo el pretexto de protección frente al emperador haitiano Faustin Soulouque. El diario sostiene que la cesión de Samaná y el establecimiento de un depósito naval francés otorgarían a Francia un control supremo sobre la República Dominicana. Aunque expresa escepticismo de que Francia pretenda conquistar Haití o la isla entera, el editorial advierte que Samaná posee uno de los mejores puertos naturales del mundo. Estacionada allí, afirma el periódico, una flota francesa dominaría el paso de la Mona, amenazando las rutas comerciales y las costas estadounidenses.

Weekly National Intelligencer

The Weekly National Intelligencer cites French press accounts

January 29, 185329 de enero de 1853

The Weekly National Intelligencer cites French press accounts explaining naval movements that sparked American rumors regarding the occupation of Samaná. According to the semi-official paper La Patrie, the frigate La Sibylle left Martinique after an epidemic merely to cruise the Antilles for crew health, and its appearance in Dominican waters generated false reports of military occupation. Meanwhile, the Journal des Débats dismissed reports that France seized the eastern part of the island, though it deemed a French protectorate over the Dominican Republic plausible. The Débats recalled that the republic previously offered Louis Philippe the protectorate before the 1848 revolution intervened.

El Weekly National Intelligencer cita reseñas de la prensa francesa que explican movimientos navales que suscitaron rumores en los Estados Unidos sobre la ocupación de Samaná. Según el diario semioficial La Patrie, la fragata La Sibylle abandonó Martinica tras una epidemia simplemente para realizar un crucero por las Antillas por la salud de su tripulación, y su presencia en aguas dominicanas generó falsos reportes de ocupación militar. En tanto, el Journal des Débats desestimó que Francia se hubiera apoderado de la parte oriental de la isla, si bien consideró verosímil un protectorado francés sobre la República Dominicana. El Débats recordó que la república había ofrecido anteriormente el protectorado a Luis Felipe antes de que sobreviniera la revolución de 1848.

Anti-Slavery Bugle

After denouncing American efforts to perpetuate and extend slavery

October 14, 185414 de octubre de 1854

After denouncing American efforts to perpetuate and extend slavery, the Anti-Slavery Bugle reprints from the Tribune a geographical description of the Bay of Samaná and the surrounding region in the northeastern corner of the island of St. Domingo. According to the account, the bay's entrance on the Atlantic is about twenty-four miles wide, its waters extend fifty miles inland, and two mile-long islands—the Grand Levantados Keys, situated fifteen miles from Cape Raphael on the south and twelve miles from Cape Samaná on the north—break the force of the winds and waves to create a secure anchorage. North of the bay stretches the hilly Samaná peninsula, which varies from four to ten miles in width, while the Port of Samaná, reported to be ceded to the United States, is formed by a cove extending from the bay into the peninsula.

Tras denunciar los esfuerzos estadounidenses por perpetuar y extender la esclavitud, el Anti-Slavery Bugle reproduce del Tribune una descripción geográfica de la bahía de Samaná y la región adyacente en el extremo nororiental de la isla de Santo Domingo. Según la reseña, la entrada de la bahía hacia el Atlántico tiene unas veinticuatro millas de ancho, sus aguas se adentran cincuenta millas tierra adentro y dos islas de aproximadamente una milla de largo —los cayos Levantados, situados a quince millas del cabo Rafael al sur y a doce millas del cabo Samaná al norte— rompen la fuerza de los vientos y las olas para brindar un fondeadero seguro. Al norte de la bahía se extiende la montañosa península de Samaná, cuyo ancho varía entre cuatro y diez millas, mientras que el puerto de Samaná, que según se afirma ha sido cedido a los Estados Unidos, está formado por una ensenada que se adentra desde la bahía hacia la península.

Según el periódico, sus compatriotas provocaron de inmediato una disputa en la rada de Santo Domingo, lo que atrajo rápidamente al lugar a dos corbetas de vapor británicas, una fragata francesa y dos bergantines de guerra. The Herald's account of the Tybee's cruise observes that the country

The Herald's account of the Tybee's cruise observes that the country, drained while under Spanish dominion, misgovernment and oppression, might with fair investment of the means of developing the country and liberal business relations anticipate a vastly increased return; the ruins of ancient palaces and the decayed splendor of her towns in themselves bring vividly to the imagination the days of Spanish opulence in St. Domingo, and it is necessary to go no further than the crumbling ruins of the early Spanish cities to satisfy the most obtuse mind of the hidden resources of the island. In view of the much sought for political relations on the part of Báez's government and the tendency of American enterprise in this direction, the house of Spofford, Tileston & Co. have taken the initiatory step toward realizing the benefits of the large trade destined to spring up between St. Domingo and the United States: the fast propeller Tybee was fitted up for a special cruise of exploration to the ports of St. Domingo city, Samaná bay and Puerto Plata. After a good run of six days and twenty-two hours the steamer reached St. Thomas, where it was proposed to spend about twelve hours in order to reach port by daylight; the extract ends in the description of the approach to the island.

In the Herald's account of the Tybee party's entertainment at St. Domingo

In the Herald's account of the Tybee party's entertainment at St. Domingo, the dinner was an elegant affair and heartily enjoyed by all the party. During the course of the entertainment Mr. Fabens, prefacing his remarks with a few appropriate expressions, proposed the health of the President of the Dominican Republic, which was drank standing. After a few moments President Báez rose and thanked Mr. Fabens and the party for their kind sentiments: there was a spirit of more than friendship existing between St. Domingo and the United States, and he hoped this would increase; he felt honored with the entertainment and proposed the health of the President of the United States, which was also drank standing. President Báez was then compelled to leave the table sooner than courtesy would seem to demand, owing to the severe pain he was suffering in the head from a renewed attack of neuralgia. The letter goes on to complain of the uncertainty of the mails between this city and the United States: letters written in March last via Havana had just reached the city upon the party's arrival, and letters sent in advance to announce the proposed departure of the Tybee had not been received; the Spanish steamers run semi-monthly, alternating on their way to Havana between this city and Puerto Plata, and returning on the same schedule to St. Thomas.
