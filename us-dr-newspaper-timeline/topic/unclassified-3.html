The Saint Paul Globe

The Globe prints a San Juan, Porto Rico, item of December 17

December 18, 190318 de diciembre de 1903

The Globe prints a San Juan, Porto Rico, item of December 17 under the head 'Wos y Gil Says Santo Domingo Doesn't Want to Join United States': Gen. Woss y Gil, former president of Santo Domingo, discussing the resolution introduced in the United States Senate by Mr. Heyburn, of Idaho, requesting the President to negotiate for the annexation of Santo Domingo, said: 'Although Santo Domingo is under the commercial and political influences of the United States, annexation would not be agreeable, because the republic is still feeling the effects of the annexation to Spain, forty years ago, which left a deep impression. Las guerras civiles de la república datan de esa época».

February 6, 19046 de febrero de 1904

The Herald prints a biographical sketch of General Jiminez, whose portrait it publishes: he is to be favored as president of the Dominican republic, but the provisional government that recently ousted Wos y Gil is strongly opposing him; he has twice been president of Santo Domingo, the last time succeeding Heureaux, whom Jiminez's agents are said to have assassinated. The News and Herald

The News and Herald reports heated debate in the United States Senate

January 31, 190631 de enero de 1906

The News and Herald reports heated debate in the United States Senate over Dominican policy, led by Senator Money, who declared that he cared nothing for Santo Domingo and concurred with Senator Lodge that annexation was undesirable. Money warned that absorbing overseas territories and inferior populations would cause the republic to sink of its own weight unless the form of government changed. In response, a senator identified as Mr. Hepburn advocated annexation by arguing the inhabitants would become Americanized and ultimately supplanted by whites, leading Senator Tillman to remark ironically that negotiations could proceed with the Dominican president currently harbored in American territory.

The News and Herald relata un acalorado debate en el Senado de Estados Unidos sobre la política hacia Santo Domingo, encabezado por el senador Money, quien declaró que no le importaba en lo absoluto Santo Domingo y coincidió con el senador Lodge en que la anexión era indeseable. Money advirtió que absorber territorios de ultramar y poblaciones inferiores haría sucumbir a la república por su propio peso a menos que se cambiara la forma de gobierno. En respuesta, un senador identificado como el señor Hepburn defendió la anexión aduciendo que los habitantes se americanizarían y finalmente serían reemplazados por blancos, lo que llevó al senador Tillman a comentar irónicamente que se podría negociar con el presidente dominicano actualmente refugiado en territorio estadounidense.

A Santo Domingo dispatch in The Providence News reports on the overthrow of President Morales and the elevation of former vice president General Ramon Caceres to the executive office. Al reflexionar sobre la situación de la república, el corresponsal atribuye su rezagado desarrollo a la negligencia y el desconocimiento de los recursos naturales por parte de los inversionistas extranjeros, junto con factores locales.

Evening Star

An article in the Evening Star recounts the establishment of the first Christian church

December 3, 19093 de diciembre de 1909

An article in the Evening Star recounts the establishment of the first Christian church in the New World, built on the north coast of Santo Domingo by Christopher Columbus. Returning on his second voyage in 1493 to find Fort Navidad destroyed and its garrison slain, Columbus withdrew to a harbor near Monte Cristi and founded the settlement of Isabella. There he constructed a stone church dedicated to St. Michael, supplied with a bell presented by King Ferdinand, along with a stone royal storehouse and governor's palace. After the discovery of gold in the interior prompted Isabella's abandonment, surviving cut stone from the ruins was collected by the warship Enterprise and brought to the United States.

Un artículo en el Evening Star relata la fundación de la primera iglesia cristiana en el Nuevo Mundo, levantada en la costa norte de Santo Domingo por Cristóbal Colón. Al regresar en su segundo viaje en 1493 y hallar el fuerte de La Navidad destruido y su guarnición aniquilada, Colón se retiró hacia un fondeadero cerca de Monte Cristi y fundó el poblado de La Isabela. Allí erigió una iglesia de piedra consagrada a San Miguel, provista de una campana obsequiada por el rey Fernando, además de un almacén real y un palacio de gobierno también de piedra. Tras el hallazgo de oro en el interior que provocó el abandono de La Isabela, los sillares labrados que subsistían en las ruinas fueron recogidos por el buque de guerra Enterprise y llevados a los Estados Unidos.

The Washington Herald reports an eccentric filibustering scheme

March 27, 191027 de marzo de 1910

The Washington Herald reports an eccentric filibustering scheme uncovered in New York, where an arms order was placed by five young American college men—three from Princeton and two from Yale. Their stated objective is to overthrow President Ramon Caceres and transfer the capital of 'Dominica' from Santo Domingo to San Pedro de Macoris in hopes of securing army commands and cabinet posts. The Washington Herald

Upon returning from a three-week trip to Santo Domingo and Haiti

July 18, 191318 de julio de 1913

Upon returning from a three-week trip to Santo Domingo and Haiti, Assistant Secretary of State John E. Osborne announced that the bones of Christopher Columbus repose in the Cathedral of Santo Domingo rather than in Seville, Spain, The Washington Herald reports. According to Osborne, Dominicans secretly retained Columbus's remains out of love for him, sending only the bones of his son Diego back to Spain, and he identified a lead casket inscribed "Christobal Colon, first admiral and discoverer of America" containing a bullet among the bones. In response, Pan-American Union Director General John Barrett noted that several locations claim the honor and that experts hold widely divergent opinions on the subject.

Al regresar de un viaje de tres semanas por Santo Domingo y Haití, el subsecretario de Estado John E. Osborne anunció que los restos de Cristóbal Colón reposan en la catedral de Santo Domingo y no en Sevilla, España, según informa The Washington Herald. Según Osborne, los dominicanos retuvieron secretamente los restos de Colón por el afecto que le profesaban, devolviendo a España únicamente los huesos de su hijo Diego, e identificó un cofre de plomo con la inscripción "Christobal Colon, primer almirante y descubridor de América" que contenía una bala entre los restos. En respuesta, el director general de la Unión Panamericana, John Barrett, señaló que varios lugares se disputan ese honor y que los expertos mantienen posturas divergentes sobre el asunto.
