Officials Baffled by Fate Of Missing Trujillo Critic

NEW YORK, Mar. 31 (AP).—The disappearance of a Columbia University assistant professor almost three weeks ago appeared no nearer a solution today than it did then.

No clue or trace has been reported in the search by the FBI and police for Spanish-born Jesus de Galindez, 42, a caustic critic of the Trujillo regime of the Dominican Republic.

Has he been kidnaped—or worse—by his political foes, as his friends contend?

Is he in hiding in fear of political reprisal?

Has he simply gone into seclusion to finish an anti-Trujillo book he was writing, unaware of the furor his absence has caused?

Or is there some other explanation?

A Spanish Republican, Mr. De Galindez went into exile from Spain in 1937 and spent the next nine years in the Dominican Republic. He came to the United States in 1946.

The mystery was touched off when Mr. de Galindez failed to appear at Columbia University to conduct his classes in international law on March 12. Since then—nothing.

His apartment at 30 Fifth avenue showed no sign of struggle or foul play.

But Dr. German Ornes, recently deposed publisher of the Dominican newspaper, El Caribe, told newsmen here he believed Mr. de Galindez was kidnaped and murdered. He said he had known de Galindez for 15 years.

Worried at his absence, members of 11 organizations in Latin-American affairs and human rights appealed on March 20 to Attorney General Brownell to send FBI men into the case.

Norman Thomas, former Socialist candidate for President, told newsmen the appeal referred to the Dominican Republic.

Mr. Thomas said de Galindez was writing a book, "The Era of Trujillo," about Gen. Rafael L. Trujillo, the Dominican chief.

"He told me he had been threatened," Mr. Thomas said. "He was very fearful."

Dominican Government sources in New York have denied "implications" that the Trujillo Government had anything to do with the disappearance.

In another telegram to Mr. Brownell, the Inter-American Press Association asked for a Justice Department investigation of the disappearance "regardless of its international implications."

The Inter-American Press Association is composed of 400 newspapers and magazines throughout the Western Hemisphere and is headed by James G. Stahlman, publisher of the Nashville (Tenn.) Banner.
