Journalists Here for Annual Parley

While feverish haste today marked completion of preparations for the formal opening of the first Pan-American Journalistic Congress at the Pan-American Union on Wednesday evening, the first of the large group of delegates that will attend from all of the South and Central American republics began to arrive in Washington. All of those now in the city are registered at the Willard, Mayflower and Shoreham Hotels.

Officers of the Pan-American Union have been formally advised that many of the Latin American delegates have already disembarked in New York City, but that they are attending personal affairs in the metropolis before proceeding to Washington. A few, however are still at sea and it is not expected that the representation of Latin America will be complete before the opening day of the congress.

Noted Delegates Attending.

Among those now in Washington is Jorge Mitre, editor of La Nacion of Buenos Aires, one of the large newspapers published in the Western Hemisphere. Others, all of whom are registered at the Willard Hotel, include Francisco Escovar, Colombia; Rene Fialo, Santiago, Cuba; Julio Herrera Gonzales, San Pedro de Macoris, Dominican Republic; Fredic[illegible] Daveriss, Peru; Luis Nunez, Venezuela; Rosada Vega, Merida, Mexico; A. Villegas, Panama, and Juan Francisco Rojas, Guayaquil, Ecuador.

Few events in all the history of the Pan-American Union have aroused such enthusiasm in the officers of the association of the nations of the Western Hemisphere than the journalistic congress. The meeting of the editors and directors of the largest newspapers of this side of the earth is hailed both in and out of the union as one of the most promising steps toward continuous peace for this hemisphere in all history.

Friendly Exchange of Views.

It will be a gathering for a friendly exchange of views between the men who, as has been pointed out, who can do more to instill a desire for permanent peace in the minds of the people of all the countries involved than any other assembly in the world. The Pan-American Union has summoned congresses on trade, on science, on government and on many other subjects, but none have encouraged more enthusiasm than that which will now be convened for the journalists of North, South and Central America.

A program that will occupy every minute of the delegates' time, from Wednesday evening until the following Tuesday, has been arranged. During that period the visitors will have discussed the task of the newspapers in molding world thought, and they will have been shown everything of popular interest in the National Capital. Immediately after the congress adjourns however, the foreign delegates will become the guests of the Federal Government for a tour of America's great industrial centers.

Probably nothing the visitors see in Washington will prove of greater interest than an exhibit that has been specially arranged for them in the Library of Congress. It is the story of the evolution of journalism in the Western Hemisphere, told in graphic fashion by some of the oldest volumes existent, and most of it will prove especially interesting to the delegates from the Latin American republics.

Prominent in the display is a copy of a magazine published in Buenos Aires more than a century ago—El Argos de Buenos Aires of 1825. Beside it are copies of La Clave of Santiago de Chile, 1827, and a publication bearing the burdensome title of "The General Magazine and Historical Chronicle for All British Plantations in America," dated 1741. In the case section are editions of the American Magazine, 1741; Gazeto de Guatemala, and Diario de Mexico, 1805.

Copy of Early Laws on View.

In another section is a copy of the earliest compilation of the laws of Portugal, dated 1528, and a treatise concerning the laws and government of the New World under Spain, dated 1609. Dr. Vasco de Puga's earliest printed summary of Spanish colonial law of 1563 and laws covering the treatment of the Indians, as prescribed by Charles V, dated 1543, are other volumes in the collection. A manuscript by Secretary of State Madison, detailing some conversations with the famous leader, Gen. Miranda, dated 1805, is another interesting exhibit.

The United States Army Band, which will furnish music at all of the score of fetes that have been arranged for the delegates to the congress, has been rehearsing for months the selections it will render at these functions. The entire musical program represents the pick of Latin American music and there are some scores in all, representing probably the most complete collection of Latin American music on this continent.

Practically every delegate will hear at some time or another the air most dear to his country. Many of the selections had to be taken from manuscripts, which were borrowed and then photographed for distribution among the musicians of the Army Band. It is said that there will be 20 different national anthems alone to be played, not to mention the scores of other musical numbers [illegible] a phase of life in the country concerned.
