The Evening Star reviewed American newspaper editorials defending the U.S. military occupation of Haiti against domestic critics. Excerpts from the Charlotte Observer, Savannah Press, and Wall Street Journal credited American administrators under Commissioner Russell with establishing stability, building schools and roads, and ending a cycle of violent revolutions. These commentaries maintained that the Haitian population benefited from American oversight and desired its continuation.El Evening Star reseñó editoriales de periódicos estadounidenses que defendían la ocupación militar estadounidense de Haití frente a las críticas internas. Extractos del Charlotte Observer, del Savannah Press y de The Wall Street Journal atribuían a los administradores estadounidenses dirigidos por el comisionado Russell el establecimiento de la estabilidad, la construcción de escuelas y caminos, y el cese de una serie de revoluciones violentas. Evening Star · March 30, 192730 de marzo de 1927

Original in English.Original en inglés.

Borno's Ban on Senator King Subject of Heated Comment

In barring Senator King of Utah from Haiti, President Borno of the island republic stirred up a lively debate among American newspapers over the status of the Haitian government and the part played by the United States in its recent history. Many editors feel that Borno would have dared exclude a Senator of the United States only if he were sure of pleasing the State Department.

"Everybody knows Borno is just as much a hired man of official Washington as Diaz is in Nicaragua," states the St. Louis Post-Dispatch (independent). "Everybody knowing that the exclusion of King, who has fought vigorously in the interests of Haiti against the aims of dollar diplomacy, was done by Borno to curry favor with his American bosses." The Rochester Times-Union (independent) remarks: "It seems that, despite the presence of our Marines, the little republic is still technically a 'sovereign state,' and can refuse entrance even to a United States Senator if it so wills. Yet if Haiti were really independent it is extremely unlikely that it would exclude a member of the legislative body which has the final say regarding treaties made by the United States." "If Secretary Kellogg had desired Senator King to visit Haiti, Senator King would have visited Haiti," declares the Lynchburg News (Democratic), while the Schenectady Gazette (independent Democratic) directs attention to the point that "nothing can prevent King and Borah expressing opinions on the Senate floor, but it is a simple matter to stifle their search for the truth through other means." The Durham Sun (independent) adds that "the whole truth of the matter is that the Senator was right"; that "the government of Haiti is as completely Haitian as the present Federal government of Nicaragua is completely Nicaraguan."

* * * *

"Secretary Kellogg is strictly correct," according to the Louisville Courier-Journal (Democratic), "in saying that when the Haitian President rules out Senator King the Haitian President is wholly within his sovereign rights. Theoretically, that is true. But actually the Haitian President has no sovereign rights not permitted him by the Washington Government. If Secretary Kellogg had been willing that Senator King should go to Haiti, a quiet little hint from the State Department to President Borno would have overcome that functionary's opposition to Senator King's explorations."

"Probably the American people have reason to be thankful," in the opinion of the Kalamazoo Gazette (independent), "that the barring of Senator King was not effected until after the adjournment of Congress. Had it taken place during the session, the resultant furor in the upper house and demands for a thorough investigation of our relations with Haiti might have prevented the august lawmakers from accomplishing even as little as they did in the way of Government business." The Providence Bulletin (independent) feels that "Senator King has shown better judgment and tact by changing his plans than Haiti did when it announced a determination to keep the Senator out of the country."

In praise of American influence in Haiti, the Birmingham News (independent Democratic) says: "If Senator King had wanted the truth, he could get it. The record is right there in Washington for him. The intervention of this Government, at the invitation of the Haitian government and that of the Dominican Republic, is one of the greatest pieces of constructive work we have to our credit. The facts are there. They have been accessible to King. But he has refused to see the good and has harped upon all he thought was bad." The Chicago Daily Tribune (independent Republican) adds that the Marines "were sent into Haiti to stop government by assassination. Assassination is probably too pale a word," continues the Tribune. "It was by butchery. The slaying of political prisoners had made Haiti politics even more sanguinary than usual for Caribbean campaigning."

* * * *

"The United States," it is pointed out by the Charlotte Observer (Democratic), "has constructed good roads for them and built schoolhouses, established a fine system of policing, and has surrounded the natives with advantages which they do not want to see taken away. * * * It is a land of primitive negroes—as primitive as ever they were in Africa—but they know a good thing when they see it and would be willing to fight to stay under Uncle Sam's roof." The Savannah Press (Democratic) holds that "Haiti is now a paradise compared with its former condition, and it will be a blessing if the President of the island remains still under the influence of Commissioner Russell."

Quoting an earlier editorial, the Wall Street Journal (financial) states: "Uncle Sam, that notorious Shylock, land-grabber and tyrant, took over the management of that island 11 years ago. In those days it would have been easier to find, because its principal harbor and capital, Port au Prince, could be located by its odor far out to sea. It was about the most loathsome pest-hole in the Central American republics. Presidents were elected for a term of seven years. Six of them, prior to our intervention, had functioned in less than four years. One was blown up in his palace, one died by poison, one was killed by the mob, and the three others were deposed by revolutionists, which was as near to self-determination as that people could get, and corresponded to a presidential election." The Wall Street paper tells of improvements in sanitary conditions, agriculture and security of life and property. Commenting on this, the Ogden Standard-Examiner (independent) suggests that "undoubtedly Senator King does not accept all that is charged against self-government in Haiti. However, it may be just as well that the Senator turned back and did not insist on landing at Port au Prince."

The Bellingham Herald (independent) draws the general conclusion, "If the Borahs and Kings and other leaders of minority blocs would cease encouraging the people of other lands to believe American policies are unpopular at home, the Nation would have a higher standing abroad."
