FROM SANTO DOMINGO Electrical Engineer Balch Tells What He Knows. LEFT PORTO PLATA AUG. 21 The Assassin of President Heureaux is on Deck. HE RECEIVES THE SURRENDER OF SEVERAL GOVERNORS. The Inhabitants Are Not Revolutionary—They Want a President With Money and Business Ideas—The Country is in Good Condition—Prosperity Everywhere—Americans as Safe as in New York—Jimenes Not Quite the Man.

New York, Aug. 31.—J. H. Balch, an electrical engineer in the employ of the Santo Domingo improvement company and who has been in the Dominican republic for several years, has just arrived in New York, having left Porto Plata August 21st.

"On Wednesday, August 16," said Mr. Balch, "the governor of San Francisco de Macoris, a town sixty miles from Santiago in the interior, surrendered the town to the insurgent chief, General Ramon Caceres, the assassin who killed President Heureaux, and who has not been captured since the murder. Caceres had but 300 men with him. The governor of San Francisco de Macoris had a garrison of only fifty infantrymen armed with Remington rifles. The governor offered no opposition; consequently there was no fighting and no bloodshed. Upon the surrender General Caceres at once telegraphed to the governors of other important cities in San Domingo who were appointed by the president, that he, Caceres, was then in supreme command at San Francisco de Macoris, adding with a fine sarcasm that he held himself at their command. This message was a subtle defiance for them to come and take him. His invitation was not accepted by any of them. He then marched toward Porto Plata, of which General Emilio Cordero was governor and I see the papers state Governor Cordero surrendered Porto Plata. I think it is quite likely that all the larger cities of San Domingo and finally the capital itself, San Domingo City, will surrender. I say this because for a state of dissatisfaction existing in such cities as Santiago and the seaports of San Domingo.

"The population of the republic is about half a million. Whenever a town surrenders the insurgents take the defeated government troops with them.

"An American is as safe in San Domingo as he is in New York. The chief cause of dissatisfaction is the currency question. A man who has prestige and who can obtain loans is the man the natives are looking for as president. Jimenes is hardly the man. He is well known in New York, Hamburg, London and Paris, where his banking houses have suspended.

"The chief products of San Domingo are tobacco, chocolate, fruits, honey, beeswax and hides. The best mahogany in existence grows there. Mahogany is the scarcest and most valuable timber known to the lumber trade. The country is in a fine sanitary condition, the interior furnishing excellent springs for drinking water.

"Notwithstanding the present insurrection commerce is not affected. San Domingo was never so prosperous as at the present time. The present generation does not inherit the revolution-army propensities of the last and for past generations. The country people do not want to fight, either for the government or against it and not infrequently in order to avoid military service they take to the bush."
