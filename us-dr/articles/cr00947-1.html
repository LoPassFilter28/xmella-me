Washington Sentinel · August 5, 18995 de agosto de 1899

Original in English.Original en inglés.

Our Imperial Prospects in Santo Domingo.

The imperialist press is almost unanimously of the opinion that the United States is under obligations to "preserve order" in the Dominican Republic. Many newspapers which cordially approved the expansion policy of the Administration are in favor of more radical steps. We have annexed Porto Rico and bought the Philippines. Why not, they contend, take possession of Santo Domingo? It is near enough to Porto Rico to give some color to the contention that a condition of anarchy in the Dominican Republic might have an injurious effect upon our Porto Rican possessions. Moreover, Santo Domingo, it is alleged, is practically under the control of an American syndicate with headquarters in New York. This syndicate directs its finances and is on good terms with the Dominican Government. If there is revolution in Santo Domingo and some of the syndicate's property is destroyed, the United States will be expected to hold the revolutionists in check and "protect American interests." Two warships have been ordered to Santo Domingo and more will be sent if necessary, according to Washington advices. Moreover, it is said to be the policy of the Administration not to permit interference by European nations which may have subjects to protect. The "Monroe Doctrine" makes it necessary for us, it is alleged, to warn Europe to keep its hands off. We are the "paramount power" in this part of the world, and if the revolutionists of the Dominican Republic do not behave themselves the United States alone has the right to restrain them. It is true the Monroe Doctrine sustained a very severe shock when the United States became a "world power" and established itself in the "Far East," but the imperialists are blind to such considerations. They maintain that it is our mission to grab whatever is desirable in any part of the world, but not to recognize the right of Europe to adopt the same principle on this side of the world.

With a reasonable pretext for intervention in Santo Domingo, it is not unlikely that the annexation scheme of President Grant's Administration will be revived. Of course we should not gain anything by annexation except an addition to our population of many thousands of mixed breeds—negro, Indian and Spaniard. All depends, however, upon the syndicate which has been financing the island. If these gentlemen can exploit Santo Domingo to advantage as an American possession, they may get their imperialist friends in and out of Congress to boom an annexation project. Syndicates have been very well cared for by this Administration and there is no reason why we should not take in Santo Domingo, if the seizure of the Philippines can be justified. All territorial syndicates ought to look alike to Mr. McKinley and "manifest destiny" ought not to make any discrimination between its friends. With 10,000,000 Asiatics in the Philippines, nearly a million Spaniards and blacks in Porto Rico and the mixed Indian, negro, French and Spanish population of Santo Domingo, the United States will soon be able to go into the "show business" in competition with the other colonial powers.
