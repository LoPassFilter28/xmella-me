Betancourt Death Plot Tied to Dominicans

A five-nation investigation committee, in a play-by-play report on the June 24 attempt to assassinate Venezuela's President Romulo Betancourt, says its evidence ties the plot directly to high officials of the Dominican Republic.

The report, made by representatives of Panama, Argentina, the United States, Mexico and Uruguay for the Organization of American States, is to be made public officially Monday night, just before the hemisphere foreign ministers meet in Costa Rica August 16.

A copy of the document obtained from independent sources says the facts gathered by the commission are that the plot began May 8, 1960, when Juan Manuel Sanoja, a Venezuelan who had lived 14 years in the Dominican Republic, went by plane to the Dominican Republic, via Haiti, and began trips and consultations which preceded the explosion of a dynamite-laden automobile near one occupied by Mr. Betancourt.

It was not Mr. Sanoja, however, the commission said, who pressed the button transmitting a signal "causing the instantaneous explosion." That man, the committee said, was a Venezuelan, Luis Cabrera Sifontes.

In its formal conclusions, announced in paraphrase form earlier this week, the commission said the attempt was an effort to "overthrow the government" of Venezuela; that "those implicated in the attempt and in the plot mentioned received moral support and material aid from high functionaries of the Dominican Republic," and it outlined specifically the materials supplied.

Diplomatic sources said it is highly probable the recent events in the Dominican Republic where President Hector Trujillo resigned and others of the Trujillo family severed connections with the home government, were related somehow to the committee's report.

There was no agreement, however, as to whether the Dominican government changes were merely an attempt to cloud the picture when the foreign ministers discuss the Dominican case in Costa Rica, or whether Mr. Trujillo and his relatives actually were going into a form of exile abroad, all with government jobs.

Frequent Contacts

The committee said it had evidence that some of the plotters were welcomed to the Dominican Republic by high army officials, that a few of them saw Generalissimo Rafael Trujillo, long-time strong man of the country, and that there were frequent contacts between the plotters and Col. John Abbes Garcia, often called the chief of the Dominican Secret Service and one of the Generalissimo's right hand men.

The committee said that Gen. Trujillo, to whom it referred as "the chief of the armed forces," offered the plotters "such aid as they might need to overthrow the Venezuelan regime and suggested that they proceed with rapidity and decision."
