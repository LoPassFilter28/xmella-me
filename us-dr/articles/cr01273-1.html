Original in English.Original en inglés.

SAMANA.

"This remarkable peninsula and bay is," says Mr. St. Remy, "one of those maritime positions not often met with in a survey of the map of the world. It is to the Gulf of Mexico what Mayotta is to the Indian Ocean. It is not only the military, but the commercial key of the Gulf." He elsewhere calls it the tete du pont, the "head of the bridge," of the highway from the Atlantic to the Caribbean Sea and the Isthmus of Darien, called the Mona Passage, between St. Domingo on the west, and Porto Rico on the east.

The Bay of Samaná is an indentation, thirty miles long and about eight miles wide, in the eastern end of the Island Hispaniola or St. Domingo. When Columbus first opened this bay, in 1493, on his return from his first voyage, he thought it was a strait separating the southern part of the Island along which he had been coasting from some other land. Samaná is in the peninsula at the north of this bay. The isthmus connecting it with the main is so low that at some time, within written history, there has been really a channel through it, practicable for boats. This channel called the Gran Estero, is now choked up with sand. Mountain chains rise suddenly from this low isthmus; and the peninsula extends from west to east about thirty miles. Its greatest width is eleven miles; the swampy land at the isthmus, its narrowest part, is five miles wide. Sir R. Schomburgh estimates the area of the whole peninsula at 225 square miles.

It has been said, not yet on good authority, however, that this whole peninsula has been transferred to our Government. We conceive it to be by no means improbable that the Republic of Dominica if asked to cede a spot for a Navy Yard should be willing to surrender the whole of it, as its population is small, and its whole extent is inconsiderable.

The Bay of Samaná is protected from the seas which would enter with north and east winds, by a formidable reef, which makes of it almost a landlocked basin. This reef extends from Cape Rafael, the south-eastern promontory of the mouth of the bay, W. N. W. to Pascual Key, which is but a mile from the main land of Samaná. The passage for vessels into this bay is wholly through the mile-wide strait which thus separates Pascual Key from Point Cacao on the main. This channel is further impeded by a shoal with only twenty four feet on it, lying only six cables' length north-west from the key—an obstruction which makes it difficult for vessels to leave with the sea breeze, and renders it advisable for vessels to stand out with the land-breeze. This narrowness of the channel gives to the immense harbor within a singular defense against enemies. The Point Cacao, which makes the northern side of the narrow channel of entrance, is a projecting, bold bluff, where a battery would entirely command the whole passage to the little sea within. Jack Bannister, the somewhat-celebrated pirate of the 16th century, availed himself of this position to drive off two English frigates which were in pursuit of him and his consort Lageroe.

Within, the bay affords good anchorage almost everywhere, and of course affords shelter and protection to the largest fleets. The French fleet sent out by Napoleon, to carry out his outrage on Toussaint, anchored here, and Le Clerc's army landed here. This fleet consisted of 60 vessels, of which 36 were ships of the line. The peculiar advantage which the bay here offers, however, as a naval station, consists of its veins of coal. Near the rivulet Almaçen are large veins of bituminous coal, which have also been met with farther east. The Almaçen flows into the bay near its west end. It is said that the coal, which has been tried, has proved "too bituminous" for the use of steamers. But the experiments have not been followed up with any energy, and the veins have not in any place been wrought to the depth of ten feet. The geological structure of the country is such that there may be coal of a better quality below. Iron, copper, and small quantities of gold, are also found on this little peninsula.

Now that our Government is in treaty for a part or the whole of this territory, it is a fact somewhat curious that a sixth part of the population is already furnished from our own country. The total population in 1851 was 1,721, of whom a considerable part were negroes from this country, who emigrated in 1824, when Boyer was President of Hayti, and this part of the island formed part of his dominions. These people, with their descendants, now number about 300—and it is a gratifying fact that they distinguish themselves by the cleanliness of their provision grounds, and their industry. They form the principal part of the congregation of the Missionary of the London Wesleyan Missionary Society, who resides at Puerto Plata, but occasionally visits this place, where is a Wesleyan chapel.

The population has been varied since the discovery of the Island. Columbus found on this peninsula a warlike tribe, and thought it to be thickly settled. The first blood shed in America in fight between the Indians and whites, was in a skirmish between his men and these Indians. Columbus gave the bay the name of the Bay of Arrows, in consequence. The peninsula was afterward made a station by French Buccaneers, whose settlement there was in a flourishing state, with the single want of women. D'Ogeron, the French Governor of Tortuga, supplied this want by sending to them a party, who had been sent to him from St. Malo, and the settlement thrived till near the end of the 17th century, when an attack from the Spaniards almost annihilated it, and in 1700, the straggling remnant was withdrawn by the French Government. Till 1756 the peninsula was neglected, but in that year the Spanish Government planted a colony from the Canaries there. Afterward some of the refugees from French St. Domingo took refuge there. The peninsula has been in the last century under the various dominion of Spain France, the Haytien Republic and Empire, the insurgent republic of "Spanish Hayti" of 1821, and the present Dominican Republic, which has held, since 1844, the eastern half of this beautiful island.

[Boston Daily Advertiser.
