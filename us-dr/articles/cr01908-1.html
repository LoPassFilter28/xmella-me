WIN, LOSE OR DRAW

By FRANCIS STANN

Of This and That

MOST NEGATIVE FEAT of the spring was performed by Bobby Avila of the Cleveland Indians, 1954 American League batting champion. Reporting to camp as the Tribe's No. 1 second baseman, Bobby lost out to Rookie Billy Moran. Shifted to third base, the Mexican veteran played so badly he defaulted to Billy Harrell, another rookie. . . . Joe Louis, who appeared at the trials and shook hands with his "old friend," Jimmy Hoffa, before the Teamster boss was acquitted of bribery and conspiracy charges, doesn't lend his support to every strong man. The ex-heavyweight champion says that he turned down a handsome fee to help Dictator Trujillo spread good will among his subjects in the Dominican Republic.

Former Jockey Johnny Bejshak's son, Johnny, who is one of the foremost amateur skaters in the United States, may be drafted any day now, making his goal of winning the next Olympic figure skating crown a bit more difficult. . . . Few, if any, horses in recent years ever received the buildup given the intriguingly named Silky Sullivan on the West Coast, and it was a Washington native who started the ball rolling. Fred A. Purner, who spent his boyhood in the Northwest section, has been publicity director at Santa Anita since that palatial track opened in 1934. It was Purner who seized on the colt's dramatic finishes—and the fact that one of his owners has heart trouble—to make Silky a household name.

Washington's national Golden Gloves featherweight champion, Norman Smith, will be managed by Irving Cohen when he turns pro. Cohen, who piloted Rocky Graziano, among others, also handled Smith's brother, Gene. . . . Dave Sisler's control has returned just as mysteriously as it disappeared last summer, which is one reason why the Red Sox think they'll do better.

* * * *

AL LOPEZ SOUNDS PLEASED with Billy Goodman at third base for his White Sox, but Billy's ex-teammates on the Red Sox apparently don't think he'll throw out all runners on bunts and slow-hit balls. When he bunted in batting practice the other day, Jim Piersall sang out, "Base hit!" Frank Malzone disputed on grounds that it was bunted too hard. Goodman's playing third, Piersall quipped. . . . Native Dancer, the original TV turf favorite, has a first crop of 15 sons and daughters ready for racing this spring, but only two are gray like their daddy.

Johnny Blanchard, a catcher, won the third annual Jim Dawson Memorial Award as rookie of the Yankees' camp, but not without a rerun. Blanchard and Pitcher Zack Monroe tied with four votes each, with Marv Throneberry receiving two. On the runoff, both of Throneberry's votes were cast for Blanchard.

Ismael Valenzuela, the jockey who rode Promised Land in the Campbell Handicap at Bowie and upset the sure-thing bettors who backed Iron Liege, is an old hand at giant-killing. He rode Mister Gus to victory over Nashua, beat Swaps on Porterhouse and was on Nashville when the Llangollen colt handed Bold Ruler his first defeat as a two-year-old. Ismael is the younger brother of Angel Valenzuela, who is famous for his succinct comment after riding Prince John to victory in the world's richest race—the Garden State—in 1956. Asked how his mount behaved, etc., Angel replied, "No excuses."

* * * *

IRELAND'S FORTHCOMING "An Tostal Golden Golf Ball Contest" (May 11-26) offers $1 million for a hole-in-one, but don't plan on spending it too soon. The tournament is played over a one-hole course that measures more than five miles from tee to green across the rolling plains of the famous Curragh of Kildare. Contestants are limited to three golf clubs, a maximum of 30 lost balls, and the "fairway" includes such hazards as tank traps, rifle pits, a grenade range, plantations, highways, a cemetery and a railway.

The exact dimensions of "that screen" in Los Angeles Memorial Coliseum, new home of the Dodgers, have arrived. It stands 42 feet high at the point where the left-field foul line meets the outfield wall—250 feet from home plate. It runs along the outfield wall at about the same height for 150 feet, then dips down for the next 30 feet to 8 feet. In deep center it joins the 6-foot outfield fence that runs across to the rightfield foul pole.

* * * *

GENE FULLMER, EX-MIDDLEWEIGHT champ who threatened to file a $1 million suit against the International Boxing Club, must be easy to please. Instead of suing, Fullmer settled for a $4,000 TV fee to fight Franz Szuzina of Louisville on May 3, the eve of the Kentucky Derby. The bout probably will be held in comparative privacy.

Red Schoendienst, who played most of last season on the same ball club, calls Teammate Hank Aaron of the Braves "the best right-handed hitter I've seen during my playing days." . . . Sunny Jim Fitzsimmons, talking of his three-year-old, Misty Flight, says, "My stopwatch says he can run, but the trouble is it doesn't tell me if he will run. He's a moody kind, but capable of beating any of this year's three-year-olds if he decides to try."

Too bad about Ed Furgol, the good golfer despite a withered left arm. The evidence is that Ed's bum wing is collapsing on him after nine holes or so. After going out in a sensational 33 and coming back in 42 during one round of the Masters, Furgol muttered, "You can't score without arms." . . . If Pitcher Ryne Duren of the Yankees adds any more eyeglasses to his collection he'll have to cut down on his wardrobe while on the road. The myopic righthander carries five pairs of glasses, three for baseball and two for street and reading wear.
