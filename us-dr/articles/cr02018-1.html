Guatemala Presses Plot Charges in Face Of U. S., Other Denials

By the Associated Press

GUATEMALA, Jan. 30.—President Jacobo Arbenz Guzman pressed charges today that neighboring Nicaragua and three other Latin American countries were linked in a United States-approved plot to invade Guatemala and overthrow his leftist regime.

El Salvador, the Dominican Republic and Venezuela were named in a presidential office communique as joining with Nicaragua in a plot to invade Guatemala with heavily-armed infantry units, supported by planes equipped with jellied gasoline bombs.

A United States State Department spokesman called the charges “ridiculous and untrue.” He said they constituted proof of an international Communist conspiracy to break up hemisphere solidarity prior to the 10th inter-American conference in Caracas, Venezuela, in March. There have been frequent charges that Guatemala is a center of Communist conspiracy in the Western Hemisphere.

Trujillo Applies Red Brand.

A statement from the Foreign Office of the Dominican Republic said the charges were “the invention of those who profess the ideas of atheistic communism.” It added that the Dominican Republic had no connections with Guatemalans, “either as friends or enemies of the Communist government of Arbenz.”

The presidential office communique claimed that letters had been intercepted from fugitive plotters to President Anastasio Somoza of Nicaragua.

The communique said the letter revealed that “the plans have the acquiescence of the government of the north (United States), that Somoza has supported the activities, and that the president of Nicaragua himself has undertaken to be the standard bearer for this humanitarian enterprise (the invasion of Guatemala) in which were also implicated the governments of El Salvador, Santo Domingo, and Venezuela.”

Alleged Statement Quoted.

The statement quoted what it called a paragraph of the letter as follows: “I have been informed by our friends here that the government of the north, recognizing the impossibility of finding any other solution to the grave problems of my country, has taken the decision to permit us to develop our plans.”

The communique said the name of Francis Cardinal Spellman of New York appeared in the letters, but it did not elaborate in any way on the reference.

Two men were named as ringleaders in the plot—Gen. Miguel Ydigoras Fuentes, a candidate for president against Arbenz in 1950, and Col. Carlos Castillo Armas. Both men are exiles. Gen. Ydigoras, commenting in El Salvador on the charges, said they were “absurd” and a “stupid invention by the regime in power.”

President's Son Scoffs.

The communique asserted the ringleaders were in communication with President Somoza and the latter's son, Col. Anastasio Somoza Debayle. The president's son, in Kansas City, Mo., on a business trip, added his voice to those labeling the charges false.

“Those people are just seeing monkeys in the air,” the son of Nicaragua's president told a reporter. “Any statement about arms must come from the foreign service of my government. But when they say I am engaged in a plot against them that is a lie. I know nothing about such an affair.”

The communique accused Gen. Ydigoras and Col. Castillo Armas of making a pact at San Salvador on March 31, 1952, to lead a revolution in Guatemala. Col. Castillo Armas was to be the military head of the revolt and Gen. Ydigoras was to emerge later as a presidential candidate, the communique added.

Ruben Villatoro, head of the National Union of Free Workers, and Horacio Cordova, an anti-Communist news and radio commentator, were among five fugitives reported to have crossed the border into Mexico when the arrests began. Police denied they had arrested Mr. Cordova, but it was learned later that he was taken to the border and ordered out of the country.
