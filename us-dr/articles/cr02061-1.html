RADICALISM SPREADING OVER LATIN REPUBLICS

Virtually All of Central and South American Nations Have Had Recent Red Manifestations.

BY GASTON NERVAL.

PROBABLY of very few countries in the Western Hemisphere could be said what Gov. Roosevelt said of the United States and its behavior through the present world-wide economic crisis.

“The great social phenomenon of this depression,” stated the Governor in his acceptance speech of the Democratic presidential nomination, “unlike others before it, is that it has produced but a few of the disorderly manifestations that too often attend upon such times. Radicalism has made few converts * * *.”

The other American republics have been less fortunate. Either because their traditional social structure is a semi-feudalism in which there is the material for a clean-cut class struggle, or because their inhabitants are biologically more inclined to change, revolution and disorder, the Latin nations on the other side of the Rio Grande have witnessed an impressive growth of radical tendencies in the last few years.

Communists of Russian type may not be numerous in Latin America, but the number of radicals and discontented laborers—faced with unemployment or pitifully low wages—who are willing to follow Communist leaders and agitators has increased notably as a result of the economic distress and the political upheavals of the past two years.

Chile Indicates Trend.

The recent disturbances in Chile and the establishment there of the first Socialist republic in the New World are only an indication of the trend of political thought in the southern continent. But they do not tell the whole story. The real significance of the Chilean upheaval will be appreciated only when it begins to reflect in other Latin American countries.

When the news of the Socialist revolt in Chile reached Washington I did not hesitate to say that the major danger lay in the propagation of similar conditions in the neighboring countries, and thence to the rest of the Latin American continent. It was easy to see that the success of the Chilean movement would have a great influence in encouraging the activities of radicals throughout Latin America. Leaders of the Left who had been playing up what they called the failure of capitalism, as shown by the world depression, and promising the setting up of a new and happier order of things, were bound to profit by the psychological influence, if nothing else, of the successful Chilean outbreak upon the masses of other Latin American republics.

Since then, cable dispatches from the Latin capitals have confirmed that fear and given an impression of continental-wide unrest and radical agitation south of the Rio Grande.

In Argentina, Socialists and Communists have long had a part in internal politics. The former constitute one of the largest and most influential sectors of public opinion, but it is from the latter that any threat of disorder comes. In fact, this menace has become so grave recently, with the recurrence of strikes and labor disturbances, that the more moderate Socialist elements have been forced to abandon their opposition to the conservative rule of President Justo and pledge themselves to support him in the event of any major Communist uprising. In the capital and in other regions of the country there is the added danger of a well organized group of anarchists, largely immigrants, who have transplanted to Argentina the methods of European nihilists.

In Other Countries.

In Brazil, although the governmental elements still have a firm control of the situation, unrest has been spreading rapidly. Following a conference of administration officials and military leaders from various parts of the country, the Government announced a few weeks ago it would take “the most energetic measures to preserve internal peace and principally to combat attempts to corrupt the Brazilian family by preaching discontent.” The Third October Club, a political organization, had just adopted a resolution urging the government to incorporate some of the principles advocated by the Socialist Republic of Chile in its original program.

In Bolivia, Peru and Ecuador, heirs to the glories of the ancient Inca Empire, Communist propagandists have been making considerable strides in their efforts to convince the native Indian classes that they deserve a better treatment from their white and mestizo brothers, who have monopolized the government since the Spanish conquistadores first landed in America. The aboriginal Indians still form a large part of the population of Bolivia, Peru and Ecuador, and the slowly and subtle, but tenacious, way in which red agitators are gaining them to their cause with the promise of a re-distribution of land, is causing great alarm to the authorities of those countries.

In Peru the problem has taken more definite form. There is an organized Communist group. And there is another supposedly non-Communist entity, the Apra party, which, while denouncing the Moscow tactics and denying any connection with Soviet plans, preaches a philosophy and follows a program almost equally radical. The Apristas made an impressive showing in the last presidential elections and since then have seen their prestige increased by the difficulties the Sanchez Cerro government has encountered in weathering the economic crisis. Last May a radical uprising started in Callao, the chief Peruvian port, the paternity of which was attributed alternately to Communists and Apristas. Geographical proximity and the co-operation long existing between Chilean and Peruvian radical leaders strengthen now the likelihood of new disturbances.

Colombia has been one of the very few Latin American countries to escape the wave of revolutionary fever which has swept that continent in recent months. That, however, does not mean that the Colombian authorities do not have a Communist problem to deal with. Press dispatches have reported raids of Communists on small towns in the interior of the country, where they attempted to establish their own form of government. Although these endeavors did not meet with much success, they have been sufficient to prove the growth of radical theories among the native population.

North of Panama, Communist elements have lately caused trouble for several of the Central American governments. With the exception of Nicaragua, where the presence of United States Marines may have had some influence in discouraging them, they have appeared at one time or another in all the other States of Central America. And even in Nicaragua, if the unpopular resistance of President Moncada to the candidacy of the liberal leader, Dr. Sacasa, finally results in a new civil war, as it is feared, the Communists across the border may find a splendid opportunity to step into Nicaragua and profit by the convulsion.

It is in El Salvador that Communists agitators have so far proven more successful in Central America. Under the regime of President Araujo they were not seriously opposed, for they, themselves, had lent support to Araujo's election. However, when President Araujo was forced to flee the country by a military coup d'etat and Vice President Martinez became head of the government, Salvadorian Communists intensified their subversive activities until they finally succeeded in starting an uprising of considerable proportions. We all know how much blood and material losses that uprising, only a few months ago, meant. British and American warships stood by, while it lasted, ready to take off foreigners in danger. Only the energy and promptness with which the Salvadorian authorities proceeded, and the overwhelming support they received from all social and political sectors to put down the Communists revolt, prevented its propagation to the rest of the country. The peril, however, has not disappeared entirely, and Salvadorians claim that it will last, and grow worse, so long as the State Department refuses to extend recognition to the constitutional government of President Martinez.

Communists in Guatemala have been the object of an intensive round-up by the authorities. Sixty-five of the agitators were arrested within a fortnight, including the Salvadorian imbroglio. And from time to time, the cable brings reports of new red plots discovered by the Guatemalan police.

Outbreak in Costa Rica.

Until recently Costa Rica was thought to be immune to the advances of radical propagandists. The very liberal and democratic institutions of the little but progressive republic were considered a barrier to the dissemination of doctrines of discontent and violent change. Two weeks ago, however, the first Communist riot took place in San Jose, the capital, after a meeting of the “General Union of Workmen and Communists.” Fifty-eight manifestants were arrested after their leader had solemnly announced, “This episode opens the Communists' fight in Costa Rica, and we will win or die.”

In the latest Honduran uprising and even in the various Cuban riots intended to overthrow President Machado, Communists' hands have been found at work. It is only natural that radical leaders encourage and take advantage of any internal political conflict, to make gains during the storm.

In Mexico, where the first seeds of Latin American radicalism were sown, a revival of the Communist spirit has lately made itself felt in certain regions of the country. Here, however, the problem is rather different, as it is the authorities of some Mexican states and not the people who are leading the trend to the left. The States of Hidalgo and Vera Cruz recently passed almost confiscatory laws against large foreign industrial interests, “in the benefit of the community.” Though the action of the federal government at Mexico City prevented a conflict with foreign interests, the popularity of such radical measures seems to be increasing in the neighboring republic along with the progress of the economic crisis.

In contrast with these dark reports from all over the continent, a dispatch from Santo Domingo, printed a few days ago in the metropolitan papers, announces that the position of President Trujillo's government is every day stronger in the Dominican Republic and that domestic unrest has disappeared in the island. The dispatch adds that governmental finances in the Dominican Republic are sound, and that, when most of the Latin American nations have been forced to suspend payments on their debts, that country has until now faithfully kept its foreign obligations and, stranger still, maintained a balanced budget.

Which goes to show, as I have many times said in these columns, that internal political unrest and governmental finances are closely interrelated in Latin America.
