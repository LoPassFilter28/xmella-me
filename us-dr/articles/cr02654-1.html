FCC to Test Radio Bug; Power Is Violation Key

Federal Communications Commission officials today said an investigation is under way to determine whether the "electronic bug" secreted in a Mayflower Hotel room was being operated unlawfully.

FCC technicians said the miniature transmitter, found Monday taped beneath a coffee table in a San Francisco attorney's suite, was given a "limited test" yesterday.

John H. MacAllister, an agency attorney, said the maximum penalty for unlicensed radio transmissions is one year in jail and/or a $10,000 fine.

The FCC entered the case yesterday less than 24 hours after Malcolm T. Dungan, a West Coast attorney, obtained an order from District Court Judge George L. Hart, jr., halting the use of the electronic device.

The 40-year-old attorney, in Washington representing West Coast natural gas interests, on Monday discovered the listening device hidden in his $37.50-a-day Mayflower suite—Rooms 633-634.

Mr. Dungan said the miniature transmitter had been used for several days to broadcast confidential conversations to a receiver found in Room 639, a few doors from his suite.

3 Get Summons

The restraining order named five persons, only two of them identified in the original complaint. The two were listed as John J. Frank, a Virginia resident "engaged in the business of providing clients with private investigations," and D. P. Baxter of New York, who was registered as the occupant of Room 639.

The United States marshal's office today, however, said summonses have been served on three persons. Marshal James P. McShane identified them as Mr. Frank; John W. Angelone, a licensed private investigator with offices at 1830 Jefferson place N.W., and John W. Leon, owner of the Allied Investigation Equipment Co., located in the Dupont Circle Building.

The marshal said the three have been ordered to give depositions at 10 a.m. Monday in the office of Paul R. Conolly, jr., Mr. Dungan's Washington attorney. Mr. Dungan, it was learned, returned to San Francisco last night.

Precise Tests Due

An FCC engineer said the commission hopes to subject the Mayflower "bug" to precise laboratory tests to determine its exact power output. Commission aides indicated that the power and range of the electronic eavesdropper, which is about the size of a soap dish, will have a bearing on whether an alleged violation exists.

Frank M. Kratokvil, assistant chief of the FCC's Bureau of Field Engineering, said the tiny transmitter was broadcasting at about 120 megacycles in a band set aside for air navigation. The set, he said, emitted a "hybrid mixture" of FM and AM waves.

Mr. Frank was identified as the Washington attorney and former FBI agent who was fined $500 in 1959 on a charge of failing to register as an agent for the Dominican Republic and the late Dictator Generalissimo Rafael Trujillo.

Mr. Leon, 38, appeared before Judge Hart yesterday in ing order and summons. He was represented by Attorney Edward L. Carey, as are the two other men on whom summonses were served.

District police and licensing officers have been examining the need for broader authority to license private detectives here.

No Individual Licenses

Under present District law, licenses are required only of the private detective agencies but not of the individual private detective employes a firm may hire.

"We want to be able to check out the past records and good character of anyone who will be working in the District as a private detective," said Inspector John L. Sullivan, head of the District's Special Investigations Squad.

"They should be licensed just as we license used automobile salesmen. This way we could keep track of all those who are acting as private detectives," Inspector Sullivan said.

His views were echoed by C. T. Nottingham, head of the District's Licenses and Permit Division, who also believes the District should formulate regulations covering private detective operations.

"Studies have been made for several years on possible regulations of the private detective field," Mr. Nottingham said. "They could be required by the District Commissioners to post bonds, carry identification cards and otherwise be more closely governed in their operations."

Without regulations, Mr. Nottingham said, there is no way of controlling wiretap activities or eavesdropping devices here when used by private detectives should such control be desirable.

There are no laws here forbidding use of the electronic devices although the Federal Communications Act bans unauthorized interception and divulgence of any communications.

Officials say there are about 50 licensed private detective agencies in the District and about 17 are under investigation for new licenses or renewals. One renewal request now being checked out is that of Mr. Angelone, an official said.
