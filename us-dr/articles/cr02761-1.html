Lobbyists Accused of Giving Distorted View of America

Key members of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee contend lobbyists for foreign governments are giving their clients a distorted—and highly uncomplimentary—view of how American legislators pass laws.

Committee Chairman Fulbright and Iowa Senator Hickenlooper, ranking Republican on the committee, both want the Foreign Agents' Registration Act brought up to date and made more enforceable to curb the activities of non-diplomatic representatives of foreign principals.

Their backing for a revised law followed release of testimony taken in closed session from Washington attorneys working for Dominican Republic sugar interests during the Trujillo regime.

One of the closed hearings centered on Michael B. Deane, a Washington attorney and public relations counsel, who admitted he had no experience in sugar, but described himself as a "pretty knowledgeable fellow around Washington." Mr. Deane was a District delegate to the 1960 Democratic National Committee.

Senator Fulbright said Mr. Deane's testimony illustrated "how a foreign agent used his slight contacts in both Congress and the Executive Branch to promote his own personal ends."

"Powerful Law Firm"

Two sets of closed hearings involved Walter S. Surrey and Monroe Karasik of the firm of Surrey, Karasik, Gould and Greene.

They were questioned about a 1956 memorandum signed by Mr. Karasik to his Dominican principal stating that a "powerful law firm" close to an unnamed Senator would try to win his support for sugar legislation helpful to the Dominican Republic. The fee for this service, according to the memorandum, was to be $2,500 plus a contingency.

Both Mr. Surrey and Mr. Karasik insisted during their first appearance before the committee that they did not know or could not recall the identity either of the Senator or of the law firm.

At a second closed session two months later, however, Samuel Efron, a former partner in the firm, identified the Senator as Harry F. Byrd, Democrat of Virginia, and the law firm as Bendheim, Fagelson, Bragg & Giammittorio of Alexandria, Va.

Mr. Fagelson testified that he was approached by Mr. Efron to use his good offices on Senator Byrd for a fee but decided to reject the proposition. Chairman Fulbright and several other members assured Mr. Fagelson after he testified that he had "acted honorably, as a lawyer should act, in this whole transaction."

To Dominican Secretary

The memorandum which Mr. Karasik admitted signing was addressed to J. M. Troncoso, then Dominican Secretary of State Without Portfolio. The memorandum began by saying that contact had been made with a "powerful law firm in the Senator's home State."

"The senior member of the firm is the executive officer of the Senator's political machine," the memorandum continued. "The second partner is the son of the Senator's first campaign manager; there are very close family connections between this man and the Senator. The third partner is the private confidential attorney of the Senator; he handles important confidential matters for the Senator's machine."

Senators Fulbright and Hickenlooper, in a joint statement when the closed testimony was made public last night, said they were concerned about the memorandum because of "the implication it carries to foreign governments and interests as to the character and ethics surrounding lobbying in this United States Congress and the manner in which members themselves arrive at legislative decisions."

Fee Agreement Not Filed

The committee's other major concern with the activities of Mr. Karasik and Mr. Surrey involved what the committee considered two contracts. One was a general retainer agreement for a $95,000 fee to file with the Justice Department. The other was a contingent fee sugar lobbying agreement, which was not filed.

Both Mr. Surrey and Mr. Karasik testified that the contingent fee agreement was not a binding formal agreement but was simply "an agreement to agree at a later date" Senator Fulbright, however, took the view that the contingent fee agreement was not filed with Justice and made a public record because it spelled out lobbying operations which might prove embarrassing if made public.

During the closed hearings, the Senators also explored the code names used by Mr. Surrey and Mr. Karasik to communicate. Senator Byrd was referred to as "petrol boy" because he had once served as chairman of the Virginia Petroleum Commission. "Kerr-type" information was used as a synonym for "scurrilous."

Assailed by Hickenlooper

Both attorneys drew the frequent wrath of committee members during their two closed session appearances. Senator Hickenlooper, for one, told Mr. Karasik that "my disgust is complete and thorough, and my contempt is utter in this case."

After the closed hearings were made public last night, Mr. Surrey and Mr. Karasik issued a statement categorically denying that they had engaged in any impropriety while representing the Dominican Sugar Commission. They said they had complied with all requirements of the law and that an examination of the testimony "will completely dispel any inference that we engaged in any unethical or improper practice whatsoever.

In the Deane case, the other set of hearings made public last night, Senator Fulbright said the committee's interest in the case primarily was due to the fact "that this public relations man apparently filed exaggerated and sometimes inaccurate reports to his principal at a time when relations between the United States and the Dominican Republic were at a critical point.

The committee chairman added that the damage such reporting can do to normal diplomatic relations is obvious and poses a problem the committee may try to solve through amending the Foreign Agents Registration Act.

Mr. Deane, who reported he was paid $57,549 in 1961 for his services in behalf of the Dominican Republic's sugar allocations, acknowledged during testimony that he might have exaggerated his ability to get things done.

Senator Fulbright put it more bluntly. He referred to Mr. Deane's "puffing and bragging" and said that Mr. Deane's reports to his foreign principals "created an erroneous and certainly not complimentary picture of how our Government operates."
