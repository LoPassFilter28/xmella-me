POLITICAL OUTLOOK.

But a few weeks since the prospect for the Republican Party was anything but assuring.

Charles Sumner was engaged in denouncing with all the power of his marvellous eloquence the policy of the President, and not very indirectly the President himself. Following this at the organization of the Forty-Second Congress, Senator Cameron was appointed Chairman of the Committee on Foreign Affairs, a position that had been held by Sumner for years. The event gave rise to much bad feeling, and how far it would cripple if it did not destroy the party could hardly be foreseen. The result of the elections in New Hampshire gave additional confirmation to all the sinister forebodings that had been freely expressed.

It is almost incredible that in so short a time such a change should occur in general sentiment. The members of the San Domingo Commission made their report, confirming every favorable judgment that had been expressed by the President, and closing as follows:—

"In reviewing the whole field of their investigations, looking to the interests of both divisions of the Island, they are firmly persuaded that the annexation of Santo Domingo to the United States would be hardly less beneficial to the Haytien than to the Dominican people. This benefit would arise—first, from the example which would thus be afforded of a well regulated, orderly, and prosperous State, the great need of that part of the world, and which it has never yet seen. A second and more direct benefit would arise from the equitable establishment of a boundary line between the French speaking and Spanish speaking nations upon that island, and its guaranty by a strong power. This would end the exhausting border warfare, which has been one of the greatest curses of Hayti as well as of Santo Domingo, and would enable both to devote their energies thenceforward to the education of their people and the development of their resources. Respectfully submitted,

B. F. WADE,

ANDREW D. WHITE,

S. G. HOWE.

To the President of the United States."

President Grant on April 5th, presented the Report to Congress, accompanied with a Message, of which we give the main points in the following:—

"And now my task is finished, and with it ends all personal solicitude upon the subject. My duty being done, yours begins; and I gladly hand over the whole matter to the judgment of the American people and of their Representatives in Congress assembled. The facts will now be spread before the country, and a decision rendered by that tribunal, whose convictions so seldom err, and against whose will I have no policy to enforce. My opinion remains unchanged; indeed it is confirmed by the report, that the interests of our country and of Santo Domingo, alike invite the annexation of the Republic. In view of the difference of opinion upon this subject, I suggest that no action be taken at the present session beyond the printing and general dissemination of the report. Before the next session of Congress the people will have considered the subject and formed an intelligent opinion concerning it, to which opinion, deliberately made up, it will be the duty of every department of the Government to give heed, and no one will more cheerfully conform to it than myself. It is not only the theory of our Constitution that the will of the people, constitutionally expressed, is the supreme law, but I have ever believed that all men are wiser than any one man; and if the people, upon a full presentation of the facts, shall decide that the annexation of the Republic is not desirable, every department of the Government ought to acquiesce in that decision."

Just at this time the result of the Connecticut election was announced, and instead of a sweeping Democratic victory according to the programme of reaction, it proved in almost every respect a Republican gain. Since that a better feeling has manifested itself in Congress.

It was intimated that Senator Sumner would through personal pique, oppose the bill giving power to the President for the suppression of disorder in the Southern States. But on the contrary he made one of his most able speeches in behalf of the measure. On Friday morning last immediately after the meeting of the Senate, Mr. Howe of Wisconsin, who had been very severe in reply to Mr. Sumner's San Domingo speech, took occasion to make an explanation looking to more peaceful relations. He stated that he had not only combated the Senator's position, but had indulged in some personal reflections upon his course of action. The explicit declaration made by the Senator from Massachusetts had convinced him that his purpose was not what he supposed it to be, and he sought an opportunity to make this declaration. Mr. Sumner received the explanation kindly, and while asserting the strength and honesty of his convictions, announced himself as a member of the Republican Party, "faithful to it always."

It would appear that all political expectations born of this party feud may be considered as indefinitely postponed.
