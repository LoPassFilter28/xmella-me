MAY. On May 11 the miners accepted a two-week truce. The King of Italy had already abdicated in favor of his son Humbert, later to be voted out of office by the Italian plebiscite. On May 14 an American Military Court sentenced 58 operators of the Mathausen Concentration Camp to be hanged. In Alcatraz prison we witnessed the two-day insurrection with the inmates fortifying themselves in the main building.

The railroad strike began on May 24. In the Far East the Chinese recaptured Changchung.

Then JUNE made its entry. On the second day of the month the McMahon Bill for national, civilian control of atomic energy was passed. In Europe Antonescu and some of his associates were executed on the same day.

June 5 saw the disastrous Hotel La Salle fire in Chicago, followed on the 10th day by the second great hotel fire in Dubuque, Iawo, when the Canfield hotel burned down.

June 7th Secretary of the Treasury Fred M. Vinson was appointed Chief Justice of the United States Supreme Court.

The month of June also witnessed the disappearance of the famous Hesse Crown jewels in Germany from Kronberg Castle near Frankfurt. At home the maritime strike was settled by the middle of the month.

Disorders occurred on a large scale in Palestine as Jewish extremists blew up five bridges, virtually isolating Transjordan from Palestine. And here in East Hartford we remember the Willimantic incident when on June 19 a Constellation plane made an emergency landing in Connecticut without injury to any of its 42 occupants.

June 24 saw the Pacific Coast earthquake with damage reported from Oregon to Canada. A star of the silent films passed away with the death of William S. Hart, famous for his portrayals in Western films of earlier movie days.

The month ended with the great atomic tests at the Bikini atoll in the Pacific. Thus we moved on into

JULY. The first day of the month brought injury to five American soldiers in Trieste when a hand grenade was thrown during a demonstration. Mass violence occurred on July 2 in the movie studio strike in Hollywood. Great religious riots took place in India with scores of dead and injured.

July 4th saw the Stars and Stripes lowered in Manila with the end of U. S. control. President Truman signed the Hobbs anti-racketeering bill.

The National Education Association at its meeting in Buffalo, New York, reported the loss of a million teachers in the ranks of the profession, due to poor wages and working conditions.

Major Alexander De Seversky predicted future missiles, making a trip to the moon in three and a half hours at speeds of 139,000 miles per hour. (That's better than 23 miles a second, folks, or about four seconds from East Hartford to New York. Want to climb aboard and take a ride?) Another oddity of the month came when Jinjiro Mizuno, member of the Japanese House of Peers, declared that all Jap males were guilty of waging war and the only solution would be an all-women government for Japan.

The Arab High Commission wrote to President Truman, suggesting to open America to the Jews indiscriminately. Milk, petroleum and other products went off price control.

The pope read a message to the Japanese Catholics, the first of its kind in the history of the Church.

The second atomic test (submarine) at the Bikini atoll came off on July 25. On July 29, four days later, the first Peace Conference opened in Paris. And time moved on into

AUGUST. August 2nd was the 39th birthday of the United States Army Air Forces. On August 4th Protestant leaders from eight nations, gathered in England, favored joint action with the Catholics on world problems.

Athens, Tennessee, saw ex-GIs drive the sheriff elect out of town and seize control of the government.

The Dominican Republic suffered great loss in a tidal wave and violent earthquake. The steamship American Farmer was deserted at sea and became a problem for international litigation.

Channing Pollock, famous playwright, died August 18.

The Yugoslavs shoot down two American planes. The State Department sent violent protest. Severe rioting occurred in Calcutta. The Norwalk, Connecticut, schools teachers go on strike for wage relief.

President Truman vacationed in Bermuda. New England scientists from M. I. T. studied cosmic rays at 40,000 feet altitude. Fifteen German spies were arrested in the American zone of occupation. Those were some of the August headlines and then

SEPTEMBER. Price control went on a spree on such items as canned fruits and vegetables and many other items. The truck strike was hitting New York. An Army jet plane did 578 miles per hour at the Cleveland races.

Marshal Tito apologized to the United States for the shooting down of our planes. A musicians' strike silenced the dining rooms of the New York hotels.

The Queen Mary arrived on September 4th with art treasures, valued at seven million dollars, for exhibition in the U. S. A. A shipping strike tied up all ports with 3,000 ships moored at their docks and 400,000 workers idle.

September 6. A two-year-old child is the only survivor of a transcontinental plane crash in Navada, with all 21 passengers killed. The Nazi top war criminals are sentenced to hang.

OCTOBER. The high spot of the month of October to most people possibly was the execution of the Nazi war criminals. When Master Sergeant John C. Woods of San Antonio, Texas, said: "I've hung my last man. I'm through," a tale of human brutality and barbariism came to an end.

Two tragic plane crashes in Newfoundland also made the headlines in October. We balked plenty at the high cost of living again because we had expected a leveling off to nearer pre-war prices. With the world slowly trying to emerge from its great illness of the war we began to concentrate at home on the coming November elections. We do not remember a year in which we saw more political prophets hit the zero mark for accuracy.

NOVEMBER and DECEMBER are really just like yesterday and might be looked at together in retrospect.

First attention came on the outcome of the elections and the many new angles of government reaction to the nation's needs and its ills. The Republican sweep came unheralded like the summer storm. The last two months of 1946 saw another coal strike, another tragic hotel fire in Atlanta, Georgia, and also the passing of Damon Runyon, one of America's greatest and most beloved newspaper men, and Governor-elect Talmadge of Georgia.

Then in December we went to work on the German war factories, destroying twenty-five plants for the manufacture of explosives and the sprawling underground installations of the Messerschmitt airplane works. We have found a permanent site for the home of the United Nations and are marching on to 1947 with many good promises ahead. We are about to make our customary New Year's resolutions and envisage a year of growth and expansion.

We become more mass-conscious of our needs and we are realizing more fully that we must look on our human accomplishments in the light of cooperation effort. Just as we do not wish to repeat our personal mistakes of yesterday or last week, we become conscious that the whole aggregate of the human race must also be made aware not to repeat its blunders and failures of the past.

And so we have looked at 1946 in retrospect and lived through the changes and happenings that the historians of tomorrow will record as the history of our land. We hope we haven't bored you with this little review. It is nice sometimes to sit down together and reminesce. It has been like a journey we all made together through the year with its laughs and its tragedies that make up life.

Thus, in conclusion, there is no better wish than to say to all our readers the one real wish we repeat so faithfully each time December hands its scepter to young January:

"A VERY HAPPY AND PROSPEROUS NEW YEAR TO YOU!"
