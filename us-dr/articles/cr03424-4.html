Original in English.Original en inglés.

Intervention in Any Form is OUT

An International Committee's Report to the Dominican Republic's Secretary of State for Foreign Relations, and to the University of Santo Domingo, in which the actions of the Organization of American States' Inter-American Peace Committee are analyzed . . . leading to the conclusion that there is a trend to disregard any documented evidence concerning invasion of Dominican sovereign territory and intervention in the Dominican Republic's internal affairs.

The members of the Study Panel who have been invited by the Secretariat of State for Foreign Relations of the Dominican Republic and by the University of Santo Domingo to give an opinion on certain juridical matters of an international nature which are of concern to the Dominican Republic, analyzed, firstly, the following questions:

a) Considered in the light of the Inter-American Treaty of Reciprocal Assistance and of precedents in the application of that Treaty, was the request submitted by the Government of the Dominican Republic to the Council of the Organization of American States on July 2, 1959, made upon sound grounds?

b) If the answer is in the affirmative, was it or was it not warranted that the Council of the Organization appoint an investigating committee to ascertain the facts alleged by the Dominican Government for the Inter-American Treaty of Reciprocal Assistance to be applied?

c) In the light of the facts and the circumstances adduced by the Dominican Government in its request of July 2, 1959, to the Council of the Organization of American States, does there exist any cause of action for international responsibility under the terms of the 1928 Havana Convention on the Duties and Rights of States in the event of Civil Strife, or in keeping with general legal principles or those of international common law?

The Study Panel was able to examine material evidence such as the documents taken from members of the revolutionary expeditions which invaded the Dominican Republic in the month of June, 1959, as well as photographs made during the embarkation of the expeditions' members and the testimony of captives taken prisoners by the Dominican Government in the military operations staged upon Dominican territory and to whom the Dominican Government has subsequently granted pardon. Included in the testimony given before the Study Panel there is that of Delio Gomez Ochoa, a former Major in the Cuban Revolutionary Army, and that of Poncio Rafael Pou Saleta and Francisco Medardo German, both Dominicans. Statements were also made before the Study Panel by the Dominican Republic's Secretary of State for Justice, Licenciate Mario Abreu Penzo, and by the Republic's Attorney General, Licenciate Luis E. Suero. As a result of its examination of that testimony and material evidence as well as of the legal texts and precedents applicable in the matter, the Study Panel expresses the opinion hereinafter set forth.

1. That from the facts upon which the Government of the Dominican Republic based the request it submitted to the Council of the Organization of American States on July 2, 1959, it is clear that the invasions of Dominican territory on June 14 and June 20, 1959, by armed bands from abroad were with the aim of starting and fomenting civil strife in the Dominican Republic, and were sufficiently grave, according to the Inter-American Treaty of Reciprocal Assistance, as to warrant that request upon the basis of Articles 6 and 12 of said instrument as well as upon those cases in which, after due investigation of the alleged facts, the Rio Treaty of 1947 had been applied. Among these precedents, the Study Panel bore in mind not only those set forth in the document of the Pan American Union entitled Applications of the Inter-American Treaty of Reciprocal Assistance (1948-1955) but also the other cases immediately preceding the Dominican request of July 2, 1959, and that are part and parcel of the international situation which caused the Fifth Meeting of Consultation of Foreign Ministers in Santiago, Chile, to be held.

2. It was likewise warranted that, in view of the facts alleged by the Dominican Government and bearing in mind precedents in keeping with the application of the Inter-American Treaty of Reciprocal Assistance, the Council of the Organization of American States appoint an investigating committee to ascertain the facts alleged by the Government of the Dominican Republic in support of its request, in order to determine whether that Treaty should be applied.

3. That from the careful study and confirmation that they have made of the facts denounced by the Government of the Dominican Republic in its request of July 2, 1959 to the Council of the Organization of American States, it develops that there do exist circumstances upon which to establish a cause of action for international responsibility, based upon either violation of the Havana Convention of 1928 on the Duties and Rights of States in the event of Civil Strife or upon universally recognized general principles of international law, such as those established under the Three Rules of Washington adopted in 1871.

The following question was considered by the Study Panel: "To give its opinion as to the Report of June 6, 1960 by the Inter-American Peace Committee, in the light of the observations of the Dominican Government on this document, dated June 20, 1960, and in the light of Resolution IV of the fifth meeting of Consultation of American Foreign Ministers held in Santiago, Chile, as well as of the Statutes of the Inter-American Peace Committee currently in force, and of the Charter of the Organization of American States."

The Study Panel has analyzed carefully the above mentioned Report of the Inter-American Peace Committee as well as all the other inter-American instruments which determine the scope of the powers of that body. It also has examined the complete record of the Committee's action and numerous aids for an appreciation of facts and circumstances which in a general manner contribute to the creation of those international tensions obtaining in the relations of countries in the Caribbean area. As a result of the above mentioned study, the Panel sets forth the following opinion.

Resolution 14 which was voted at the Second Meeting of Foreign Ministers held in Havana in 1940, in its only WHEREAS clearly defined the aim of that Resolution by giving as the reasons for it that the solution of the differences existing between some American States is imperative in order to attain greater unity of the Continent.

Based upon this, the Resolution ordained the following: the establishment in the Americas of a committee that would see to it permanently that those States between whom there exist or arise any conflict, of whatever nature it might be, solve said conflict as quickly as possible, and that the committee suggest for that purpose, without prejudice to the manner that might be chosen by the parties or those methods chosen by them, methods and proposals leading to such a solution.

The Inter-American Peace Committee has been functioning as an investigating committee, as a Commission d'enquete, in its first stage, and has devoted itself to ascertaining the facts; but once those facts have been established, it goes on to report, giving—and this is its principal mission—methods and proposals leading to a solution, or what is the same, to conciliation. Adapting, therefore, the provisions of the Gondra Pact and of the General Inter-American Convention on Conciliation in a sui generis fashion and in a quicker and more expeditious procedure, the Inter-American Peace Committee is capable of rendering invaluable services, if only it does not defeat the ends for which it was created, as it has done in its Report.

In accordance with its own provisions, the field of application of the above mentioned Resolution 14 comprises the following elements:

a) A conflict already existing or arising at a given moment.

b) A conflict of any nature, that is, whether legal or political.

c) Need of an immediate or urgent solution.

d) Steps taken by the Committee to find the solution.

e) The Committee's power to suggest methods and proposals leading to the solution of the conflict or difference.

f) Ability of the parties to choose formulas and methods leading to this same end.

g) Express responsibility of the Committee to permanently endeavor to find a solution to the conflict.

With respect to this it is interesting to note that the Statutes currently in force emphasize the separation of the functions of the Inter-American Peace Committee. In the first place, it will be noted that Article 1 of the Statutes introduces an element in the basic definition of the Committee which does not appear in Resolution 14 of the Second Meeting of Consultation of Foreign Ministers, by virtue of which Resolution this body was created. In fact, while Resolution 14 stipulates that the Committee will permanently endeavor that the States between whom there exists some conflict, etc., the Statutes of the Peace Committee states in Article 1 that the Inter-American Peace Committee will permanently endeavor, "within its powers," that the States between whom there exists some conflict, etc.

Moreover, Article 16 of the Statutes in force of the Committee states that the Committee will offer its services in fulfillment of its mission and in accordance with the nature of its functions.

Within the fundamental aims and the field of application of the powers of the above mentioned Committee, the Fifth Meeting of Consultation of Foreign Ministers, held in Santiago, Chile in 1959, extended the powers of the Committee, without abolishing its basic functions, to the following questions:

a) Consideration of the situation of international tensions in the Caribbean area, in its general and multiple aspects, in the light of the principles and norms which govern the Inter-American system, and of the means to insure observance by the States of the principles of non-intervention and non-aggression.

b) To that end, it empowered the Committee to examine: 1) methods and procedures to prevent any activities coming from abroad and intended to overthrow legally constituted government or to create cases of intervention or aggression contemplated in instruments such as the Convention on the Duties and Rights of States in case of Civil Strife—and its Protocol as well, we add—; 2) the relation between the violations of human rights or lack of exercise of representative democracy, on the one hand, and the political tensions which affect the peace of the Hemisphere, on the other hand.

c) The immediate undertaking of a thorough study of the above mentioned questions.

d) The new powers—paragraph 4—were temporary in nature, extending until the conclusion of the XI Inter-American Conference.

Upon examining the Report of the Inter-American Peace Committee, the members of the Study Panel find that this document does not correspond to the ends and competence of the Committee for the following reasons:

a) It has limited the conflict, that is, the political tensions, to the Dominican Republic, in violation of the broader mission embodied in Resolution IV which ordered the Committee to study the situation of international tensions in the Caribbean area in its general and multiple aspects.

b) In submitting its June 6, 1960 Report, it has acted as a Court of Justice by ascribing responsibilities, leveling charges and pronouncing absolutions. The Committee's purpose, which is to investigate for the purpose of bringing about conciliation and pacification, to contribute with the delicate and complex task which an assignment of this nature regarding sovereign powers entails, has been perverted.

c) In dealing with a case directly connected with the general situation of the political tensions obtaining in the Caribbean area, and in view of the intense subjectivity of that situation, the Committee should have borne in mind that circumstance in giving out its Report, inasmuch as the terms in which it has been conceived lend themselves to producing affects that are contrary to the conciliatory aim that constitutes the Committee's raison d'etre.

d) The Committee has failed to exercise permanent vigilance to bring about the disappearance of the above mentioned tensions, which in this case creates a situation of conflict.

On the other hand, and with reference to the new powers provisionally granted to the Committee, it has exceeded its authority also, inasmuch as: a) it has exercised improperly powers which were expressly denied to the Inter-American Committee on Human Rights when the Council of the Organization of American States discussed the Statutes it had drafted for the latter Committee to function; b) in a marked manner, it has not afforded the methods and procedures recommended in order to forestall subversive activities intended to overthrow the Government of the Dominican Republic, ignoring, inexplicably, the investigations conducted by its own Sub-Committee which visited Ciudad Trujillo for the purpose of investigating the invasions of Constanza, Maimon and Estero Hondo; c) it abstained from pointing to the other countries involved in the causes of the tension in the Caribbean area, to condemn only the Dominican Republic, thereby overreaching its authority, as it made a singular condemnation instead of a generalized pronouncement in pursuit of conciliation.

In addition to the foregoing, the members of the Study Panel regard as a substantive objection to the Report of the Inter-American Peace Committee—and, at that, one having a general character—the unwarranted use that body made of secret evidence that was not subjected either to debate or to examination by the Dominican Republic, dropping the full weight of its accusation upon the latter, with an obvious disregard for the right of defense and the judicial guarantee that should have been the rule in said action wherein the Dominican Republic has been condemned without knowledge and without being afforded an opportunity to challenge evidence with which it still remains unacquainted.

For all these reasons, the members of the Study Panel invited by the Dominican Republic's Secretariat of State for Foreign Relations and by the University of Santo Domingo

DECLARE:

That the Report presented by the Inter-American Peace Committee, which is related to the Dominican Republic's situation which has been studied, deviated from the prior record of application of the America instruments which have been approved in inter-American assemblies, meetings and conferences, inasmuch as in that Report there is implicit an exceeding of the Committee's statutory powers ever since its origin, and it does not follow from those additional assignments intrusted to it by the Meeting of Consultation of Ministers of Foreign Relations held in Santiago, Chili in 1959, and, finally has promoted a fissure in the basic conceptions of the Organization of American States which stands in urgent need of a permanent and integral cohesion among the peoples and governments comprising this regional community.

1. International policy in the Americas is grounded upon two abiding basic concepts: one is political in nature and consolidated by the practice of regionalism, and it is marked by integral solidarity; the other is eminently juridical in nature and hallowed by the American republics—that of non-intervention. Integral solidarity, which covers the entire range of the progress of peoples, has moved forward amid obstacles that were not unforseeable in its development. Non-intervention, which stems from an evolutionary process with which the American continent is well acquainted, defines historically the unchanging policy of the American republics. From these concepts which the peoples of the Americas have defined, subscribed and ratified in meetings and international American conferences and which identify the nations of this hemisphere, there stem consistently renewed affirmations proclaiming the recognition of independence, sovereignty and the juridical equality of states in the establishment of the principle of non-intervention.

2. That independence which entails a full awareness in making free pronouncements and the organic structure of nations allowing its exercise against all arbitrary acts by either their own or alien forces, whether domestic or foreign, inasmuch as that state enjoying it is its own master and the one responsible for its own destiny, is a trend that expresses itself unhesitantly.

3. Sovereignty, which is grounded in complete internal autonomy and becomes manifest in unqualified external independence, is assured in the international relations of the peoples of this Continent not by a policy of force, but instead by that juridical harmony that is generated by the rule of law which makes the strong bend before the weak and exalts those principles that go to make the abiding foundations of that rule of law.

4. The juridical equality hallowed in the fundamental rights and duties of states defined, subscribed and ratified in instruments that are in force; unaltered by the contingencies in the international life of the Americas, and whose foremost synthesis is to be found in the Charter of the Organization of American States — presupposes the same powers and capabilities to develop their existence as subjects of international law. There is no admitting differences that are offensive to the honor of democratically organized peoples, nor those that may diminish the full realization of basic equality in the practice and exercise of their activities, either individually or collectively.

5. The principle of non-intervention which has been attained through painful and unforgotten stages of American history and marked devotion to the rule of law, and to which the American peoples have dedicated no less a thing than a policy informed by the loftiest civilizing and progressive spirit would seem to be in jeopardy owing to a backsliding that no one would have thought possible among the countries of this hemisphere, particularly in the light of the history of sovereignty in the Americas, which arose amid the relentless struggle for the conquest of nationality.

THEREFORE, and having in view the solidarity of the American nations that are joined in the Organization of American States,

We Are of The Opinion That:

a) The independence, sovereignty and juridical equality of the American States which have become a hallowed tradition, should be safeguarded by those regional organizations that already have been created or in future will be created, those that are already operating or have still to become so, and, moreover, should be upheld so as to further the most equitable realization of the fate of the peoples of this Continent.

b) Intervention, whether direct or indirect and whatever its cause, in the domestic or external affairs of States, affected by one or more States or by regional international organizations belonging to either the inter-American system or to the world system, is counter to the immutable principles of NON-INTERVENTION, and any weakening whatsoever of this international rule, be it from excessive individualism on the part of the strong or from pressure exerted by international political organizations, implies a deplorable juridical contradiction that impinges upon the effectiveness of those matters falling exclusively within the realm of sovereignty, and is harmful to any organized cooperation in solidarity.
