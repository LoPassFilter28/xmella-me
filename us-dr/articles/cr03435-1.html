THE NATIONAL SCENE Summer Fare of Politics and Scandals

First evidence of pay-offs was uncovered in the Billie Sol Estes hearings last week. But that was only the centrifuge about which events swirled faster and faster.

Senate investigators were hard put Independence week for time to properly observe the high principles that attended our national birth. The subcommittee digging into the Billie Sol Estes mess almost lost the headlines to Johnny-come-latelies smelling out fresh scandals in such assorted commodities as cotton, rice and sugar.

The Billie Sol story won out in a photo finish only because two dejected Agriculture Department agents from Eufaula, Okla., 'fessed up they accepted $1,640.80 of Billie Sol's pyramiding, paperish wealth for transferring about 330 acres of McIntosh County cotton allotments to Estes' Reeves County (Texas) stronghold.

Chairman McClellan of Arkansas, sometimes the indignantly righteous prosecutor, quietly coaxed from these neighbor-State Okies repudiations of previous affidavits denying any payments from Estes.

They told of accepting $5 an acre for locating and transferring the allotments at the behest of Estes' agents. Estes is accused of using illegally obtained allotments for building a multi-million dollar cotton-fertilizer-grain storage empire.

Not Bad People

At the conclusion of one of Estes' most damaging days, Chairman McClellan told the agents:

"I don't believe you are bad people," and commended them for telling the truth as "victims of a bigger scheme to get rich quick."

But the Agriculture Department refused to let the men resign and instead suspended them—apparently for life.

Up to this point the witness chair had been occupied for three days by the man in Washington's hottest seat—Agriculture Secretary Freeman. A great deal of the time was spent in jousting with Republican Senators Mundt of South Dakota and Curtis of Nebraska, old hands at inquiry.

More heat than light was generated as Senator Mundt demanded to know why Secretary Freeman had ordered a study of his and Senator Curtis' correspondence with the Agriculture Department the past 10 years. One word led to several thousand, none very revealing, and the outpouring was climaxed with a demand from the South Dakotan "to put up or shut up."

Senator Mundt wound up by educing assurances from Mr. Freeman that, as far as he knew, his department had punished all known offenders by firing three Washington and five field employes. He thus contributed a bit more to Agriculture Department embarrassment when the Oklahoma payoff was revealed a day later.

Freeman Hits Back

But ex-Marine Freeman, a battler all the way, had not departed without leaving his mark. Studiously he had brought to Thursday's inquisition a statement on the General Accounting Office's Monday exposure of the first of the week's new spate of scandals.

This, too, involved the luckless Agriculture Department. GAO had revealed that from 1959-61 brokers acting as official sales agents for the Commodity Credit Corporation "sold to themselves $400 million worth of Government cotton at prices averaging $4 less, and ranging up to $20 a bale less, than established market prices."

Mr. Freeman wanted to point out that this unethical, if not strictly illegal, dealing in surplus cotton was perpetrated under a Republican administration except for the last three months. This drew little sympathy from some committee members who, acting as though they had been hoodwinked, apparently wondered whether they had heard a suggestion that two wrongs make a right.

The Secretary was careful to vouch for the administration's intentions of recouping, if possible, the $12 to $15 million the Government lost through the cotton sales.

It's Texas Again

But it didn't end there. Texas came into the spotlight again as the Agriculture Department acknowledged skullduggery in the rice fields. Again the gimmick was the transfer of allotments, this time between the Agriculture Office managers in two counties. Now under suspension, the managers reportedly received $28,000 for selling allotments to farmers since 1959. A third county manager was paid $1,500.

This budding scandal may bloom, for the FBI is rummaging around Texas' 20 rice-growing counties. It came to light when one of the managers died and a woman clerk in the office, refusing an offer to continue the arrangement with another county manager, blew the whistle.

Not to be outdone, the already involved sugar legislation wound up the week as the foundation for yet another investigation. In all the furious jockeying for premium-payment United States sugar quotas, no fewer than 25 foreign countries had lobbyists scrounging through the Capitol in quest for real sugar.

They got it—to the tune of some $500,000, according to some estimates. President Kennedy deplored this mooching through non-diplomatic circles, he made clear at his news conference. Quick to take up the cudgels, the Senate Foreign Relations Committee slated a year-long investigation into these and all other efforts of foreign governments to "influence United States policy."

Touching this off was the revelation that agents of the late Dominican Republic's Dictator Trujillo attempted to influence Representative Cooley, chairman of the House Agriculture Committee, in the quest for sugar preferences. Mr. Cooley emphatically denied giving the Dominicans, or any other country, the privilege of secret meetings.

Mr. Kennedy is trying to get away, by stages, from the lobbyist-drawing premium quota system of sugar purchases. But there are friendly relations to be maintained with Latin American markets, and Friday the Senate tried to placate three of the more unhappy countries, the Dominican Republic, Argentina and Peru, with an amendment setting aside additional quotas for them. The bill must go back to the House for confirmation.
