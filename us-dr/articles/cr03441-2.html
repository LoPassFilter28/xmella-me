U. S. Renews Relations With 2 Latin Countries

By JOHN M. HIGHTOWER

Associated Press Staff Writer

The United States reopened diplomatic relations with the Dominican Republic and Honduras yesterday but kept the pressure on their junta governments by stalling the return of ambassadors and continuing to withhold aid.

The action ended more than two months of diplomatic boycott policy toward the two countries, instituted after their constitutional governments were overthrown by military uprisings.

The publicly-stated reason for recognition of the non-democratic regimes was that both have set up "election timetables" for restoring elected constitutional governments in about 18 months.

Uprisings Feared

State Department officials said prolonged refusal to reopen normal diplomatic contacts with the two countries could have brought on a new uprising in one or both of them and thereby contributed to greater political instability.

These officials said also that by holding off the return of ambassadors and the restoration of aid the United States holds considerable bargaining power in reserve. Washington will watch closely to see if political liberty is respected in the two countries and if they move forward on their announced programs for restoring democratic rule.

Officials denied that the decision to grant recognition represents some change in United States policy under the Johnson administration. They said they would have been happier to have a faster election process in the two Caribbean countries, but that actually United States policy objectives calling for a return of democratic government and for respect for international obligations were in fact being fulfilled.

U. S. Envoy May Return

Depending on how the administration of President Oswaldo Lopez Arellano handles these issues in the next few weeks, Ambassador Charles R. Burrows probably will return to his post at Tegucigalpa Honduras sometime next month.

Whether Ambassador John Bartlow Martin will ever go back to Santo Domingo, however, is uncertain in spite of word from official sources earlier this week that both men would resume their positions following recognition.

Mr. Martin has been critical of the three-man civilian junta installed after the military revolt which ousted President Juan Bosch in late September.

The State Department's announcement said United States recognition came after "consultation with other hemisphere governments."

The statement cited the promised "election timetables."

In Honduras the timetable calls for a constituent assembly to assume power March 16, 1965. The schedule set up by the Dominican regime says an elected government will take over August 16.

Notification of recognition was delivered to the governments in Santo Domingo and Tegucigalpa.

After the dominican and Honduran constitutional governments were ousted in the early fall, the United States cracked down hard, denying recognition, suspending aid and calling its ambassadors home. The effect was to cut off a flow of deliveries to the Dominican Republic under promised assistance mounting to $55 million and to Honduras under scheduled help amounting to $6.5 million.

International Debate

One reason for the quick decisive reaction was the desire here to prevent any trigger effect in other countries. Many officials believe that a military take over in one Latin American capital sometimes inspires potential revolutionists in other capitals.

The situation also set off an international debate over what could be done to prevent military coups. Venezuelan President Romulo Betancourt advocated strong international action. Most other Latin American leaders were less enthusiastic than he about the possibility of raising barriers to defend constitutional governments.

Mr. Betancourt, a longtime foe of military dictatorships in his won country, has been under pressure from Communist terrorists who wanted to force a military seizure of Caracas. Two weeks ago Mr. Betancourt successfully carried through his plan to hold national elections.

State Department officials said this eased their problem of deciding to resume relations with Honduras and the Dominican Republic.
