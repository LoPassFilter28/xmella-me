Our Soaring Society Editor Thrilled by Dominican Capital

TRUJILLO CITY, Dominican Republic, March 21—Sitting high above the river that flows by the Capital of the Dominican Republic, we'll try to review the day for you. A day so full of differences and excitement that you'd scarcely believe it could all be crammed into a brief 12 hours. Up with the dawn as Miami rose out of bay and ocean. A police escort—oh, my yes, we've all the attentions of visiting royalty—whirled us to the airport. There, en masse, we breakfasted on the airport terrace.

You can't imagine that? Well, you've never seen that Pan-American airport, all blue and gay, with handsome attendants flitting at your beck and call. Ham and eggs we had, in true American fashion. The last snack of unimaginative food we have had since!

Into the clipper and up in the air, out across the sea, smoothly, sailingly. For two hours we floated along, and then Cuba. Not Havana, but the airport town of Antilla, tiny and quaint. Then on again to San Pedro. Meantime, with seven hours of flying on our hands, we decided to learn a few things about the country we were heading for. Santo Domingo de Guzman, that used to be the capital. When Trujillo gained control of the republic and later became president, the generalissimo renamed the capital for himself. The city was founded on Aug. 4, 1496 by the brother of Christopher Columbus. And that it was called until the General decided he preferred his own name.

DISLIKES OLD NAME

They do say that the name was changed by the citizens, but the General doesn't like references made to the old name. In fact, when mail is directed to Santo Domingo, it is returned "place unknown." But what's in a name? It is the oldest city on this side of the world; boasts the oldest cathedral, the oldest university. The first horse brought to the Western Hemisphere was taken to Santo Domingo in 1551 and the first cow in 1554.

About 2:30 we sailed over San Pedro, 30 miles from Trujillo City, and tho we had lunched nicely on the plane, in the old Spanish custom, we were fed immediately and well—several hours of eating—at the famous Killbourne sugar plantation. More of that later.

Seven distinguished men met us, with all the fanfare—again we felt it—of visiting royalty. Cabinet officers and important citizens. We heard first, just as we landed, of Arturo Logrono, "Secretario de Estada de la Presidencia." To us that means, not secretary to the President, but Secretary to the ofice of the Presidency.

HE'S QUITE HEFTY

Senor Logrono is amazing. A youngish fellow of 38, he weighs, well—conservatively speaking—in the upper 200 brackets. He has several chins, and smokes a pipe incessantly; a pipe that is shaped to follow the contours of the chin-line—or lack of it. His desk is built, well, perhaps we can call it kidney shaped. Anyhow, it is carved to fit around the rotundity of Senor Logrono. They do say that he hasn't seen his feet for 20 years.

Now we've just set down our effects in Casa Senora, a huge rambling house where half the party is billeted. Senora de Senora, a distinguished lady of English descent, runs the house and in the genteel southern manner, takes paying guests. The hotels of Trujillo City still have a long way to go, and anyhow it is more charming to stay with somebody who really lives in a strange city.

HAVING WONDERFUL TIME!

The other half of the party—the official members—are quartered with the generalissimo himself, in his vast town house. Later during our visit we'll have a party at his fabulous mountain estate. The city house is handsome, and decorated thruout in the French manner. One of the General's ancestors was a French marquis and the tastes hold over.

Tonight, as soon as we find a bath tub, array ourselves in full regalia, pin on the car load of orchids which has just arrived, we'll be off for a dinner and reception given by some kind friends whose name we haven't yet managed. Then begin the ceremonies for which we made this delightful journey. For luncheon, President Jacinto Peynado is lunching us at the presidential palace. The next night there is a great reception at the palace. Then we attend a session of parliament. And that night we retire to Trujillo's mountain villa for a grand ball.
