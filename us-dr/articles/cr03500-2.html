Peace Corps Goes Its Unruffled Way As U. S. Halts Other Dominican Aid

By GEORGE SHERMAN

Star Staff Writer

SANTO DOMINGO, Oct. 3.—Upsetting political crises may come and go in this Caribbean country, but the Peace Corps has taken deep root.

It is the only United States aid operation which has not been suspended in retaliation against the military ouster of the constitutional President Juan Bosch, last week.

"Our programs are not touched because we do not deal with politics," said Andres Hernandez, middle-aged but dynamic Peace Corps director here. He himself came here from six years of work on land reform in the primitive-rain forests of Guatemala.

"One of our slogans is that we are here to help people, not the government in power," he explained.

Political Effect Seen

But in a larger sense Mr. Hernandez is wrong. The Peace Corps is the most radical political operation which the United States has going in the Dominican Republic — no less than in the rest of Latin America.

Since the arrival of the first volunteers here in July, 1962, 150 of these "Americanos pobres" (poor Americans) have fanned out into 50 communities across the countryside. Through such simple devices as poultry raising and hog breeding they are launching a lethal assault on that stifling inertia which has held the mass of underprivileges here in semi-bondage for generations.

"The most exciting reward of our work is a new sense of optimism you begin to feel," said Mr. Hernandez. "In many small ways the awful burden of defeatism begins to lift. That is the spirit which will survive our departure."

A perfect example lies right here in Santo Domingo in the barrio (quarters) of "Ensanche Luperon," one of the numerous slums which Mr. Hernandez labels "our hardest nuts to crack."

Carve Out Ghetto

In three small blocks of mangled shacks some 3,000 squatters have carved out a miserable ghetto. Most are ignorant peasants who have flooded here from the countryside in search of nonexistent work.

Rude homes of scrap lumber and palm leaf are packed so close together that whatever small patios exist are used for latrines rather than gardening. Inside the houses, flimsy partitions of cardboard and newspaper make scant provision for privacy in families numbering anywhere from four to eight children. Outside, naked babies with swollen bellies and spindly legs tell a mute tale of their parents' desolation.

A volunteer chosen to work with this smouldering resentment is Angel Lara, Mexican-born American. His task is "community development" — a phrase he admits he cannot define.

Thirty-four years old, short and swarthly, Angel is the prototype of simple dedication.

"Owes a Debt"

"I came here because I owe a debt to the United States," he said in his softly-accented English. He meant the debt of citizenship. Angel Lara first came to the States in 1948 as an illegal "wetback" from Mexico. He was caught and deported.

But that was only the beginning of a two-year campaign against American authorities during which he invaded Texas three times and was caught and sent back to Mexico twice.

The third time he was thrown into jail in El Paso, where his luck suddenly changed. A Texas businessman "adopted" him and after an eight-month legal battle he obtained a residence visa in 1951. At the age of 24 — now in Los Angeles and virtually illiterate — he decided he needed an education. With the help and understanding of teachers, he completed all requirements of the elementary grammar school system of California in one record year. He raced through high school during the following year. He became a naturalized citizen in 1957.

Approach Is Simple

"I was raised on a tiny farm in Mexico," he said. "Maybe it does sound corny but I know what these kids feel."

Angel's approach to his barrio is simple—to show the inhabitants that they can improve their lot themselves with the crudest materials.

First he concentrated on the sanitary conditions. He wheedled the Dominican Department of Public Works into sending over several truckloads of gravel to put on dirt roads and grounds which turn into swamps during the torrential rainy season here.

"At first people did not want to spread gravel," he said. "They said that the government should do it for them. But when I picked up a shovel and went to work, several others began to help and after the first day everyone pitched in."

Today the main swamps are gone and the mosquitoes they bred have vanished.

Perhaps Angel's greatest success has been with recreation for teen-agers and younger youths who aimlessly overrun the slum by the hundreds. He spotted a large vacant lot nearby used for grazing goats and for garbage disposal. From the city government he received permission to clear the lot for a baseball field, basketball and volleyball courts.

The boys had just finished clearing the grounds when the head of a construction firm with influence in the public works department arrived on the scene and claimed ownership of the lot. After several days of argument he brought the police, kicked the youngsters out, and had his workers put barbed wire fence around the land.

"The boys wanted to stone the man and the police and tear down the fence," related Angel. "But I said let's get rid of him legally. We already had permission from the city to make the recreation field."

Fences Fall

Within three weeks the case was settled and the fence was down. But not until Angel went to Mrs. Bosch—wife of the deposed president who took a personal interest in this slum, and she interceded against the alleged owner. Today the park is still not cleared of all grass due to this delay. But the boys are preparing to put in the first posts for a backstop which the local brewery has bought for them at a nominal $2 apiece.

The problem of the adults is more difficult to solve.

"Politics mean nothing to them," explained Angel. "They only want jobs. A good government is one which gives them work; a bad one is one which does not."
