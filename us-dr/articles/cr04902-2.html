Gen. Trujillo Still Has a Man-Sized Grip On Dominican Republic After 20 Years

By PAUL SANDERS

Associated Press Staff Writer

CIUDAD TRUJILLO, Dominican Republic.—Rafael Leonidas Trujillo, aging generalissimo of this little tropical land, still seems very much the boss after nearly 29 years in power.

Some Dominicans, and foes around the Caribbean, would like to see him go and democracy established in the Dominican Republic. The country never has had a democratic system in its turbulent history, the bloodiest in Latin America before Trujillo took over.

No handpicked successor is in evidence.

Now 68, the usually soft-spoken but iron-fisted Trujillo rules by dealing out a combination of welfare, theatrics, ruthless suppression of political opposition, economic development, employment and efficient administration.

In some ways he acts like a perpetual political candidate. If there's a flood or serious fire, Trujillo is on the job dispensing relief. He's out in full dress at major celebrations.

Suppresses Criticism

His controlled press and radio lets Dominicans have only the news he thinks they should have. Much of this material glorifies the generalissimo and his little country. Foreign publications carrying anything remotely anti-Trujillo are suppressed.

The sunbathed republic has a population of nearly 3 million—almost three times what it was 30 years ago—and covers 19,333 square miles, about twice the size of Vermont. The population density is more than 115 per square mile.

Trujillo's propaganda machine, playing up to national pride, ceaselessly reminds the country of his record in making a once-bankrupt and revolt-ridden country financially sound and prosperous—prosperous by Caribbean standards. The 1958 income per capita was $242. It's more than $1,900 in the United States.

Trujillo's program, including his brain-washing machine, has been effective—but not entirely so.

Dissidents—their numbers and unity difficult to ascertain—believe he has outlived his usefulness and should get out.

Whether an internal revolt will develop is a question.

To a visitor Trujillo appears to have a man-sized grip on the country, particularly among the working and peasant classes. It is among these people, two-thirds of them colored or mulatto, that the welfare programs and propaganda is most effective.

What opposition there is seems concentrated among the well-educated younger people who have been abroad and know what political freedom means. But Dominicans are hesitant to talk about politics, and Trujillo has a well-organized intelligence system keeping tab on things at home and abroad.

Single Party System

A single party system puts the generalissimo's project through the legislature—and not infrequently passes resolutions glorifying him or his family. The Dominican Party was organized in 1931 and through it Trujillo carefully handles legislation in a thoroughly legalistic fashion.

Dominican exiles in Cuba, Venezuela and New York speak hopefully of toppling the bemedaled Benefactor of the Fatherland—one of the titles voted by the Dominican congress—but this may take a lot of doing.

To threats of invasion by the exiles—supposedly with the help of volunteers from Cuba and Venezuela—Trujillo replied in typical fashion.

He strengthened his armed forces, implied that he might counterattack if necessary, and organized an "anti-Communist foreign legion." The legion, made up largely of Dominicans, was given the anti-Communist tag because Trujillo likes to make people think that all his foes are Reds or fellow travelers. The Communist Party has been outlawed in the Dominican Republic since 1936.

He is reputed to have an efficient army numbering around 18,000 and a good little air force by Caribbean standards. The goal of the "foreign legion" is given as 25,000, but it may turn out to be much smaller.

Attacking His Critics

Dominican newspapers and radio stations direct a steady flow of epithets at those who have been most critical of the generalissimo—Prime Minister Fidel Castro of Cuba, President Romulo Betancourt of Venezuela, ex-President Jose Figueres of Costa Rica and Gov. Luis Munoz Marin of Puerto Rico.

His brother, Hector, 51, was given the presidency by election in 1952, and re-elected in 1957 for another five-year term. Hector is reported to have much ability, but lacking the benefactor's personality and dramatic touch. Hector formerly commanded the national police and also has the title of generalissimo by act of Congress.

The dictator's oldest son, 30-year-old Gen. Rafael Leonidas Trujillo, jr., who figured as a playboy in Hollywood last year, is chairman of the joint chiefs of staff. The young man, nicknamed Ramfis, was a colonel when a young boy.

Gen. Jose Garcia Trujillo, a nephew, is secretary of defense, and Col. Luis Rafael Trujillo, another nephew, secretary of war and aviation.

The republic's tragic history of invasions and bloody wars helps explain the hold Trujillo has on the country. To many Dominicans he means peace and order.

At the beginning of the 20th century, the republic owed $30 million to United States banks. To safeguard the investment, the United States established control of Dominican customs in 1905, ending it in 1940 after Trujillo had been in power 10 years.

The Marine Occupation

Back in 1916 the republic's finances and politics had been so chaotic that President Woodrow Wilson sent the Marines to occupy the country. They stayed until 1924—and produced Trujillo.

Then 27 and standing 5 feet 7 and a lithe 127 pounds, Trujillo entered the new Dominican National Guard late in 1918. The guard was organized by the Marines to provide a "nonpolitical" army-police force, something the republic had never had. Before joining the guard Trujillo had been a Morse code telegrapher, a typist and the chief of guards at a sugar mill.

Under President Horacio Vasquez, Trujillo rose to be commander of the national guard, which had been named the national police.

When Vasquez changed the rules to get himself re-elected, another revolt started—the last serious uprising in the republic. Trujillo kept his police on the sidelines. Vasquez was forced out.

In elections held August 16, 1930, Trujillo, then 39, was chosen President.

Only 18 days after Trujillo took office, a vicious hurricane hit the capital. More than 1,000 persons were killed and 4,000 injured. Hundreds of flimsy dwellings were flattened.

The way Trujillo tackled disaster relief made a lasting impression on Dominicans. He declared martial law, called for help from the United States, put into effect a swift relief and rebuilding program.

There were sporadic attempts at revolt in the early years of what the Dominican Congress has labeled the "era of Trujillo." But his political-military machine steamrollered the opposition.

City Named for Him

The capital city of Santo Domingo—one of the oldest in the Western Hemisphere—was renamed Ciudad Trujillo by Congress in 1936.

Almost every business house in the city of 300,000 displays pictures of the benefactor and his brother, the President, and tokens of homage are everywhere.

An obelisk in Trujillo's honor was put up in one of the city's principal squares in 1937. It is inscribed with several of the generalissimo's remarks:

"My best friends are the workers."

"There is no danger in following me."

"Trujillo will not abandon his people. The people will not abandon Trujillo."
