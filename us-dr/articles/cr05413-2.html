MRS. READER READY TO CHAPERON SANTO DOMINGO.

New York, March 13.—Mrs. Ella Rayls Reader, chaperon of Santo Domingo and foe of the modus vivendi, waits in all patience for the Macedonian cry from the Caribbean. Things have been going by sixes and sevens in the Black republic; United States gunboats are getting in each other's way, and revolution follows revolution, yet the Dominicans need not despair. Mrs. Reader has notified Elihu Root, secretary of state, that she will soon proceed to the island, hold elections in the name of the people and assume the place to which she had been appointed as first lady of the treasury.

That there will be no need for further worry about the million dollars of customs duties which are now piling up in the City Bank, and neither Jiminezists, Moralesistas nor the followers of Morales or Caceres need apply. The whole island waits for a woman to intervene who will lead the way out of a financial wilderness.

"Of course, at the present time," said Mrs. Reader, when I saw her in her office on the top floor of an office building at 45 Wall street, "I have nothing to say. I am just waiting, and I intend to let Mr. Root do all the talking. Just please say that I am the most ignorant woman in New York."

Mrs. Reader's notice to Mr. Root reads as follows:

"I have the honor to enclose herewith for your excellency's information a certified copy of my authority from the central patriotic committee of Monte Cristi, in Santo Domingo—which, representing all the other patriotic committees in Santo Domingo empowers me to represent them in all parts of the world and to obtain for them international recognition and an arrangement of their financial difficulties."

In all the due forms of diplomatic heraldry she also tells the secretary of state that she will issue a call for the appointment of an international committee which will take up the question of the relation of all its foreign creditors.

Comparatively little is known of the central patriotic committee of Monte Cristi, but it is destined, if Mrs. Reader has her way, to be as renowned as the Three Tailors of Tobley street, who addressed to the British parliament a petition with that memorable preamble, "We the people of England." There are few diplomats, however, who would argue themselves unknown by saying that they did not know Mrs. Reader.

Not so many years ago she had a typewriting office in the Mills building in this city. She transcribed prospectuses, legal briefs and such things, and from coming in contact with the busy brains of Wall street she arrived at the conclusion that she might as well control the destinies of men and of nations as to be continually recording the plans of others. So she became a promoter, a dealer in railroads, both new and second-hand; an agent for valuable concessions, written on the best paper, and neatly folded and brass bound. She acquired copper mine concessions in Peru, and made contracts for improving the territories of eastern potentates and sued millionaires for breach of contract.

It occurred to her about two years ago that Santo Domingo needed reconstruction. So she acquired many reams of the very best Irish linen and got a typewriter with the latest improvements and went to work. In this task she was assisted by her husband. Mrs. Reader sent him to the distressed island with her picture and also with plans and specifications for a new fiscal system. It was not very long before she discovered that Santo Domingo wished her to be the financial agent of the republic, and everything was in readiness for the change when a United States gunboat appeared in the offing, and its commander assumed charge of the custom house.
