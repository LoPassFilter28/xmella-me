May and Wife Losers In Income Tax Cases

Mr. and Mrs. Herbert A. May will remember 1961 as the year both lost big-money income tax cases in the United States Tax Court.

The court this week rejected an appeal by Mr. May, retired industrialist, asking that $75,000 he received in 1956 be considered tax-free.

Mr. May's attorney, Earl F. Reed, reached by phone at Miami Beach, Fla., said he had not yet seen the court ruling. However, he added, "I imagine we will appeal the ruling."

Involved Yacht Sale

Last March the court ruled that a four-year attempt by Mrs. May, the former Marjorie Merriweather Post, to sell her luxurious seagoing yacht did not justify a $357,655 income tax deduction claim.

Mr. May, former senior vice president of the Westinghouse Air Brake Co., had contended that the $75,000 he received from this company was tax-exempt, since it was a reimbursement for expenses he had incurred on the company's behalf.

The court said the Internal Revenue Service originally had refused to allow a total of $145,846 in deductions claimed by Mr. May for entertainment, promotion and other expenses for the company between 1946 and 1952.

Mr. May said he had not been reimbursed by the company for these expenditures because of a company policy which sought not to have his expense accounts "out of line with others."

Agreed on $90,463

After negotiations, the court noted, IRS agreed to settle the tax case for $90,463, with $75,000 to be paid in cash and the balance over a period of several months.

Mr. May then wrote to the executive board of the Westinghouse Air Brake Co. in 1956, stating that it would be impossible for him to pay the $75,000 unless the company were willing to reimburse him for business expenses totaling this amount.

He noted in his letter that if the case went to trial, "I am going to have to give the names of a great many customers that I have entertained at times to account for my claimed deductions."

The company agreed to give Mr. May the $75,000, but claimed this as a deduction on its 1956 Federal corporate income tax return.

Not Shown on Returns

Mr. May did not submit any expense account or other substantiating documents to Air Brake for the $75,000, the court said. It added that Mr. May had not included the $75,000 in his gross income for his tax return that year, since he contended it was merely a reimbursement for business expenses.

However, the court said in its memorandum ruling that no evidence had been presented that Mr. May had a right to reimbursement from the company.

The court added that testimony of Air Brake executives showed that "the primary reason" the company made the $75,000 payment to Mr. May was that its chief executive officer had said that if the case went to trial, it would be "extremely embarrassing and detrimental to the company."

Mrs. Mays had claimed her deductions for losses she suffered while trying to sell the yacht Sea Cloud.

This vessel was built for her at Kiel, Germany, in 1931 at an original cost of $1,225,000 and finally sold in 1955 to George W. Gibbs, jr., of Jacksonville, Fla., for $500,000. He resold it for $550,000 to the late Generalissimo Rafael L. Trujillo of the Dominican Republic.
