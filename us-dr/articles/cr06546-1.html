Our 'Inconsistent' Foreign Policies

Your editorial, "Break With Trujillo," helps emphasize the difficulties we encounter when we undertake drastic measures against a right-wing dictatorship, such as the Dominican Republic, which has given us no offense, while we permit left-wing dictatorships, such as the kind now in control in Havana, which try to undermine our country, to get off almost unscathed. You might have added that it is policies such as these which account for our losing, as we have been doing, the cold war. We have invariably tried to close the stable door after the horse was stolen. The only thing consistent about our policies has been our inconsistencies.

It has been known for many years that Communists have infested the Latin American countries. They have pursued in these countries the process of infiltration and subversion by which they have been able, without the loss of a single soldier, to obtain dominion over one-third of the world's population and one-fourth of the world's surface. It was inevitable that sooner or later they would be able to challenge us in our own backyard. While we were pursuing what has come to be known as a policy of containment, by which further advances of the Communists would be halted, we were the ones who were actually being contained by them. While we were building bases in other lands, and relied on these bases, as France once relied on the Maginot Line, they were building far more effective bases among the peoples who make up these bases, so to destroy them.

Now that the inevitable has come to pass, so that it is even impossible to get the Latin American countries to take a strong position against a common enemy, we hope, by an expenditure of $600 million in Latin America, to undo the damage which has been done by our many years of neglect. It would be a cheap way of buying security, if security could really be bought that way.

But that method of making friends and influencing people is not likely to be more successful when applied to Latin America than it has proved to be in other parts of the world. Since the end of World War II we expended more than $70 billions in foreign aid. We have fewer friends in the countries to which this money was expended than we had before we started. We ought to continue to spend such money for humanitarian reasons, but as a means of furthering our own security or that of the free world this method has been a bust. The Russians get better returns on their promises than we got for our cash.

This policy of foreign aid, which we would now extend to Latin America, was predicated on a theory, for which there is no foundation, that if we could abolish poverty throughout the world the basis for Communist growth would be removed. While poverty is undoubtedly an ally of communism it does not alone account for Communist growth. When Russia took over Czechoslovakia the latter was the most advanced country economically in Europe. Subversion and infiltration can proceed apace in the advanced countries.

Secondly, even if it were true that the abolition of poverty would prevent the spread of communism, there is no reason to assume that we are in a position to abolish poverty everywhere in the world. We haven't even abolished poverty in our own country. It is a mistake to believe that there are no problems afflicting the world that money cannot cure, or that even if that were the case, we have the money to do it.

Nor will we obtain the security against aggression which we seek by applying sanctions to countries which have never threatened us, while we refrain from applying sanctions against countries which say they will use rockets against us, and which make common cause with our enemies. If we are to refrain from applying sanctions to the latter because to do so would be an unwarranted interference in their internal affairs, we should refrain from doing so against countries that have no designs on our security. If we are to embark on a course that will eliminate dictatorships wherever they exist, which is something we have nothing to do with, and which, in the final analysis, cannot succeed, we can count on a few Red dictatorships that ought to be eliminated. Instead, we are giving these Red dictatorships, such as Poland and Yugoslavia, financial and economic aid. It doesn't make sense.

It would be better to adopt, if it is not already too late to do so, measures that will attack communism where it is most vulnerable.

One of these measures would be to keep alive the hopes of the peoples in the captive nations that we will come to their aid if they revolt. It is there that the Russians are most vulnerable. The freedom loving people in those countries are our real allies. The leaders of the Russian Communists know that these people are being held by force and will revolt if war should break out and they have an opportunity to regain their freedom. That, more than our military bases, will be our best protection. It may involve what has come to be known as brinkmanship, but whenever we displayed brinkmanship the Communists backed down. That will preserve the peace, not endanger it.

We must also train, as Russia has done, the people who can successfully present our message to the peoples of the world as the Russian-trained students have presented theirs. There is now pending a Freedom Academy bill in Congress to accomplish this purpose. The development of this plan will take time. Whether there is that much time left is problematical. But we must not delay any longer. It would be one way of halting the retreat of the free nations.

Marx Lewis.
