Haiti: Threatening

Tensions in Haiti were high last week following a protest by the Dominican Republic to the Organization of American States on violations of its diplomatic immunity in Port-au-Prince. The United States warned of a "potentially explosive" situation in Haiti.

Hispaniola, the first land settled by Columbus, has again come alive with threats of invasion, civil war, and American intervention. Across the Windward Passage from Cuba, the island's two nations—the Dominican Republic on the east, and Haiti on the west—have been two of the most volatile in the hemisphere.

The Dominican Republic, dominating two thirds of the island, is now relatively advanced. Its literacy rate is good, and the Spanish-speaking descendants of Columbus till a fertile, mineral-rich land. Politically, however, the Dominicans are new to democracy, having held their first free election last year after 30 years of a Trujillo dictatorship.

Haiti has had a strange history. Originally inhabited by sophisticated and peaceful Indians, Haiti was quickly dominated by the Spanish. They imported African slaves to work the plantations. The native Indians died out, and the Spanish in turn were replaced by the French who chased the Spaniards into the eastern half, now the Dominican Republic.

The revolt of the Africans in 1793, led by French-educated men such as Toussaint L'Ouverture and Gen. Dessalines freed Haiti of the Europeans, but only instituted a dictatorial rule of black over black. Haitian history for the last 150 years is a bloody story of civil war, strife between Haiti and the Dominican Republic, and intervention by the United States.

Presently, President Duvalier rules, having been elected in 1957 after a military coup in 1956 in the capital of Port-au-Prince. A medical doctor, Dr. Duvalier probably was more popular with the 3½ million illiterate, poor masses than former tan or mulatto rulers who were often more French than the French.

Election 'Fix'

Normally, his six-year term would expire on May 15 (the Constitution provides only one term), but in April, 1961, he arranged for his election to a "new" six-year term, expiring in 1967. The United States and other nations have never recognized this illegal amendment to the Haitian laws, and as the 15th approached, various opposition groups have been threatening to overthrow the dictator.

As military law was imposed in the streets of Port-au-Prince around the Presidential Palace, tension grew in the past two weeks. Various murders were reported and Dr. Duvalier purged his small army of officers he did not trust. Some of the officers as well as other Haitians, sought political asylum in the Dominican Republic's Embassy.

Last Sunday, President Juan Bosch in Santo Domingo reported that Haitian police had entered the Dominican Embassy, and had stationed police on the grounds. He threatened war if Dr. Duvalier or the Organization of American States did not do something about this infringement of diplomatic immunity.

Inquiry Set Up

The OAS dispatched a mission to the island, and the guard was lifted from the Dominican Embassy, ending the immediate threat. Tension, however, is still high. The United States Ambassador Raymond Thurston last week advised all 1,000 Americans to stay indoors at night, and formally complained of harassment of an embassy official by Haitian police.

He is in contact with United States Navy ships a few miles off shore, in case a long-feared explosion breaks out in the city. Since almost all diplomats and foreigners are white, there is the chance that Dr. Duvalier may unleash his secret police on the foreigners in a sort of racial war—in an effort to cement his own shaky leadership.

Washington, which has virtually cut the dictatorship off from aid, despite the poverty of the people (per capita income averages $67 a year), would prefer a change of government, especially since Cuba is ominously across the passage. But most opposition leaders have long since been executed, jailed, or deported.
