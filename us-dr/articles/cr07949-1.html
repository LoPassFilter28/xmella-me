Broke Trujillo Ties

The Kennedy administration believes the United States got a black eye throughout Latin America for too-close association with Gen.. Trujillo, a generally hated hated oppressor.

Washington sought to remove this stigma by breaking diplomatic ties with the Trujillo government last year.

Consul General Henry Dearborn, after a period of communications blackout following the assassination Tuesday, was again able to reach Washington by telephone and telegraph today.

While this country has no diplomatic representation in the Dominican Republic, the consulate handles commercial and other such affairs for approximately 5,200 United States citizens there.

May Resume Aid

Some United States authorities foresaw a struggle for power, both within the Trujillo group and between it and the large elements of Dominican society persecuted during the dictator's 31-year rule. A continued harsh right-wing regime without Gen. Trujillo's personal leadership, they reasoned, would set off revolt—perhaps pro-Castro the next time.

The United States had several lures to attract Gen. Trujillo's successors down the democratic path, a main aim of Mr. Kennedy's "Alliance for Progress" Latin American program.

One is resumption of United States aid, cut off when Washington joined the other American republics last year in breaking diplomatic relations and normal commercial ties with Gen. Trujillo. Another is an increase in United States sugar purchases. The Dominican economy has been sagging.

The United States also could take a leading role in a move by the hemisphere organization, the Organization of American States, in readmitting a progressive Dominican regime to the American family.
