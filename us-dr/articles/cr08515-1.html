THE U. S. OCCUPATION OF HAITI

THE tenth session of the Popular Government League Forum recently held in Washington dealt with the American invasion and occupation of the Dominican Republic, which occupies the eastern two-thirds of the Island of Haiti. The island won its independence from France in 1804. Since 1884 the Dominican Republic has been recognized in international law as a free and sovereign State.

Hon. Horace G. Knowles, of New York, former minister to the Balkan states by appointment of Roosevelt, later as minister to Santo Domingo by appointment of President Taft, and later still to Bolivio, was the speaker of the occasion. Although Mr. Knowles' address was given in a calm, dispassionate manner, the Forum was moved to its depths. A mass of official documents at hand was read or offered to prove every statement he made. For more than an hour after the address two-thirds of the audience remained questioning him and discussing the various phases of the occupation.

Mr. Knowles declared that in repudiating the action of the government toward the Dominican Republic he considered himself serving his own country more than he was the Dominicans, on the grounds that the honor and pledged word of the United States was at stake in this issue, and the hour was at hand whether we would uphold the principles on which this nation was founded and which its people cherish, or ruthlessly disregard its obligations and become an imperialism comparable with the old monarchies of Europe.

The following is a condensed summary of the case as presented by Mr. Knowles:

The Invasion.

In May of 1916, when the Dominican Republic was at peace with the world, while it was a party to two existing treaties with the United States and in direct violation to one of them, and without the slightest attempt to appeal to or use diplomatic means or submitting any question in dispute to The Hague tribunal, President Wilson ordered a part of the United States Navy, without any notice to the Dominican Government, to go secretly to Santo Domingo to seize the government and proceed to subjugate the people. This was done without consulting or informing Congress, without a declaration of war.

American battleships entered the roadstead of Santo Domingo City, and under the cover of long range guns landed a force of marines and took possession. This was a paramount act of war. A little later the United States presented to the Dominican president, Francisco Henriquez y Carvajal, a treaty which completely destroyed the liberty, independence and sovereignty of his nation, putting it under the absolute control of the United States. It was an exact duplicate of the treaty which had just been forced upon the helpless Haitian government by guns and bayonets. President Henriquez refused to accept the demand, whereupon the American admiral, acting, of course, under orders from Washington, proceeded to use pressure and force. The military force was increased, and on November 29, 1916, nearly seven months after the invasion, a proclamation of occupation was issued and the admiral declared himself military governor of the country.

Wholesale Atrocities.

Following orders from Washington, the President and his ministers of state were forced out of office; the government treasury was seized; the national congress dismissed; elections prohibited; thousands of marines were spread over the country, with unlimited authority over the natives; public meetings were banned; a censorship of tongue, press, mail and telegraph of the severest kind was established; a reign of intense terror was inaugurated; destructive bombs were dropped from airplanes upon towns and hamlets; every home was searched for arms; homes were burned; natives were killed; tortures and cruelties were committed; "Butcher" Weiler's horrible concentration camps were established, and the brutal methods of Weiler, that did so much to bring about our war with Spain, were imitated.

Repressions and oppressions followed in succession. Persons making protest were fined and imprisoned; bullets and bayonets followed any attempt at resistance or defense. Those who criticized the action of the military government or used patriotic expressions were severely punished by fines and imprisonment. Hundreds of capable native government employes were dismissed, and their families distressed, and their places taken by men sent from the States. The Dominican treasury being tapped to pay all their traveling and incidental expenses. Their foreign indebtedness has been greatly increased, against their protest and in violation of the treaty of 1907. For five years this policy has continued. Thousands of natives have had their homes burned over their heads and in sheer terror have fled to the forests. Those of them who have attempted resistance in self-defense of their lives and their country have been designated "bandits" by the occupation.

Alleged Reasons.

The official reasons set up by the United States Government for this invasion of Santo Domingo were, first, violation of the Roosevelt-Dominican treaty of 1907, and second, to restore peace and order to the country.

Mr. Knowles denied that the Dominicans had violated the treaty, but that the United States had openly and flagrantly violated it. Even had the treaty been violated by the Dominicans and denied, the United States had not the right to set itself up as the judge of the point in dispute. And further, had there been such violation and no denial, the United States, by resolution which it offered and which was adopted by the Second International Peace Conference at The Hague in 1907, was precluded from using force in such occasion.

As to the second reason, Mr. Knowles made a number of startling disclosures. There was on revolution in the capital or disorder in the country as charged, but that the representatives of the United States were endeavoring to encourage and provoke a revolution. He produced documentary proof to show that the resignation of President Jimenez was caused by acts of government agents and further that deception was used in land troops. The United States claims that it landed troops for the purpose of guaranteeing a free and deliberate election of a new president by the Dominican Congress, but as a matter of fact it arrested Dominican congressmen and used armed force to compel them to vote for a president picked by the agents of the United States.

Further, that an attempt was made in the American Legation to bribe the Dominican Minister of War to use his influence and power with members of congress to force the election of a certain man for president.

Specifically, the treaty of 1907 was to provide for a loan of $20,000,000 and to protect the bondholders. There has been no default in the payment of interest and amortization, and never for a moment had the bondholders' interest been in danger. Contrariwise, the reports of the Navy Department show that overpayment has been made to such an extent that the entire loan will be paid in 1925, instead of 1958, or 33 years before it is due.

Mr. Knowles solemnly insisted that the life or property of any American had never been in danger, and throughout the history of the republic only one American has been killed in Santo Domingo, that by pure accident, for which the Dominicans paid the heirs $30,000.

Harding Follows Wilson.

Mr. Knowles showed that the subjugation of Santo Domingo followed promptly upon the like subjugation of the Republic of Haiti, which was described to the Forum at the previous session by Dr. Pierre Hudicourt, of Port au Prince, the whole being a campaign evidently designed for complete conquest of the whole island.
