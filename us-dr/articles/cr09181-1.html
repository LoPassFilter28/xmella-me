The Washington Daily News reported on the diplomatic elevation of missions between the United States and seven Caribbean and Central American nations, including the Dominican Republic and Haiti, from legations to full embassies. Under reciprocal agreements, the respective ministers were elevated to ambassadors, leaving Canada as the only Western Hemisphere mission remaining at ministerial rank. Minister Bids Minister to Party, but They Are Ambassadors on Meeting

By EVELYN PEYTON GORDON

The invitations were issued from "the Minister of Nicaragua and Mme. De Bayle in honor of the U. S. Minister to Nicaragua James B. Stewart." But by the time the guests arrived the legation had changed to an embassy and the Ministers were bona fide Ambassadors!

What a world in which we live! It all came about because the U. S. decided, with the five republics of Central America, Haiti, and the Dominican Republic, to exchange ambassadors instead of ministers. Soooo—the works went thru yesterday. James B. Stewart, one of the men longest in our own foreign service, was confirmed by the Senate. When he leaves shortly for Managua he will be the first ambassador in history to go from Washington to that capital.

And by reciprocal action youthful Leon De Bayle will soon present his credentials to the President as the first Nicaraguan Ambassador. The other day I remarked that a "H'ya, Mr. Ambassador" is a safer greeting these days than any lesser salutation. It's about true.

ONLY ONE MINISTER NOW

On this whole vast Western Hemisphere there remains only one diplomatic chief of mission who is of ministerial rank—Leighton McCarthy, the Canadian Minister. The representative of a dominion larger than the United States, with a population of 13,000,000 people, hasn't yet been elevated to top rank. He's outranked by 20 envoys from this side of the world!

Well, anyhow, all the envoys of the Latin American republics were on hand yesterday to congratulate their popular host and to receive good wishes themselves.

"No, I'm still just Maria Recinos to my friends," laughed pretty Senora de Recinos, wife of the Guatemalan Min—, no, Ambassador, when I greeted her as "Madame l'Ambassadrice."

The De Bayles have recently moved into the George Garrett house on New Hampshire-av, and a very excellent spot it is for an embassy. The house was decorated with huge sprays of fruit blossoms, and with pretty, little Mme. De Bayle, who has been named one of the best-dressed women in the Capital. She wore a floor-length frock of pale gray embroidered with white braid and fashioned with a short, long-sleeved jacket.

On the wall of the dining room hung a new portrait of the hostess—a beautiful painting done by a young American artist, George Quintas—picturing the lady in a bouffant gown of white net, holding in her hand a spray of the deep purple orchids she wears so frequently.

The De Bayles had what I consider a perfect wartime buffet. There were plenty of tiny hot finger rolls filled with sliced turkey. There were platters of hot fried shrimps with bowls of spicy tartar sauce. There were salted nuts and two big cakes. And then there was a bar. No frills and no folderol.

Hundreds of guests came to welcome Mr. Stewart and to congratulate the host. Our own Ambassador to Nicaragua, who was born in Philadelphia in 1882, started his career in 1915 when he went to Pernambuco, Brazil, as vice consul. Thence he went to Chihuahua, Mexico, and to Tampico, Mexico. He knows South and Central Americas well.

His tours of duty in Europe have been brief, but effective. He was Consul General in Budapest in 1933, and later was "exchanged" to the foreign service to become counselor of our legation there. Back to Mexico he went, and then to Zurich, Switzerland, from which place he was sent to Nicaragua last March, 1942.

James B. Stewart is sandy and ruddy in coloring. He's genial and well liked. His colleagues call him "a helluva good fellow," which means a lot in any business or profession. He's older than Leon De Bayle, who was first known to Washington, when he came here in 1937, as "Dr. Henri De Bayle's baby brother."

LONG REPRESENTED HERE

The De Bayle "tribe" has been representing Nicaragua in Washington for a good many years. First came "big brother" Luis, as charge d'affaires, in 1932. In 1933, with the Roosevelt dynasty, came brother Henri, and then young Leon.

A De Bayle without an M. D. after his name is almost a rarity. Leon De Bayle is a doctor. But a doctor of political science and economics rather than of medicine, which almost makes him an apostate. His father was a physician and both older brothers, but Leon's tastes ran to government and finance rather than anatomy and the cardio-vascular system.

I looked around the crowded embassy yesterday and saw all the distinguished representatives from south of the border. All of them were smiling. Hemispheric unity seems to be waiting only the decisions of Argentina. When he first came to Washington, Leon De Bayle thought no world war was in the offing. Yet even in those days he believed in a more closely knit America—North, South, and Central.

Yesterday, with a world war raging, the young envoy was happy that knitting had been so well achieved. It was a great day for the De Bayle family.
