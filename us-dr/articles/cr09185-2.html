"Our people must learn to wait. It is part of growing up," said one harrassed leader of the National Civic Union (UCN), the one-time opposition group of middle-class lawyers and doctors who now staff the new government.

Since the new government was installed during the last week in January, the UCN has changed from a national civic group to a mass political party. The seven members of the new State Council and their subordinates are only lieutenants in this group. But they have committed themselves to holding the fort until they resign next February after a new constitution has been drawn up and genuinely free elections are held in December.

All these democrats inside and outside the government have been tried in combat with the old Trujillo dynasty. Past suffering is their chief source of popular strength. Their claim to leadership rests on it. And they count on the continued "good sense" of the Dominican people who got rid of one of the worst dictatorships in Latin history with a minimum of bloodshed.

"But everyone seems to be in a big rush now," said Dr. Manuel Vaquero, secretary of the UCN. "No one wants to wait. People expect everything to change overnight." He and others told of fantastic claims for 400 per cent wage increases which—though justified in terms of living costs—cannot be met from near-empty coffers.

Statistics Tell Story

"How do we make people realize that the Trujillos left the country flat broke?" moaned another official. Statistics tell a woeful tale. An estimated 50 per cent of the million-man working force is unemployed. Books at the treasury have been rigged for so long no one is sure how much money is in circulation, how many millions the Trujillo family took out of the country with them, or how vast are their property holdings now in hands of a government receivership.

In such straits, the government and its supporters really rely on much more than the good will of the masses. They are counting on three main trump cards to see the new order through this painful "reconstruction" period:

1. Their own resolve to maintain law and order, even if temporary cost to their own popularity.

2. Quick economic recovery through well-prepared reforms.

3. All-out aid from the United States.

"Within legal limits we intend to put down everything which violates the public order," President Rafael Bonnelly said during an interview. "I and others have said this repeatedly on the radio, and we mean it." We spoke in the presidential palace where the State Council now presides.

Several days later the council issued a decree calling for deportation of "agitators and provocateurs" after proper legal investigation. Maximo Lopez Molina, chief Communist leader in the country, is currently being sought under this decree.

It was issued after gangs of terrorists had burned an estimated 245,000 tons of sugar cane (worth some $2.7 million) as it was being harvested in fields all over the island. The Dominican economy is based upon sugar, and this crop is absolutely vital for economic recovery.

Military Backs Leaders

In addition, the police have begun to crack down on gangs of anti-government youths. Recently pitched battles have raged around the Cafe Sublime, favorite hangout in the capital for organizing raids on city shops and the American consulate.

Government resolve seems to be strengthened by the conviction that the military stands firmly behind it. The Secretary for the Armed Forces is directly responsible to the new President. He has now moved his office into the presidential palace.

The feeling in Santo Domingo is that the young officers now taking over control in the military want to support constitutional government and will not meddle in politics, if—and it is a big if—the government keeps their oversized forces and privileges intact. For the moment, at least, the government appears to have little choice.

"Once the economy returns to normal, Communist subversion will not find fertile soil in the Dominican Republic," President Bonnelly said. "We hope to begin distributing the first land under the land reform in April," he added. "Everything is being planned now for the period of renovation."

This careful, step-by-step approach is the creed of the new order. It is the creed of basically conservative men with social consciences, men who believe in evolution, not revolution. Rather than drama, they want stability.

They have already raised the basic wage of sugar workers 100 per cent—to a minimum of $3.25 per day. These and other lesser wage increases—plus confiscation of the huge Trujillo holdings—are estimated to have redistributed 40 per cent of the wealth of the country already. An emergency public works program emphasizing manpower more than scarce materials is also making the first minor dent in widescale unemployment.

"We are open for foreign investment," affirmed Mr. Bonnelly, "and we want it. But that investment must benefit both the Dominican people and the investors. It will be channeled through our new planning commission."

U. S. Aid Important

It was a gentle—but clear—slap at the small trickle of North American "businessmen" who have arrived to speculate on the Dominican future. They check in at the best hotel in town and then pursue new officials with a high-pressure sales pitch.

But the new leaders are far more intent on how the United States Government lives up to its commitments of all-out aid. This potential aid is the third and by far the strongest trump in government hands. By general consensus the aid is essential to swift economic recovery. And that recovery, in turn, is essential for stable democratic government.

The stated North American aim is to build a "showcase of democracy" next to Castro's Cuba in the Caribbean. United States warships hastened the downfall of the Trujillo clan and their hangers-on last November. The United States took the lead in removing hemispheric economic and diplomatic sanctions in January, once the old order was completely removed.

Subsequently the Kennedy administration extended a $25 million long-term loan for emergency relief. The Dominican Republic has been readmitted to its share of the former Cuban sugar quota which at special prices in the United States should net another $25 million during the first half of 1962.

But that is just the beginning of United States involvement. A high-powered mission of the Agency for International Development has set up shop in the capital to help plan financing of long-range development. A new United States military mission has arrived to help train and educate the Dominican military for the new democratic order.

Some 50,000 simple Spanish-language pamphlets—"What is a Communist?"—already have gone through the United States Information Service office. Nine other similar titles in equal numbers are arriving. Ten distribution centers are being opened in the interior. Graphic documentary films on Castro's Cuba—one is entitled "The Eyewitness" — are shown in gatherings as varied as society clubs and workers meetings.

Battle Against Leftists

United States trade union officials — together with their democratic counterparts from Latin America — have helped set up a new trade union organization. This new "front" is fighting against a leftist-orientated group for the allegiance of some 100,000 workers now freed from the Trujillo-dominated Confederation of Dominican Workers.

An equally bitter struggle goes on at the autonomous University of Santo Domingo among some 4,000 unorganized students. Rebellion against the emerging order is greatest among youth. It is led by the "14th of June" group—a radical offshoot of the National Civic Union, and led by a young lawyer, Manuel Tavarez Justo.

Mr. Tavarez Justo and his followers identify themselves with Fidel Castro. They want the same "national solution" for the Dominican Republic as Mr. Castro has found for Cuba against "Yankee imperialism." They claim they are not Communist. Many refuse to believe Mr. Castro is a Communist. And their organization is a perfect cover for Communists who need to camouflage their real aims with nationalist labels.

In recent student elections at the university, the 14th of June group lost ground. Dominican student leaders who returned from exile in Costa Rica, Venezuela and Puerto Rico joined with home-trained Catholic action groups against the extremists.

Furthermore, all three candidates for new rector of the university are reputable democrats, and the government has recently called upon a special commission of the Organization of American States for assistance in reforming teaching methods.

The final battle is far from won—either among the students or elsewhere. The struggle for power can only gain in intensity in the months ahead. For all sides now know that the stakes go far beyond this small half an island perched between Cuba and Puerto Rico.

The United States and other democratic forces in the hemisphere are fully committed. Their prestige rides on the outcome.
