Ciudad Trujillo, Hemisphere's Oldest City, Is 450 Years Old

CIUDAD TRUJILLO, Dominican Republic—The oldest city in the western hemisphere, Ciudad Trujillo in the Dominican Republic, will celebrate its 450th anniversary on Sunday, August 4, when a three-masted caravel, modeled after one of Columbus's ships, sails up the Ozama river to reenact the founding of western civilization's first permanent site.

Wearing late 15th century costumes, sailors will moor the ship to a replica of the tree used by Columbus's brother, Bartolome, when he founded the city of Santo Domingo in 1496. The ceremony will open with a colorful water pageant featuring flower decked floats and will be highlighted with an address by Dr Rafael Trujillo, president of the Dominican Republic.

Thousands on thousands of visitors are pouring in on this city, oldest in the New World, to attend the colorful ceremonies that will accompany the observation of the 450th anniversary of the founding of the western hemisphere's first permanent site.

Many are coming by plane, for the city is an aerial crossroads of the continents, being directly in the line of route between North and South America. Others are coming by sea from New York, Miami, New Orleans and other points.

Beside the dramatic reenactment of Columbus's landing in the New World, a highlight of the celebration will be the display of church treasures worth $5,000,000 in a procession led by Msgr Ricardo Archbishop Pittini archbishop of Santo Domingo and primate of the West Indies. Included in this exhibition will be the invaluable altar bell made by the famous artist and designer, Benvenuto Cellini. The original cross planted by Columbus on Dominican soil after the battle of Santo Cerro will also be in the display.

Accompanied by other church dignitaries, Archbishop Pittini will bless the placque placed at a replica of the Bartolome tree, where Bartolome Columbus tied his vessel on landing to establish the city in 1496.

City Is Renamed

Originally called Santo Domingo, the people of the city renamed it after President Trujillo because his government rebuilt it along modern lines after a hurricane in 1930 had completely demolished the city.

Two million people in the Dominican Republic who will celebrate the anniversary have a deep pride in their capital city's long history but they are even prouder of its modernity and up-to-dateness. Although the city has the moss-covered ruins of some of the first buildings in America, it also has modern structures, spacious boulevards, suspension bridges and some of the finest hospitals and schools in the world.

Ruins of the first hospital built in the Americas are a short distance from the sun decks of the city's ultra-modern tuberculosis hospital. The vine-covered arch of the first monastery in America offers an exciting contrast to the clean-cut, modernistic verandas of the Hotel Jaragua, reputed to be the Hotel Jaragua, rebetween Miami and Rio de Janiero. The Cathedral of Santo Domingo, another "first" in the western hemisphere, is close to a long row of modern government buildings.
