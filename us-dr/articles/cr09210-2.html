Original in English.Original en inglés.

Ruins in Ciudad Trujillo are the work of centuries, but modern improvements are the work of 16 years spent in office by the Trujillo government. When he was inaugurated in 1930, President Trujillo found the country in a state of political and economic collapse. A tragic touch came two weeks after the inauguration when a hurricane turned the capital city into a mass of rubble. At the time, three-fourths of the population could not read or write. Many of the principal streets of the capital were unpaved. Agriculture and industry were languishing under a moral as well as economic depression.

Trujillo's Accomplishments

Yet the task of modernizing the birthplace of the New World was accomplished in the brief span of 16 years without once resorting to foreign loans. Illiteracy dropped 20 per cent due to the building of hundreds of new schools and the allotment of 13 per cent of the national budget to furthering education. Beautifully paved roads and 70 new bridges put formerly isolated regions into easy communication with one another and with the capital. Vast irrigation projects brought 85,000 acres of land under cultivation. Factories employ twice as many people and produce three times as many exportable items as previously. New hospitals, clothing manufacture, water works, schools and public buildings transformed an ancient city of ruins into a modern metropolis. Little wonder that a grateful people over-rode the protests of their president and gave Santo Domingo the name it bears today—Ciudad Trujillo.

Home of Columbus

The Dominican Republic occupies the eastern three-quarters of the island of Santo Domingo—an area slightly larger than Kentucky. Ciudad Trujillo, its capital, straddles the mouth of the Ozama river and is only four hours by air from Miami. The city threatens to rival Rio de Janiero, Havana and Miami as a year-round playground for American tourists. Among the attractions are the delightful climate, picturesque ruins, fishing and swimming and the luxurious resort grounds of the American operated Hotel Jaragua.

The first tourist of them all, Columbus, spent so much time there instead of looking for new lands that when he left Spain on his fourth and last voyage, the king and queen ordered him not to land at Santo Domingo. But he did, in spite of the royal decree.

Springboard for Adventure

Columbus's son, Diego. became viceregent of the island and built a magnificent palace at Santo Domingo whose ruins still stand today. He gathered around him one of the most brilliant courts of the time—nobles seeking gold, scientists and explorers and rich, enterprising merchants. They made the city the glittering capital of the New World and the springboard from which new conquests were launched. Nunez Balboa, who found the Pacific, and De Soto, discoverer of the Mississippi, began their adventures there. So too, did Ponce de Leon, who searched for the Fountain of Youth and found Florida. Both Francisco Pizzarro and Hernan Cortez lived in the city.

But the prosperous days of Santo Domingo were short lived. The discovery of enormous wealth in Mexico and Peru soon drained the city of its importance and brilliance. In 1586 Sir Frances Drake sacked and burned the city with such violence that the scars of his plundering still remain on the walls of the Cathedral. This was the first of many invasions that turned Spain's brightest colony into a mere pawn in the game of colonial conquest.

Columbus Remains in City

In 1795, when Spain ceded the eastern portion of the island to France, the priests of the city removed the ashes of Christopher Columbus from the Cathedral to Havana. But in the darkness the priests made a fortunate blunder. They opened the wrong crypt. The coffin taken to Havana and later to Spain did not contain the remains of Columbus, but those of his son, Diego. The ashes of the great admiral were found later in a crypt where records showed they had always been. They now rest in a magnificent tomb in the Cathedral at Ciudad Trujillo awaiting transfer to the Columbus Memorial lighthouse, which is soon to be built on a high promontory just outside the city.

The memorial will be a mile long, shaped like a Christian cross and will serve as a beacon to both planes and ships. When completed the huge recumbent cross will be, in the words of President Trujillo: " . . . a symbol of international harmony through the supreme law of love, without which happy fraternity among the nations cannot be achieved." The memorial will also be remembrance of Columbus himself, who first brought Christianity to the New World when he landed on an island he described as, "no finer land under heaven."
