HAITI'S FUTURE MAY DEPEND ON THE OUTCOME OF THE HISPANO-AMERICAN WAR. Bulldozing Germans Waiting for an Opportunity to Wrest the Place from Its Black Owners, CONCESSION TO AMERICANS THAT MAY INVOLVE THE LITTLE REPUBLIC IN NO END OF TROUBLE. Europeans, and Especially Germans, Will Expect Same Commercial Privileges as Yankee Traders. MANY CUBANS NOW IN HAITI SEEM TO CARE LITTLE ABOUT WHAT IS GOING ON AT HOME. Minister Powell's Warning to the Haitian Administration—Interview with Brutus St. Victor.

(Copyright, 1898, by the Associated Press.)

PORT-AU-PRINCE, May 8.—German war ships some time ago made soundings in the principal ports of Haiti—Port-au-Prince, the Mole St. Nicholas, Cape Haiti, St. Marie, Jeremie and Jacmel. What Germany might undertake to do in this part of the world, in the event of a general conflict of the nations, a dread of which Lord Salisbury is said to have expressed in a recent speech, is a question that is occupying the attention of the public men of Haiti. Well-informed observers residing here do not doubt that if America were involved on one side, Germany would send a fleet to seize the island. German traders fervently cherish this hope. Their feeling against the Haitians since the Luders incident has been intense and, so far as a volatile people is capable of cherishing old scores, the animosity is reciprocal.

Of all the masters of vessels coming to Haitian ports, the Germans treat the native officials and stevedores with the least consideration, often with unconcealed contempt. The wonder is that their attitude does not oftener provoke conflicts. If you walk the streets here and a lower class native hurls after you a certain epithet, you may be sure it is because of the ill will towards the Germans and not on general principles. Yet the Germans are by far the most numerous class of traders here. It may be that this has not a little to do with the action of the government in conceding the amendment of Article 5 of the treaty with the United States, about which I cabled you this week, granting to Americans the same commercial rights as that enjoyed by the Haitians. However this may be, it is certain that the concession will involve the Haitian government in vexatious difficulties with other nations, unless it does the same by them. Whether M. Brutus St. Victor, the able minister of foreign affairs, has looked so far ahead as this I cannot say, though it is very reasonable to assume that he has.

By the concession to the Americans, Haiti loses about $50,000 in gold a year in revenue. In order to afford to treat other foreigners with like liberality, she must devise new taxation. It looks very much as if, counting on the protection of the United States in case of need, the foreign minister expected to refuse an equal concession to other nations, on the ground that the treaty of 1864 is purely reciprocal, each party making to the other a specific return for specific privileges granted. But such an excuse is likely to have little force if the West India waters become the principal naval battlefield in a general war.

WAR ECHOES FROM HAITI.

Cuban Refugees—Minister Powell's Warning to the Government.

(Copyright, 1898, by the Associated Press.)

PORT-AU-PRINCE, Haiti, May 8.—It is estimated that there are about 1,000 Cuban refugees in Haiti, the great majority, of course, in this city. A singular absence of enthusiasm among them over the intervention of America in behalf of their people has been remarked. If hope is at the fever point they do not show it. At one special resort in Port-au-Prince Cubans of nearly every class congregate in the evening, drink, smoke, play cards, sing and talk of the war. They evince by far less excitement over the hostilities than do the Haitians, who group themselves daily, a little before and a little after the hour of the siesta, in front of the restaurant De la Paix and the Hotel Central and divide their attention between the movements of the gold premium and the achievements, actual and prospective, of the Americans. The wildest and strangest and most absurd rumors find ready currency in the city. Not one in ten, when traced anywhere near its source, can readily be made to have a serious foundation. Whether the Cubans invent the majority of these, in competition with the Spaniards at Havana, it is impossible to say.

The chairman of the Cuban Junto in Haiti is Dr. J. E. Nunez, who declares himself an American citizen. Another leader of the Junto is Dr. E. Heraux. Dr. Nunez is seriously considering the suggestion that if the Cubans in Haiti would secretly organize a force to join the insurgents the United States would undoubtedly send arms and munitions down to some convenient point of the eastern end of Cuba for their use. They could pass from here to Cuba in a sailing vessel without much danger of capture. A recent rumor that a Spanish cruiser had taken an American three-master off the Mole St. Nicholas was received in Port-au-Prince with absolute incredulity, and thus far there has been no confirmation of it. It is hardly believed that any single Spanish war-ship would venture within reaching distance of the American squadron in the West Indies.

About a week ago a former Haitian general, Dorsainville, called on the American minister and offered to raise a battalion of 500 men to serve under the American flag in Cuba. He was informed that the American government would not authorize or sanction the recruiting of an armed force in a foreign country, as that would be a flagrant violation of international law. Of course, to equip and provision such a force after it had landed in Spanish territory would be a different thing.

The report that the United States government has negotiated with the Haitian government for the purchase of the gunboat Crete A. Pierrot, the finest vessel in the latter's navy, has been persistently circulated. Admiral Killeck, the highest naval officer of Haiti, told the correspondent of the Associated Press yesterday that it was true, and that the Haitian government had refused the offer made to it for the express reason that it could not spare the vessel. The Crete A. Pierrot, named after one of Haiti's famous battlefields, is built on handsome lines, and when she is in the harbor always attracts more than any other Haitian war ship the attention of strangers. She has an imposing ram and her armament is excellent, her guns being of the latest type. She has an entire complement of rapid-fire ordnance. With only one boiler, she is said to make sixteen knots an hour. She has a triple expansion engine, and, with a double boiler, which could be put into her at short notice, could make, her engineer declares, about twenty knots an hour. Her officers would like to serve in her under the American flag. Her second officer is an American, who was in the federal navy during the civil war.

A Haitian deputy, who professes to be well informed on all political matters, said yesterday that the government was certain to favor the elimination of Article 6 from the Constitution at the session of the Chambers, which will probably begin this week. The correspondent of the Associated Press derived from Brutus St. Victor, the secretary of state for foreign relations, an entirely different view of the parliamentary programme. Mr. St. Victor denied categorically that the government contemplated any project for the removal of the prohibition of alien ownership of real property. Such a step, he said, if ever taken, must be yet a very great way off. "The people," he continued, "are not ready to accept such a change. There must be a gradual preparation of public sentiment before the government could even suggest it. There is, no doubt, such an opinion favorable to it, but it has as yet manifested itself chiefly in the cities, and it must be remembered that the mass of the Haitian population is rural, and that it is strongly wedded to national traditions."

Mr. St. Victor would not speak definitely as to the means which the government intended to take to check the increase of the premium on paper money; but he admitted that this was, perhaps, the most urgent business which would claim the attention of the chambers. He mentioned the contract recently entered into by the government with the Bank of Haiti to burn publicly every month a certain percentage of the paper money received in payment of customs duties. This would reduce the volume of paper currency quite rapidly. "The real chief cause of the premium on gold, however," said Mr. St. Victor, "is the low price of coffee. It has begun to rise again, and it is hoped will be restored in a few months to its normal value."

The correspondent asked the foreign minister whether the stories which had been printed about the distressed condition of the people in the interior had been exaggerated. He replied that among the country people there was some distress, but no actual want of the necessaries of life. Nobody could starve in the interior of Haiti who was industrious enough to scratch the soil ever so little by way of cultivation. It was in the cities that the real misery existed, and there was danger that the Hispano-American war would render it worse. The city folk could not live without money as the country people could. They had been dependent for many cheap articles of food upon the American trade. This supply had been largely reduced, and with the premium and prices generally going up, the small wage earners were threatened with dire need.

Mr. St. Victor also said that Haiti was resolved to maintain the strictest neutrality as between Spain and America, but, when questioned as to the special privileges granted to American ships of war in the treaty of 1864, he evaded a direct answer. He confirmed Minister Powell's statement of a few days ago to the Associated Press correspondent, to the effect that Americans and Haitians in this country were hereafter to be upon an equal commercial footing. Whatever view critics may take on the policy of the American government in persisting in sending men of color to represent it in the island of Haiti, they will not be able to find fault with the present minister at Port-au-Prince, William F. Powell, on account of a lack of energy. A man of seemingly very deliberate action and of extremely quiet manners, he yet seems to have made a noteworthy impression on the powers that rule at either end of the island.

These dispatches have already called attention to the steps which Mr. Powell has taken, in pursuance of instructions from Washington, to commit the Haitian government to the literal fulfillment of all the provisions of the treaty of 1864 with the United States, which give to that country in time of war special advantages which cannot be enjoyed by other nations and also the article which purports to place Americans on an equal footing with Haitians as to the privileges of local trade. The former achievement was less difficult than the latter, which follows some twenty years of unsuccessful effort in the same direction, beginning at the time when Hamilton Fish was secretary of state. It is easy to believe, however, that the increased martial prestige of America, consequent upon that country's going to war with Spain for the deliverance of a west Indian people from oppression, has impressed Haitian statesmen and the Haitians in general much more effectively than any amount of forcible diplomacy could have done. Mr. Powell, nevertheless, so far as one can discern at this moment, deserves full credit for having "risen to the height of the occasion." His language to the minister of the exterior on the treaty obligations has been of no uncertain meaning. He has several times strongly stated that the fate of Haiti as a nation depended in a large degree upon the attitude of the government towards America during the present war. The Haitian administration already realized this fact, but in an alternative

However virile and worthy of respect may be the present disposition of Brutus St. Victor, the foreign minister, President Sam himself is inclined to choose the less decisive course whenever the other presents a chance of danger. It was not difficult, however, to convince the Haitian government that to provoke resentment on the part of the American people in the present emergency would be to jeopardize the benevolent friendship which they have exhibited towards the former since the days of Dessalines. The Haitians are rightly indifferent to the friendship of the Spaniards, though they have some fear of reprisal upon the part of the latter if the special privileges accorded to Americans should be so freely used as to provoke a declaration that they contravene the laws of neutrality. To cut short any possible period of vacillation on the part of the Haitian government it was necessary for the American minister to assume a tone which had been rarely, if ever, used by his predecessors. He caused the government to understand that now was the opportunity to assure forever the substantial support of the United States or to invite their lasting aversion and enmity.

Another negotiation has been in progress at Santo Domingo for some time. It involves the project of a treaty between the United States and the Dominican republic granting to the former even a broader preference over other nations than accorded in the Haitian treaty. One of its articles has a political significance which will astonish and may dismay some of the Europeans, who are anxious to achieve something of consequence in this island. The correspondent hopes soon to be at liberty to be more explicit on this subject.

Simon Sam’s Birthday.

(Copyright, 1898, by the Associated Press.)

PORT-AU-PRINCE, Haiti, May 15.—The second anniversary of the election of Gen. Tiresias Simon Sam as president of the republic of Haiti, was celebrated here to-day. There was a brilliant review of troops and there was an imposing religious ceremony at the cathedral, where a Te Deum was chanted. All the members of the diplomatic corps were present. President Sam made a tour of the city, which was in holiday attire, and was warmly acclaimed by the population.
