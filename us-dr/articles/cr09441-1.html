Rayburn Squelches Bid To Senators in Probe

Speaker Rayburn, Democrat of Texas, said today a House investigating committee will not invite testimony from Senators whose names have come into an inquiry into alleged backstage maneuvering over a Miami TV license contest.

"We're not going to ask them to come over here and if I were the Senators I wouldn't come voluntarily," Mr. Rayburn told newsmen.

"If a Senate committee asked me to go over there I would tell them to go dig potatoes deep."

Mr. Rayburn said the Senators whose names have been mentioned during the House hearings have a forum of their own in which to testify if they wish.

"They'v egot a floor over there and they've got committees," he said. "We're not going to investigatge Senators."

Possibility Squelched

Mr. Rayburn's pronouncement, as head of the House, squelched whatever possibility there may have been that the House Legislative Oversight Subcommittee testimony from Senators Kefauver, Democrat of Tennessee; Holland, Democrat of Florida, and Smathers, Democrat of Florida.

These three Senators have figured in testimony about a four-way contest for Miami's TV channel 10.

The committee's digging into this case, as part of a general investigation of the Federal Communications Commission, led to the resignation of FCC Commissioner Richard A. Mack.

Mr. Mack protested he had done no wrong but said his usefulness as commissioner had been ended.

The three Senators have all said they were willing to testify—if invited by the House Committee.

Next Big Name Witness

In recess today, the committee's prospective next big name witness is Col. George Gordon Moore, a brother-in-law of Mrs. Eisenhower.

The House investigators are increasingly curious about Col. Moore's Dominican Republic business ventures with an official of a Miami television company.

The subcommittee in recess until Monday while a newly appointed chief counsel prepares to start work, made clear that Col. Moore will have a sion, Inc., get the controversial Miami Channel 10 award from the FCC last year.

Col. Moore denied in a letter to Subcommittee Chairman Harris yesterday that he had anything to do with helping National Airlines or its subsidiary, Public Service Television, Inc., get the highly coveted and controversial Miami Channel 10 award from the FCC last year.

Two Claim Information

But Democratic Representatives Mack of Illinois and Williams of Mississippi said they had information—strictly unconfirmed—concerning reported business dealings between Col. Moore and George W. Gibbs, jr., of Miami, a National Airlines director and a Public Service Television vice president.

This information, Mr. Williams said in questioning George T. Baker, National Airlines president, involved Generalissimo Rafael L. Trujillo, Dominican Republic strong man, and stock he reportedly gave to Mr. Gibbs in a business project in which Col. Moore also might have been concerned.

To Seek Relevancy

Mr. Williams emphasized that the reports might be inaccurate. He nevertheless insisted that the subcommittee should explore them for possible leads in the Channel 10 case and interrogate Col. Moore.

Chairman Harris said the subcommittee would try to determine whether the reports bear any relevancy to the investigation of improper influence or interference in the FCC's action in the Channel 10 case.

In any event, Mr. Harris told reporters, Col. Moore's letter showed willingness to testify under oath that he never "engineered" the National Airlines application before the FCC.

Two Defend Moore

Mr. Baker and Alexander G. Hardy, National Airlines senior vice president, who concluded their testimony yesterday, denied that Col. Moore had any connection with the National Airlines application for the TV license. They also maintained they knew nothing substantial about Col. Moore's business dealings with Mr. Gibbs.

Mr. Baker explained he had heard that the two men had been engaged in a shipyard project in the Dominican Republic. He added that he did not know whether a shipyard was built or whether it amounted to anything.

The subcommittee had planned a comparatively calm session yesterday after rearranging its schedule to postpone until next Wednesday the reappearance of Richard A. Mack who resigned under fire this week as an FCC commissioner. Just before the subcommittee quit for the day, it had held morning and afternoon open hearings, two closed sessions, and tried unsuccessfully to get an agreement on whether to send formal invitations to the Senators mentioned in previous testimony.

Choose New Counsel

The members did agree unanimously on selecting a chief counsel to succeed Dr. Schwartz. He is Robert W. Lishman, a 54-year-old Washington attorney with extensive experience in governmental and private practice of law, particularly in administrative, utility and regulatory problems.

Senator Kefauver wrote recently and stated again yesterday that he exerted no improper influence in behalf of Col. Frank Katzentine in the Channel 10 case. Similar statements were made by Senators Holland and Smathers. All three have expressed willingness to testify if the subcommittee so requests.

Tells of 1954 Letter

Senator Smathers, Democrat of Florida, disclosed today that in February, 1954, he notified an applicant for the Miami Channel 10, that he never would intercede with the FCC. He insisted that he has maintained that policy and never tried to pressure former Commissioner Mack or anyone else.

The 1954 letter to the late L. B. Wilson, one of three unsuccessful applicants for Channel 10, was forwarded to the House subcommittee. It was in addition to the longer letter in which Senator Smathers explained in detail how he indorsed Mr. Mack for appointment in 1955 by President Eisenhower. He said he mentioned the Channel 10 matter on only two occasions in 1955 and 1956 and then only casually without seeking any commitment.

Senator Smathers denied what he described as "irresponsible and generally false" allegations by Mr. Baker that he had tried to prevent the FCC from giving Channel 10 to the National Airlines subsidiary.

Request Disclosed

The letter to Mr. Wilson disclosed that Senator Smathers asked Mr. Wilson to remake a will which had named him coexecutor. Senator Smathers made this request when he learned that Mr. Wilson was an applicant for Channel 10 in 1954.

After the death of Mr. Wilson, Senator Smathers said he learned that the will had not been revised and that he was still a coexecutor. He recalled that in the 1954 letter he specifically informed Mr. Wilson that:

"It has been in the past and will continue to be my policy to take no part whatsoever in any such contests (before the FCC) since the law invests in the FCC, a quasi-judicial body not only the right and duty but the sole responsibility to determine which applicant can best serve public need and necessity."

Explains Differences

As for Mr. Baker's charges, Senator Smathers said his differences with the National Airlines president go back 10 years and originated at the time of a strike against the company. He explained also that he long has felt that, in general, an airline company should not be given a TV station license. He insisted, however, that he made no attempt to persuade Mr. Mack or anyone else to vote against the National Airlines application.

"Mr. Baker," Senator Smathers charged, "is a desperate and troubled man and while anxious to hang on to some of his possessions he is regrettably willing to smear and besmirch without regard for fact, truth, or conscience."

A letter from Senator Magnuson, Democrat of Washington, also was received by Mr. Harris yesterday. He did not release the text. He said it was previously published and contained an account of Senator Magnuson's attitude in 1956 and 1957 concerning the policy problem of an airline applying for a TV station license.
