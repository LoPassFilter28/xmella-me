The column recalls the nation's colonial origin, Columbus's arrival nearly 500 years earlier, and a brief return to Spanish rule during the 1860s under Queen Isabella II. Dominican Republic Party Celebrates Its 100th Anniversary

By EVELYN PEYTON GORDON

I know now what is the favorite flower of the beautiful Mrs. Joseph E. Davies—red roses. At least that is the flower which she and her former-ambassador husband send to friends on congratulatory occasions.

A huge vase of long stemmed red roses bloomed in the Dominican Embassy yesterday and attached to them was the card of Mr. and Mrs. Davies who are "rusticating" in Palm Beach this winter. Mr. Davies used to represent the Dominican Republic and yesterday was the 100th anniversary of the island republic.

The red roses didn't bloom alone. There were masses of other flowers all about the rooms of the big mansion on 16th-st which now houses the embassy. It was once the home of a local movie-house king, and boasts among other assets a private tennis court.

It was the first party given by the first ambassador of the Dominican Republic—San Domingo, if you belong to a former generation. Senor Copello, the incumbent envoy, is a businessman from Santiago—a beautiful city high in the hills among the mahogany forests—whose fortunes are derived from tobacco. And, a a prominent citizen of the Dominican Republic, he found many old friends on hand to greet him—men who had known him years ago: Gen. Dion Williams who had lived in San Domingo long before it was called the Dominican Republic; Maj. Gen. Holcomb, former commandant of the Marine Corps, and a group of State Department officials who had been there over a period of years.

Back in 1844, San Domingo had just won its freedom from Haiti, which seems strange, since the latter occupies only one-third of the island and boasts no such riches as the Dominican Republic. Previously it had been a Spanish colony. Remember please, that Christopher Columbus found the island nearly 500 years ago on his second voyage of discovery.

It's a garden spot, that Dominican Republic. A country of tropical flowers, sea and mountains which are cool and lofty. In the 1860s, while we were engrossed in a war between the states, a certain leader of San Domingo thought it would be a good idea to belong to the mother country again. That was in the reign of Isabella II—not to be confused with the Isabella who financed Columbus. But that didn't last long.

Just before World War II cut off tourist trade, the Dominican Republic was becoming quite a winter resort, not yet discovered by too many tourists. There is to be found the oldest cathedral on this side of the world. And in that cathedral repose the bones of Columbus—despite some claims of Cuba and Spain that the bones are in their cathedrals. Anyhow, there still stands the palace in which Bartolomeo Columbus, brother of Christopher, resided as governor of San Domingo.

It was Generalissimo Trujillo, president and "benefactor" of San Domingo, who changed the ancient name of the capital city, calling it after himself—Ciudad Trujillo. And in that city, for a week, celebrations have been gala. Today his envoy to Washington—like those of other Latin American republics—is an ambassador.

Diplomats from most nations of the world were on hand to offer congratulations—the dean of the corps, Peruvian Ambassador Manuel de Freye y Santander; Soviet Ambassador Andrei Gromyko, whom I have discovered has a sympathetic twinkle in his somber eyes; bluff Ambassador Leighton McCarthy of Canada, and the Chief of Protocol, dapper George T. Summerlin.

Cuban Ambassador Concheso introduced a compatriot to Mexican Ambassador Castillo Najera: "My brother from our sister republic of Mexico," he said in Spanish. I could only think of Gilbert and Sullivan "his sisters and his cousins and he had them by the dozens; his sisters and his brothers and his aunts!"

There was Finnish Minister Hjalmar Procope and his pretty English wife; the Swedish Minister and Mme. Bostrom—the only two envoys of ministerial rank I saw in that gathering of ambassadors. But—the hosts? Yes, they're an asset to our Capital. Senora de Copello is tall and handsome and there are two pretty young daughters ready to take their place in society. They're all chic and they all speak English.

"I wish I knew basic English," said Mrs. Leighton McCarthy. Well, one doesn't need it in Washington. Everybody who comes here learns "American" in two shakes. Even His Britannic Majesty's attachés have been known to carol "Howya doin'" at the end of two weeks' stay!
