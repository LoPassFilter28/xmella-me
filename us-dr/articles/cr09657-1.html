Gen. Roderiguez Slain In Puerto Plata Fight Leader of Revolutionary Forces Meet Death at End of Three Days' Battle—Sought the Presidency.

CAPE HATIEN, Jan. 4.—General Roderiquez, the revolution leader in Santo Domingo, has been killed at the end of three days of fierce hand-to-hand fighting in the outskirts of Puerto Plata. This information was received this morning by the Dominican minister, who called at the State Department.

General Roderiquez had proclaimed his intention of making himself president of Santo Domingo in case he should emerge from the conflict in which the odds were so overwhelmingly against him.

The Dominican minister considers the situation in Santo Domingo as exceedingly alarming. Although he discussed the situation at some length with officials of the State Department it is not known if he made any request for interference on the part of this Government.

Wire communication throughout the republic of anto Domingo is badly crippled, according to dispatches that reached the State Department this morning from its representatives there. All service in that part of the island is niterrupted except between Santo Domingo city and Puerto Plata, and a delay of as much as twenty-four hours is being experienced on that line.

This will greatly effect communication with the South American country over the French cable, about which there has arisen international complications between Venezuela and France, is relayed through the present seat of hostilities.

Cespedes Defeated Rodriguez's Troops

PORT AU PRINCE, Haiti, Jan. 4.—Reports received today from Santo Domingo state that Gen. Demetrio Rodriguez, who commanded the Morales troops in the fighting which has occurred near Porto Plata, has been killed. Rodriguez had announced that if his attack on Porto Plata proved successful he would set himself up as a candidate for President of Santo Domingo.

The Morales troops were repulsed in their attack on Porto Plata by the Caceres forces under General Cespedes. Later Cespedes attacked General Rodriguez's position outside Porto Plata. Many men were killed and wounded on both sides, and the battle resulted in the defeat of the Morales troops.

General Rodriguez, whose death is announced, was a prominent factor for years in San Domingan affairs. He was educated at Fordham's College, New York. He was considered to be one of Morales' most formidable opponents, but after the last revolution laid down his arms when promised he would be allowed to collect the customs and other revenues in the province of Monte Christi.

Before the Senate attempts seriously to approach final action on the Santo Domingo treaty, there will be a determined effort to secure more information than is now possessed, either by the State Department or the Committee on Foreign Relations, concerning the merits and facts of the present outbreak in the island.

It is supposed that reports are now in process of preparation, or are on the way from the island, containing the full exposition by Minister Dawson of the conditions which led up to the remarkable and unexpected action of President Morales. That action is still without satisfactory explanation, and friends of the Administration take some comfort in pointing out that the very fact of its being unexpected, and in no wise guarded against, disproves the claims that the Washington Government was practically dominating the administration of the island.

Powerful Argument.

The most important and powerful argument in favor of the treaty and one which is said to have been making itself felt recently through various channels, is that of Americans who are looking to the island as a promising field for investment. It turns out, according to people who have recently been in the island, that a large amount of the best land there has been optioned to Americans. Whether they will finally close their deals depends, it is said, mainly on whether the treaty is ratified.

These prospective developers of the island's resources have no design of undertaking such an effort, and investing their money there, unless they are assured of the stable conditions that would ensue upon the ratification of the treaty. If it goes through, millions, it is said, will very soon be invested in the wonderfully rich sugar and tobacco lands of the islands, but if it fails, the whole effort will be dropped.

Seeing fortunes in prospect, these interests are reported getting busy to help secure ratification of the treaty. They are likely to prove a most useful assistance to the Administration.
