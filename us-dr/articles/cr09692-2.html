SAN DOMINGO. Jiminez Wants Another Term as President. WAS ONCE DRIVEN AWAY. Some Light on Conditions in the Turbulent Island.

(Washington Star.)

The ever tempestuous island of San Domingo is again attracting the attention of the American press. A cablegram from Berlin of recent date says that according to the Hamburgisher correspondent (the organ of the Bismarckian party), an American lately arrived in Germany to make preliminary arrangements for a filibustering expedition to Haiti and Santo Domingo, with the object of promoting a civil war that would give a pretense for armed interference on the part of the United States. This supposed expedition is described as being no way different from the Jamieson raid on the former Boer republic.

ATTITUDE OF THE UNITED STATES.

About the same time the Dominican government in its efforts to establish neutral ports brought out a strong protest from the United States. The Dominican government was notified that the United States would not tolerate the making of certain ports free; that the United States would not allow the establishment of any neutral ports, or the concession of any portion of territory to European power; that the United States would not permit any nation to make exclusive use of Dominican waters in time of peace, much less in time of war, or would the United States allow any portion of the Dominican territory to be classed as neutral.

Shortly after this came stories of a new revolution, and the turning back of American and Cuban ships at Porta Plata. This was followed by the dispatch of the Baltimore to Santo Domingo. The telegrams of later date state that all the northern part of the island is in revolt against the government; that Gen. Augustus Morales, a participant in many uprisings, is principally at the head of the revolution. A remarkable phase in this uprising seems to be that the parties of Gen. Juan I. Jiminez and Gen. Horatio Vasquez will be united in the revolution against Alexandro Wos y Gil, formerly a genreal under Jiminez.

JIMINEZ'S ATTEMPT IN 1898.

The family of Jiminez is very prominent in the affairs of Santo Domingo. The father of the present general and ex-president was the first president of the republic, in 1849. The grandfather was put to death along in the thirties for taking part in a revolution to secure the independence of the people. Jiminez himself made an unsuccessful revolution against Santo Domingo in 1898. Prior to that time for eight years he had been an exile.

While he was a man of wealth and large property interests in Santo Domingo, his political aspirations were so much feared by the former president that he was not permitted to remain within the confines of the government. These eight years were spent in efforts to organize revolutions against Santo Domingo at various commercial centers of the world, principally Hamburg, Paris, New York and Havana.

The first revolutionary party in 1898 sailed from Mobile, Ala., under most romantic circumstances, and the true story of that expedition has never been disclosed by those who were with it. The U. S. S. Finita was equipped with arms, men and ammunition at Mobile, under the American flag, ostensibly for an attack on the Spaniards in Cuba. Jiminez went under the name of Rodriguez. Mr. Meiklejohn was assistant secretary of war. The Finita did not go to Cuba, but instead went to Santo Domingo, and the question has always been whether Jiminez ran away with the Finita or whether Mr. Meiklejohn in the war with Spain was attempting to capture Santo Domingo, in order to use one of its splendid harbors at Samana bay.

The Finita landed at three o'clock in the morning at Monte Christi, but a cablegram had been intercepted and Jiminez was not expected. He landed with eleven men, and, proceeding to the home of the governor, called upon him to surrender. This the governor did. He was taken to the fort, and commanded, under penalty of death, to order the garrison, to surrender. Instead of this he ordered the garrison to open fire, and Jiminez's party fled in disorder. Of the party Jiminez alone escaped. Seven were killed outright and four were found concealed under buildings, more or less wounded. Jiminez escaped by a ruse. A wide salt marsh lies in front of Monte Christi. Crossing this to the wharf runs a narrow road. Jiminez jumped upon a horse he found tied, and, calling out that he would bring up reinforcements, dashed down the narrow path. After waiting at the boat a few moments for some of his companions, if they should escape, he took the oars himself and pulled to the Finita under fire from the pursuing forces on the shore. He landed at Inagua, a British possession, where he was aprehended by a cable order from Santo Domingo and held for six months on the charge of violating the neutrality laws. He escaped on a technicality, as he was returning from a revolution, not going to one. He came to New York, and in 1899 appeared in Havana at the head of another revolution.

JIMINEZ BECOMES PRESIDENT.

This time the revolution was better organized, and it followed immediately upon the assassination of Heureaux, who had ruled the country for seventeen years, and also ruined it. Jiminez had some trouble, however, with the American authorities in Cuba, as General Brooke was determined that the neutrality laws should not be violated. His bodyguard of honor, thirty-one young men who had been officers in the Cuban army, were detained at Baracoa for several months, they having been discovered in the possession of arms and amunition for the revolutionists. Jiminez went by a different route, and while he was detained at Cienfuegos and Santiago de Cuba, he was eventually allowed to take possession of the vessel that he had chartered, which was waiting for him in the harbor of Santiago.

General Horatio Vasquez was at the head of the provisional government in Santo Domingo, and after the assassination of Ulyssus Heureaux held the reins of government until General Jiminez should be able to break loose from the American entanglements in Cuba and reach Santo Domingo.
