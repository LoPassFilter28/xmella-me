Woman's Great Trip Covers 40,000 Miles in Out-of-the-way Places. Laughs at Her Many Perils Mrs. Harriet Chalmers Adams, Writer, Lecturer, and Traveler, Is Wife of Franklin Adams, of Bureau of American Republics. Only Ill Once in Long Trip.

To have encircled the entire South American continent within three years, to have been the first white woman to set foot upon many points reached by her in her travels, and to have endured hardships and privations that many women could not survive—this is the proud record of Mrs. Harriet Chalmers Adams, writer, lecturer, and traveler, of Washington.

Mrs. Adams is the wife of Franklin Adams, editor of the Bulletin of the International Bureau of American Republics, says the Baltimore Sun.

A Californian by birth, as is also her husband, Mrs. Adams became interested in the early Spanish history of her native State. This led her to study the Latin American republics, and before Mr. Adams became identified with the Bureau of American Republics she had accompanied him on an extensive trip through Mexico and Central America. Then, starting from Panama, the couple traveled southward through Ecuador, Peru, Chile, Patagonia, Argentina, Brazil, Guiana, Venezuela, Colombia, and other countries, including the West Indies, making a trip of approximately 40,000 miles.

On this trip Mrs. Adams endured great hardships in the Andean highlands and on the forest frontiers. Ecuador was visited first, the port of entry being Guayaquil. The railroad between that port and the highland capital of Quito had not then been constructed, and the trip was made on horseback or in the crude conveyances that were a feature of travel in that section in that period. Peru was traversed for its entire length and breadth. By means of the second highest railroad in the world, connecting Mollendo, the main port, with Lake Titicaca and La Paz, Bolivia, the Americans journeyed to that point in the highlands, from which they started on a saddle trip of 1,000 miles across the Andes. The way from Tirapata, which is 11,000 feet above the sea level, led to the third and highest chain of the Andes, the great Cordillera, which was crossed at the Pass of Aricoma, 17,000 feet above the sea.

The trail then brought Mrs. Adams down the 1,300 feet of mountain into the savage country of the Peruvian Montana, which marks the borders of Brazil. Here the saddle trail ended, the journey was resumed on foot through great forests and by canoe. In this vast section the wild Indians, known as the Chunchos, not to speak of wild beasts, monkeys, and bird life in wonderful variety, were encountered. Returning to the highlands, Cuzco and other prehistoric cities of the Incas were visited, and the Andes again were crossed into a forest country farther to the north.

On Foot Through Forests.

The Adams party visited Chile some months later, and after calling at its chief cities Terra del Fuego was gone over. At Punta Arenas a sailing vessel was chartered and a visit paid to the southernmost port in "Argentine-Patagonia." Through Patagonia they journeyed to Buenos Ayres, and up the great river highway, the La Plata, Parana, and Paraguay, to the wilds of Matto Grosso, Brazil, and Eastern Bolivia. Returning to the Parana, the party went up the Alta Parana to the falls of the Iguazu, the only rival of Niagara on the Western Hemisphere.

Mrs. Adams is the first woman to make the journey along the coast from the Amazon river to Cheyenne, French Guiana, a cattle boat to the Oyapoc river being employed. With her husband she spent three months in the forests with the Indians and Bosch negroes, known as the wild negroes of the bush. The three Guianas were visited, canoes and sailing vessels being the sole means of conveyance. A launch was used in the exploration of the Orinoco river. After some months spent in Venezuela, Colombia, and in steaming up the Magdalena River, the Adamses returned to Panama, their starting point.

That a journey of this kind should have been marked by lively adventures is not surprising, but it is surprising that Mrs. Adams should regard as commonplace and uninteresting events which in the lives of other women would stand as startling epochs. Her most interesting experience, because of its extreme peril, was met with in the Andes, when the Adams party was caught in a driving blizzard on the shoulder of Mount Mystic, at an altitude of 17,000 feet.

"We found a herd of llamas, and we crept in among them and nestled close to the animals," said Mrs. Adams. "I pillowed my head on the warm fur of one and got my feet under the body of another. I slept warm and comfortably, as did all the rest of us. It was a queer, but enjoyable, experience, and although the altitude at times almost baffled our efforts to reach the top, we managed to make the climb."

Her Only Illness.

Although Mrs. Adams' health had in her early life been deemed delicate, during her three years of travel she never had a day's illness, except once when she ate of a fowl killed with a poisoned arrow. She and her husband at that time were without guides and the nearest doctor was two days away by steamboat. Mrs. Adams drank plenty of milk supplied by the natives, and although at one time her husband despaired of saving her, she recovered and was able to resume her journey after a week's delay.

On one of their side journeys in the mountains the mule which carried the wardrobe of the Adamses fell from a cliff 1,000 feet high and was never seen again. This caused Mrs. Adams much inconvenience, but it did not disturb her serenity. It rained incessantly for two days thereafter, and when the party reached their next stopping place she smilingly admits she was wearing the blouse of a mining superintendent, the underclothes of a mining engineer, and the suit of an assistant manager of mines.

The scientific fame of Mrs. Adams, who is one of three women who were invited to address the Royal Geographical Society, will rest upon her discovery in Santo Domingo, a few weeks ago, of five rare specimens of the Colenodon, the ancestor of the rodent family. Three of these were presented to Bronx Park, New York, and the others to a zoological garden in Washington.

This West Indian journey, just completed, was one of the most interesting in Mrs. Adams' experience, for she virtually crossed the Haitian and Dominican republics on horseback. The trip was undertaken in the interest of the International Bureau of American Republics, and was quite successful.

Said Mrs. Adams:

"We motored from Havana to the famous tobacco lands of Pinar del Rio, crossed Cuba by train and took a steamer at Santiago de Cuba for Santo Domingo, the mother city of the Americas. It was here that Pizzaro, Cortez, Balboa, and Ojeda spent their boyhood. We visited the tomb of Columbus, where we saw the body exhumed, and actually held some the great explorer's ashes in our hand. From Cape Haitien we traveled the length of Haiti in the saddle, then went along the Haitian frontier to the old dividing line between French and Spanish territory.

"Then we went through the Dominican republic. It took five days to cross the Cordillera Central on horseback to La Vega, the famed royal meadow of Columbus, and to Santiago de los Caballeros, where there is railway connection with Puerto Plata. Saddle journeying on the islands was not difficult when compared with the terrible hardships that confront the traveler in the wilds of South America.

Natives Advancing.

"We found that the natives of Haiti and Santo Domingo are good natured, hospitable, and industrious. The Dominicans have the courtesy and charm of all Spanish-American peoples, and their history is among the richest of all the New World countries. In both republics there is marked advancement and development along commercial lines. There is to be constructed quite soon a railroad in Haiti which will traverse the island for its entire length, and which doubtless will be followed by remarkable development of its resources.

"When the hotels now being planned for the islands are built Haiti and Santo Domingo should be a mecca for the tourists of the world. In Santo Domingo, which was founded in 1496, and which until recently showed no changes since the days of its foundation, many improvements have been made."

Although Mrs. Adams says little about her prowess as a hunter, she admitted she could shoot and that she had brought down plenty of big game with the rifles provided by her husband for use during their South American tour.

"My father was one of the Argonauts of California," Mrs. Adams laughingly remarked when asked how she came to adopt the calling of an explorer. "He crossed the plains with an ox team and was one of the pioneers of the Golden State. If the call of what I might term the unbeaten path caught him, why should I escape? I love to travel, and my ambition will not be satisfied until I have crossed Africa and seen Tibet and other curious countries whose histories have roused within me the wanderlust."

At the banquet given to Sir Ernest and Lady Shackleton, in Philadelphia, on April 22 last, Mrs. Adams was the only woman called upon to respond to a toast. She made a distinct hit with the diners with the remark that "Dr. Wiley says that anything kept in cold storage for a year is spoiled. Sir Ernest Shackleton disproves this theory completely."

Mrs. Adams possesses rare and curious historical data regarding the countries she has visited and it is her purpose to write a history of her travels. Meanwhile she lectures in the principal cities, and her interesting talks are made enjoyable by stereopticon views and moving pictures, most of which were made by herself en route over mountain, plain, rivers, and seas.
