Right-Wing Neighbor Nations Keep Costa Rica on Hot Spot

By Robert S. Ball

North American Newspaper Alliance

SAN JOSE, Costa Rica, July 24.—Of all the hot spots in Central America today, Costa Rica is the hottest.

For Costa Rica is on the defensive in an all-out war of nerves with its Caribbean neighbors to the north and east. And elements within the country are helping to keep things stirred up.

Prime target of this nerve war, which some fear could become a shooting war, is Costa Rica's militantly democratic and dictator-hating president, Jose (Don Pepe) Figueres.

Mr. Figueres is probably the most despised chief executive in Central America, a distinction he has deliberately sought and appears to relish.

Outspoken Liberal.

Don Pepe's friends say he is regionally disliked because he is an outspoken liberal who never hesitates to tee off on what he describes as dictators of the extreme right. His enemies simply state that he is an international trouble-maker—and they want him out.

Inasmuch as Mr. Figueres was elected only last summer for a four-year-term, they are going to have to throw him out if they are in a hurry. And that is exactly what is being attempted.

(The Costa Rica government sent out a strong unit of civil guards yesterday to pursue a band of 20 armed followers of former President Rafael Calderon Guardia who attacked the telegraph office at Vara Blanca, several miles north of here, the Associated Press reported.

(The men cut telegraph wires and took as hostages three farmers. They then headed north to San Miguel, where they raided a bank and made away with about 27,000 colones ($4,000). The men were believed to have headed toward the Nicaraguan frontier where, unconfirmed reports have said, Calderonista elements were concentrating.

(Meanwhile it was reported that Costa Rica has asked El Salvador to mediate the ill feeling that has been mounting between Nicaragua and Costa Rica.)

The external pressures come chiefly from President Anastasio Somoza of Nicaragua, who blames Mr. Figueres for an assassination attempt last April; President Hector B. Trujillo of the Dominican Republic, who says Mr. Figueres is a Communist, and President Marcos Perez Jimenez of Venezuela, whose bitterest political enemy, Romulo Betancourt, was granted asylum in Costa Rica and has become an advisor to Mr. Figueres.

There is no love lost between Mr. Figueres and Col. Jose Antonio Remon, President of neighboring Panama.

Leaflets Dropped on San Jose.

Events of the past 30 days serve to illustrate the nature and scope of the war of nerves and its build-up in intensity.

Just a month ago a Venezuelan airplane showered anti-Figueres leaflets on San Jose.

Two weeks ago, Mr. Figueres ordered a blackout of the capital, set up anti-aircraft guns at the airport, and mounted security guards about the city.

He acted on reports received by diplomatic communication from Nicaragua. He fully expected the worst in the way of an enforceable ultimatum from Gen. Somoza's comparatively modern air force.

At about this same moment, Mr. Figueres announced receipt of an emergency shipment of arms from the United States.

This was followed almost immediately by publication of a report from Mexico that secret service agents had uncovered a plot to smuggle $1 million worth of small arms and grenades into Nicaragua.

Four P-47s at Managua.

On top of all this, and adding to Mr. Figueres' anxiety, is his knowledge that four unmarked P-47 fighters, believed to be the ones used in behalf of Col. Castillo Armas's invasion of Guatemala, are parked on the Managua airport; that increasing troop movements are reported inside Nicaragua, and that Gen. Somoza is doubling his military budget.

Costa Rica, which abolished its army in 1949, has only a civil guard of less than 1,000, and no combat aircraft. The country would be helpless before Gen. Somoza's army and air force if he should blow his top.

Most of the internal opposition to Mr. Figueres stems from wealthy businessmen. They regard him as a Socialist, though not a pro-Communist.

The center of the political attack on Mr. Figueres is the Union National Party, headed by an ex-president, Otilio Ulate Blanco, publisher of two bitterly anti-Figueres newspapers and owner of a San Jose radio station.

Challenges Arms Deal.

Mr. Ulate, whom Mr. Figueres succeeded in 1953, has challenged the legality of the arms purchase on the ground that Costa Rica has no army and is not eligible to receive United States arms under the mutual security program.

He is also critical of Mr. Figueres' move to reimpose a special 10 per cent super-income and assets tax in order to pay for the $500,000 shipment.

Communists are active in Costa Rica, although the government is tightening up its controls on their activities. The 1949 constitution outlawed the Communist Party as a political entity because it advocated the violent overthrow of the Government.

Communists have been active in the labor confederations, however, and Mr. Figueres is dealing with that situation through a new and stringent law banning Reds from membership in the confederation.

Mr. Figueres is not worried about a Communist-inspired coup from within; but he is vitally concerned with the possibility of a right-wing attempt from without.
