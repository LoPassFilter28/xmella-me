Accurate Map of Dominica and Haiti

FORT CLAYTON, Canal Zone. — A highly accurate map of Dominica, 30,000-square-mile island home of two republics, now approaches completion.

It is the work of the Inter-American Geodetic Survey, carried out during the past 12 years by engineers trained at a specialist school here, and with the technical guidance of Capt. F. M. Lindgren of the U. S. Army Engineer Corps and Robert Simms, project engineer.

Especially in the Dominican Republic, Capt. Lindgren explains; some hitherto undescribed geographical features are outstanding. There is, for example, Largo Enriquilo, the "little salt lake," well inland near the Haitian border, literally a New World "dead sea," although one species of small and primitive fishes are found in the waters. It is 240 feet below sea level, perhaps a remnant of some ancient body of water which divided the present areas of Haiti and the Dominican Republic into two islands but which was eliminated in some ancient cataclysm.

Impenetrable Area

In the Dominican Republic the engineers encountered, due north of the present Ciudad Trujillo, one of the New World's most impenetrable areas, the region of Haitises, which could be mapped only by air photography. It is an unexplored region of about 800 square miles of coral ridges rising one after another and all covered by a dense cactus jungle through which are wild goat trails in a jigsaw pattern which is essentially unmappable on the ground. The land is uninhabited, although native goat hunters venture into the cactus labyrinth. Without streams or ponds, it would be a barren desert except for the fact that the coral absorbs water like a sponge during the rainy season.

It was necessary, however, for one of the Army engineers, James McIntyre, to go through the wild country first on horseback and later on foot, to locate focal points shown on the air photographs. This was a very difficult journey of two days.

Among the curious features of the island, as described by Capt. Lindgren, is the existence within 50 miles of each other of the highest and lowest points in the Caribbean area—the bottom of the salt lake and the summit of two-mile-high Pico Trujillo.

The engineers have mapped the courses of six Dominican Republic rivers, including the Yaqui del Norte, Yaqui del Sur, the Ozama and the Bisyasica, which make the western half of the island so much more fertile than the Haitian area.

A complete map of the 11,000-square-mile Republic of Haiti, perhaps the first New World country known to white men, soon will be available for the first time in its 500 years of history.

All the basic controls and most of the air photography in the mountainous land has been completed, according to Capt. J. J. Slager of the United States Army Engineers, project director of the Inter-American Geodetic Survey, and F. A. Chavez, the project engineer. The work most of the time has been extremely laborious. It has been necessary for the engineers to climb mountain after mountain with altitudes up to 9,500 feet, carrying their heavy equipment as far as possible on horse or mule back and on their own backs the rest of the way. Most of the work has been done by Haitian engineers trained in a school maintained by the IAGS here.

Helicopters Used

But, says Capt. Slager, within the past two years it has been possible to eliminate most of this hard work with helicopters. Formerly it required four to five days for a survey party to reach the top of a Haitian mountain. Now the men are picked up one by one at a base camp and flown to a mountain top. A job which used to require a week now is completed in half a day.

There have been maps of Haiti, after a fashion, for centuries, Capt. Slager says, but they have been made piece by piece under different governments with almost no attempt at co-ordination. Basic features of the country's geography, which can be utilized as control points, have been badly mislocated.

The new map, it is expected, will have considerable economic value for the country and the IAGS' work is being supported in part by the Point Four program, designed to provide economic aid to the smaller New World nations.
