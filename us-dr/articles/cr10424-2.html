MEMORIAL TO COLUMBUS (From The Times-Star)

One of the greatest symbols of Pan-American union will be the Christopher Columbus Memorial Lighthouse, to be erected on the coast of the Dominican Republic by the joint efforts of the 21 American republics. First conceived years ago, shortly after the remains of Columbus were found reposing in the cathedral of Santo Domingo, the idea advanced to reality last September first, when the international contest for an architect's design of the lighthouse opened.

The first stage of this contest will close on April 1, when the ten best designs will be selected to enter another more exacting competition from which will emerge a fitting tribute to the discoverer of the New World.

Preliminary requirements show that it will be a magnificent memorial. It will be so elevated that its base can be seen above the neighboring trees. The architects are requested to produce something extraordinary, and are reminded that mere academic perfection will not produce a winning design. They must present the achievements of the great historical figure in their significance in the evolution of the world civilization.

It is a task for genius. No one possessing it can complain of a mediocre subject on which to exercise his talents. He has an opportunity for world poetry.

When completed, the memorial will be one of the shrines of the New World. Situated on the coast of a West Indian island, it will be the tribute of all American nations to one who inaugurated American history. That all these nations should work together in this aim is a remarkable example of their unity.

That unity is even more definitely exemplified in that portion of the specifications which calls for the designing of a large airport adjacent to the memorial, with an explanation that Santo Domingo would be the half-way stop in an air route from Montreal to Buenos Aires.

That look into the future explains why such a memorial by all nations of the New World is possible. The day is fast coming when communication and social and trade intercourse will render the distinctions between these nations negligible. It is fitting that, on the eve of the great Pan-America, they should unite in such a splendid memorial to the man, who, after allegiance to the flags of three nations, became the first American.
