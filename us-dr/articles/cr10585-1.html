Allies Pleased by Quiet But Firm Berlin Stand

By EARL H. VOSS

Star Staff Writer

Secretary of State Rusk's quiet but firm determination to keep the West in Berlin while giving Soviet Premier Khrushchev "every opportunity to avoid a miscalculation" was being well received by allied diplomats here today.

The United States' principal allies agreed that the West must again provide a backdoor through which the Soviet Premier can slip if he decides he cannot risk war to oust them from West Berlin.

In this connection, diplomats here were discussing views on a single sentence in Mr. Khrushchev's Wednesday speech that could be regarded as a pledge to preserve the West's access to West Berlin, which is surrounded by Communist East Germany.

"Neither the Soviet Union nor the German Democratic Republic intends to restrict the links between West Berlin and all countries of the world," Mr. Khrushchev is quoted by Tass, the official Soviet press agency.

Concerned for Allied Rights

In his press conference yesterday, Mr. Rusk indicated the United States' main concern was in maintaining the rights of the Western powers in Berlin rather than signing of a peace treaty between East Germany and Russia.

This would not mean that the West would accept without a protest such a separate treaty.

A reporter asked Mr. Rusk what would happen if the treaty were signed, but no effort was made to block access routes.

Secretary Rusk answered: "Any attempt to freeze the position in Germany without due regard to the wishes of the German people would be, in our judgment, a very unfortunate step to take. The Soviet Union and its representatives have stood up in the United Nations from time to time and made a great play over their commitment to the notion of self-determination in various parts of the world. They have been unwilling to apply that same principle to Central Europe."

"Heart of the Matter"

The Secretary's assumption apparently was that a separate peace treaty between Russia and East Germany would be an attempt to freeze the partition of Germany without elections to sanction it.

But, "the heart of the matter," Mr. Rusk said, is the Western position in Berlin. "Our own national interest starts with our position in West Berlin and our commitment to the people of that city."

The West also has an interest in East Berlin and a peace settlement for all of Germany, he said, "but I wouldn't want to water down in any way the heart of our interest, which is our position in West Berlin."

In an opening, prepared statement on the Berlin problem Mr. Rusk struck back at several Soviet propaganda themes. Mr. Khrushchev made much of his claim that the situation in West Berlin was "abnormal" in the 1958-9 crisis.

"Abnormality" Stressed

To blunt this propaganda point, Mr. Rusk applied the "abnormal" adjective to a larger context.

"Due to the defacto division of Germany, the entire situation in that country is abnormal," he declared. "The Soviet position in regard to this matter is predicated on the belief that the division of Germany is normal, that the division of Berlin is normal, and that the sole abnormality that persists is West Berlin. This is not a formulation of the problem which is acceptable to the United States."

Mr. Rusk also attacked "many contradictions and historical fallacies," in the Russian position.

Hits at Peace Threats

"The Soviets talk constantly of peace but threaten the obvious peace which exists in West Berlin," he said. ". . . If the world is full of anxiety and uneasiness over Berlin, this arises directly from the threat of the Soviets to the rights of others and to the liberty of the West Berliners rather than to anything in the present situation in Berlin."

On other topics Mr. Rusk asked about the situation in the Dominican Republic since the assassination of Gen. Rafael Trujillo, commented, "I personally believe the OAS (Organization of American States) committee left the Dominican Republic somewhat too soon."

The six-nation group spent only a week in the Dominican Republic investigating the new regime's actions to restore democracy.
