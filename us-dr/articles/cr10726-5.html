The New-York Tribune reported further statements by Judge Durham concerning the collapse of the Dominican currency system under late President Heureaux. Durham disclosed that Heureaux systematically evaded agreements with the San Domingo Improvement Company by shifting printing plates between American firms to produce excessive quantities of unauthorized paper money. This unchecked currency inflation created widespread commercial distrust, leaving the Improvement Company with large unusable balances and bringing local commerce to an abrupt standstill upon the president's death.El New-York Tribune informó sobre declaraciones adicionales del juez Durham relativas al colapso del sistema monetario dominicano bajo el mandato del difunto presidente Heureaux. FINANCES IN A SNARL. RESTORING SAN DOMINGO'S SHATTERED CREDIT—AN ALMOST HOPELESS TASK.

San Domingo, March 2 (Special).—An interesting talk with Thomas MacLelan disclosed the fact that he is strongly in favor of American occupancy. He is a Scotchman, and is general manager of the railroad running from Sanchez to Las Vegas. There are sixty-three miles of railroad in all, and it is owned by the Baird nobility of Scotland. During the revolution the people of Sanchez tore up the railroad track to keep the insurgents from getting into the city, and the insurgents at Las Vegas tore up that end to keep the soldiers of the Government back, and between the two not much of the railroad was left. The heavy rains carried off most of the bridges and the railroad is laboring under difficulties.

Mr. MacLelan is at the capital asking settlement of the claim for damages arising out of the revolution. He is an able man and has the bluntness characteristic of his people. "I left Scotland thirteen years ago a Monarchist," he said, "and I am more of a Monarchist than ever." Still I recognize that there is no difference between a republic and a monarchy where the government is honest and intelligent. I think Europe for a great many years has recognized the inviolability of the Monroe Doctrine and that the West Indies and Central and South America must yield to American influences. I tell the politicians of my town that the United States will control San Domingo inside of two years and that they will be glad of it, but of course they do not believe me. As I am not an agitator I am allowed to express my opinions with impunity.

THE DOMINICANS NEED A RUDDER.

"What the people need here is a power behind them to maintain order and protect life and property and there will be wonderful progress. What is most exasperating is the failure to meet contracts. When we contract for 18,000 ties we cannot expect to get more than 10,000. When we employ men to do certain things we expect them to be half done. That is the case generally outside. With my employes, there is full service rendered and the men take pride in meeting my expectations. They are sober and industrious when properly trained and render efficient service. Our line passes through the most fertile land in the world, most of it uncultivated and apparently unowned. When we come to build an extension, however, we find half a dozen people with titles to the same property asking damages.

"Sanchez, the town of which I am president, is the most industrious city in the republic, and I think my efforts have led the people to be proud of their thrift and education. We have good schools and are instructing the masses. We are under many disadvantages in the management of our railroad. Every time we pick up a piece of coal we have to pay some kind of a tax on it. There is the import duty, the wharfage, the lighterage, the cartage, and so on until the coal costs us three times the price in the United States, not considering the cost of transportation. These obstacles we have to contend with and still make our road pay."

WHY JUDGE DURHAM SUBSCRIBED.

Judge Durham, at one time United States Consul at San Domingo and later Minister to Hayti and San Domingo under the Harrison Administration, was at the capital at the same time as Mr. MacLelan. Mr. Durham is administrator of the Consuelo sugar estates at Macoris and has claims against the Government arising out of the currency question. When at one time the French claim was being pressed the late President called upon Mr. Durham for assistance in meeting the demands. Mr. Durham knew that a refusal to meet requests of this sort was usually followed by a death or a fire in the sugar cane, and so he gave a draft for $12,000 in gold and took $40,000 of Dominican paper money in exchange. He has the paper money yet. During the recent excitement over the French claim Mr. Durham subscribed $100, Dominican money, to the popular fund, but the heirs of the Bass estate (American), of which he is administrator, cabled to subscribe nothing, probably thinking that $12,000 gold was more than their share. This action has been very unfavorably commented on by the Dominican papers. Judge Durham said:

"I am one of those proverbial Philadelphia lawyers, and I do not see a way out of the present financial difficulties of this Government. All the cultivated soil is taxed away out of sight by the export duties here and the import duties of the United States, and no further taxation is possible. The improvement company has control of the revenues to its loan, and it pays the Government $60,000 a month on its budget, which is not sufficient to meet current expenses. Now, what is the remedy? I confess I do not know; popular loans and subscriptions will soon exhaust the patriotic spirit of the people. The foreign capital that came into this republic came without thought of political intrigue but recognized the fertility of the soil and the possibilities. As a result of that honest investment foreign capital and foreigners bear all the burden of taxation. That is practically an attempt to drive foreign capital out of the republic and to take a step backward in the National progress. Here are some figures on our sugar business. We pay in the way of taxation on 100 pounds of sugar:

Import in New-York............$1 68

Export in San Domingo, including duties, tonnage dues, additional and extra duties, with monument tax for Christopher Columbus...................... 33

Total..........................$2 01

Freight, insurance, commission, lighterage in New-York......................... 33

Grand total...................$2 34

WORKING ON A SMALL MARGIN.

"We sell in New-York for $4 25, so that we have to do business on a margin of less than $2 a hundred pounds, pay for the cultivation of the crop and the gathering of it, take risks on losses at sea and by fire, and compete with Hawaii, Puerto Rico, possibly Cuba, and meet the provisions of the West Indies treaties, the German beet sugar treaty, the American bounties on beet sugar and the Louisiana sugar product. Yet this is the principal product the Government depends upon to meet the demands of the San Domingo Improvement Company in its handling of the revenues. How is the Government going to better this situation? It is absolutely necessary that there be a reduction in this taxation that we may successfully market our product in the United States. At the same time the Government has an equally urgent necessity for increased revenues. The late President had at one time twenty-five clerks employed signing this paper money. The issue was authorized by the San Domingo Improvement Company to the extent of about a million, but when the President began the issue he had control of the plates and went on issuing. The improvement company tried to get control of the plates, but as fast as they traced them the President changed them to some other printing establishment in the United States and the company was unable to control the issue. The company itself had agreed, in order to give them circulation, to accept a certain per cent in customs duties, but so great was the issue that the company found itself in possession of $400,000 of the money that it was unable to dispose of. The abundance of this money created general distrust, and when the President was assassinated there was practically no currency in the island and business was at a standstill.

A NATIVE WHO WOULDN'T ACCEPT GOLD.

"We could buy no meat of any kind for three days, and our people lived on what they could pick from the trees or had in the way of fowl. I called to my office a man who had some cattle to sell and tried to buy them. I laid down a $20 gold piece and offered him all he wanted of them in equal exchange for his cattle. He pulled out of his pocket a Dominican silver dollar and said: 'They tell me this has no silver in it; that it is worthless; how do I know your metal is any more valuable?'

"I said, 'This is gold worth its weight the world over.'

"'How do you know it is? Did you make it?'

"'No,' I said, 'but I tell you it is.'

"'Well, how do I know that you are not mistaken? I thought this silver dollar was silver, but now I find that it is not, and you are just as likely to be wrong as I am. I do not want your money. I know my cattle are real and valuable, and I will keep them.'

"I had to have some meat, and was somewhat nonplussed. My eyes fell on a pile of barbed wire, used everywhere for fencing. I said, 'You know what that is worth a pound?'

"'Yes,' he said.

"'Well, I will trade you that for your cattle.' He jumped at the offer, and for three months we were back to the original savage idea of barter and trade. I had to send to New-York three times for barbed wire to meet the demands. Later I offered $18 worth of barbed wire or $20 in gold in an effort to restore confidence, but it was useless, for the barbed wire always received the preference.

"The labor question is also a serious one here; there is no provision for the future, and nature supplies food practically free, so that when a man works a day he wants to spend his day's wages and enjoy them before he works again. We employ men by the week, paying them every Sunday, and also have piecework, for which we pay every noon, the men getting in from one to five hours. Most of the men prefer to work at the piecework, as they work only a few hours and get a little money daily. At one time a governor of a province was ordered by the President to clear a road from Macoris to intersect the road to the capital. Clearing a road here means cutting out the underbrush low enough and the path wide enough for a horse to get through. The Governor was a man of some energy, and he called out his soldiers, employed his laborers and made them work six days in the week on the road, paying them in full for the week's work. This they considered a great hardship, and he was much hated. A short time after that, in a revolution, he became a political prisoner, and was led captive down to the capital over this same road. This the natives claimed was the act of Providence in retribution.

MONEY OF ALL KINDS DISTRUSTED.

"In the adjustment of wages there is a great difficulty on account of the general distrust in all kinds of money. One man I employed asked $18 a month gold, and I asked him what he meant by gold, and he said he did not know, but that was what everybody asked. Reliable labor is hard to get, and some of the sugar estates adopted the expedient of offering payment in American gold. We met this by offering American gold, Mexican silver or Dominican money—gold at par, Mexican money at 50 cents and Dominican silver at 20 cents. This caused endless financial discussion among the employes, but resulted in their taking gold when paid off on Sunday, changing it for Mexican money Monday at an advantage to us of 6 per cent, and again changing what was left of that to Dominican money on Tuesday at a further discount.

"In our payments for piecework we had a great need for small change, which we could not get in the country. I sent to New-York for 1,000 one cent pieces, which were sent to us new from the mint. We had a great deal of trouble in explaining their value in Mexican and Dominican money, and they were accepted reluctantly. Then there was a great run on the one cent pieces that I could not understand. Employes brought back gold, silver, Mexican money, Dominican money and any other kind and wanted the copper money. I made an inquiry and found that an Arabian pedler had been in town and had mistaken the bright, new copper for gold and had given an employe a shirt and a Dominican dollar in exchange for one of them. I did not want to be a party to a deception of this sort and locked the pennies up, to be used only when necessary.

"What I have stated indicates the popular idea of money and the financial difficulties the Government has to overcome. One remedy is apparent—closer relations with the United States; but just now American intervention is not popular."
