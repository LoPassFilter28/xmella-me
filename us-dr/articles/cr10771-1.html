RECIPROCITY ATTACKED

Germany Secretly Working Against the United States.

BRIBERY AND COERCION EMPLOYED

The State Department Cognizant of the Overtures Made to the Pan-American Republics to Abrogate the Existing Treaties With This Country.

NEW YORK, Oct. 3.—That there is a conspiracy among the great European nations to force the Pan-American republics to abrogate their reciprocity treaties with this country seems to be a settled fact. All the means known to the diplomats of the Old World, from coercion to bribery, are being used without stint, and all the benefits which have resulted from reciprocal treaties to the manufacturers and producers of the United States are liable to be lost unless the most decisive steps are taken by this government.

That such steps will be taken may be regarded as certain, for Secretary of State John W. Foster, when approached by the representative of the president of one of these republics with the request that the United States outbid a bribe offered by Germany, replied:

"This country is neither to be blackmailed nor bullied. If your country breaks the treaty it must be prepared to bear all the consequences of that act, for in my mind the word 'reciprocate' is coupled with 'retaliation.'"

The moving cause for this action on the part of the European nations is that the trade which for years they have monopolized with the Pan-American countries has, since the reciprocal treaties with the United States went into effect, been fading away.

The republic which at present is considering offers of bribes is San Domingo. Although small she has heretofore done a large trade with European countries, particularly Germany.

Two months ago a formal protest was made to the Dominican government by the representatives of Germany, Italy and France against the further continuance of reciprocity with this country, and it was accompanied by a demand that the treaty be abrogated at once. The Dominican government stood firm, however, and the consul in New York, Francisco Leonte Vasquez, was instructed to visit Washington and consult with Secretary of State John W. Foster. He did so, and while nothing definite was decided upon he was assured that the United States would see that San Domingo was protected in her rights. With this assurance the consul returned to this city.

Germany the Moving Power.

As soon as it was found that bullying did not succeed another tack was taken. Germany was chosen as the moving power of the conspiracy, because her relations with the little republic are peculiar.

In 1885 a treaty was signed between the two, in which Germany was named as the "most favored of nations." Her goods were permitted to enter Dominican ports at extremely low imposts, and her citizens were to receive special favors and exemptions.

On the other hand, many Dominican products were given precedence in Germany, and under this treaty a large and valuable trade grew up between the two nations. Dominican tobacco especially found favor in Germany and soon crowded out the Sumatra product, and at present almost the entire product of Puerto Plata is used in Germany.

The loss of this market for its staple product would be a serious blow to the San Domingan people, and it was on this line that the conspirators went to work.

A special envoy was sent from Germany to San Domingo, and about two weeks ago he had an interview with President Ulises Heureaux, in which the harm which could be done his country should Germany close her ports to Dominican products was fully set forth. The German envoy then offered a personal subsidy of $50,000 a year to the president "to recompense him for any unpopularity he might incur" if the reciprocity treaty with the United States was abrogated.

President Heureaux at once dispatched an emissary to this country to see if he could not obtain $75,000 from the United States. The person chosen for this work was a merchant, who is a correspondent of the exporting and shipping firm of Eggers & Heinflein, of 5 South William street, this city. On his arrival he saw Mr. Eggers and explained his mission. He requested Mr. Eggers to accompanying him to Whshington to interview Secretary Foster. Mr. Eggers refused, and the San Domingan then visited other friends and requested their aid with a like result. About ten days ago he went to Washington alone and saw the secretary.

Only the Entering Wedge.

The answer he received from Mr. Foster is that quoted above. The San Domingan told friends here that he would go to Germany in hopes of getting that government to raise its offer. He sailed for Europe last Thursday.

When questioned about the transaction Mr. Heinlein, of the firm of Eggers & Heinlein, said: "I know little or nothing about it. I was away at the time, and my partner, Mr. Eggers, had charge of the business. However, I have known for a long time that the European nations would try to break up our reciprocal relations with the Pan-American countries.

"I have been to Germany this summer, and I know that country has been trying to force San Domingo to abrogate the treaty, and I expect she will be successful. You see the entire tobacco crop is used in Germany, and the commercial relations between the two countries are very close. For that reason she, of all other nations, is the one now to move in this matter, and if she can force San Domingo to abrogate the treaty, then an immediate attack will be made on all the other republics having reciprocal relations with this country to force them to follow her example.

"If the Dominican treaty is broken I fully expect to see all the others broken as well, and that within a very short time. The fact of the matter is that England, Germany, France and Italy all have large and valuable interests in South and Central America, and they cannot afford to have their trade with those points destroyed because of reciprocity with the United States. All of these countries have experience in handling such matters, and it is quite certain that nothing will be left undone to gain the end desired. Germany's step is but the entering wedge."

When questioned as to the name of the merchant who acted as the messenger in the attempt to blackmail the United States government he refused to give the slightest information.

San Domingo Asks Our Aid.

Consul Francisco Leonte Vasquez, of 31 Broadway, who represents the Dominican republic in this city, when seen by a reporter seemed very much surprised that the matter had become known. He said, "This affair is still in the hands of the state department at Washington and it was agreed that it should be kept secret for the present.

"The outlook for a practical monopoly of the Dominican trade was excellent until the present troubles began. When Germany, Italy and France made demand upon our government for the abrogation of the reciprocity treaty our government stood firm. Then they demanded to be placed on the same footing in all respects as the United States, which would have been a practical annullment of the treaty. It was then I was sent to Washington to see what could be done. Of course our small republic would be almost helpless with these three nations attacking us at once, and we wanted to be certain of the aid of the United States in case of trouble. I had a talk with Secretary Foster over the matter, but while he was most encouraging he wished the matter to remain in abeyance for a time.

An Envoy to Crespo.

"No course of action was decided upon, and the certainty that Germany would use our tobacco industry to force matters to a crisis has troubled me greatly. That Germany will try to produce this result unless we recede from our treaty obligations with the United States I have for a long time known.

"Another feature of this attempt of the European nations to cause the abrogation of all reciprocity treaties with Pan-American republics is that the present Venezuelan government is being aided with money and supplies by England, Germany and France. An offer was made and accepted shortly after Crespo's rebellion had begun, and the terms have been ratified by both parties since. The agreement was that the present government should refuse to ratify the reciprocity treaty with the United States and should refuse all overtures to that end while opening its ports freely to European trade.

"An envoy was sent to Crespo, and he agreed that on obtaining power his government would promptly ratify the treaty thrown aside by the present officials.

"On his pledge to this effect money was sent him and the South Portland and one other vessel, the name of which the members of the "junta" refuse to give, were sent down laden with arms.
