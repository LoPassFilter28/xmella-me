JOE LOYD WRITES MORE ABOUT U. S. MARINES Hot Spring County Boy Tells of Life of Marine Corp in the Dominican Republic,

In the Eastern part of the island of Santo Domingo and twenty-one kilometers from the seaport of San Pedro de Macoris, there is a small town known as Chicharrrones. It is very small, having only two Bodegas, a bakery shop and about twenty five small houses. The town sits on the west side of the road and on the east side is a company of U. S. Marines.

The 44th company of Marines is one of the very few mounted companies of the marine corps. We look into this company of marines and find an ideal bunch of young Americans from eighteen to thirty years of age. They are strong, sturdy young men with sterling characters, selected from the many applicants at the various recruiting stations of the Marine Corps throughout the United States.

We do not claim to be the only company worthy of the highest recommendations, but we do claim our place in the corps as a whole.

We see these Marines "hit the deck" in the morning at the first break of day and make for the stables to feed the horses. The feed is carefully given to each horse, care being taken not to overfeed or underfeed them. Feeding done the men return to their sleeping quarters and prepare for breakfast. Breakfast call is sounded and the men fall in two ranks and march in the mess hall. Breakfast is served in an orderly manner. After the breakfast is served the men repair to their quarters to await the call, stables." All men on line duty "turn to" and groom the horses for inspection. Each horse is carefully groomed and given especial attention which he may need, medical aid, shoeing, clipping or washing. About two hours are taken up in grooming when the "water call' is sounded. Each man takes a horse and the remaining horses are headed to the river about a kilometer from camp. The horses are then returned to the stables and turned into the corral where they are allowed to roam and take exercise until they are fed again at noon.

The men go back to camp and "turn to" to do the policing of camp and make repairs. Again in the afternoon the have "police call" where everyone "turns to" and polices the camp for a few minutes then "recall" is sounded. The men then do the famous exercise known as "bunk fatigue" when they take a rest.

At about 3:15 "water call" again and the horses are watered, returned to the stables and fed.

Now we find the men preparing for supper which is served at five o'clock. After supper the time is yours to do as you please as long as you conduct yourself properly.

Four times per week we have moving pictures shown for the service men free of charge. Most of the pictures shown here are secured from the Navy Motion Picture Exchange of Brooklyn.

We have a good library of fiction, some of the newest books out, and all the men pass their evenings reading or studying. Correspondent courses are given free. The Marine Corps secures these lessons from the International Correspondence School and at present has about six thousand Marines matriculated in their wonderful school. We have many periodicals to read. Some are given by individuals and some by welfare boards. All donations are gratefully received.

Perhaps this is a vgue description of the daily routine, but I have received a few letters from individuals asking what the Marines did in the tropics. I have told you what the 44th does, but the other company follow the infantry.

Do not be impressed that the service men live the life of Reilly, because they are hard working men. Remember, too, all of your hours you may go and come as you choose while with service men they have less than twelve hours to themselves out of twenty-four.

Joseph W. Lloyd.
