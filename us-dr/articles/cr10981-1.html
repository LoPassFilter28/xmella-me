Alto Velo.

From the Chicago Tribune, May 1.

The ghost of Alto Velo is still hovering around. It made its appearance in the Impeachment Court the other day, and almost provoked bloodshed. What is it? Alto Velo enjoys the distinction of having been one of the West India Islands discovered by Columbus in person. Upon it he raised the standard of Spain, and formally proclaimed it a possession of his sovereign, nearly four hundred years ago. Its recent history is as follows: On the 18th of August, 1856, Congress enacted a law that whenever any citizens should discover a deposit of guano "on any island, rock, or key not within the lawful jurisdiction of any other government, and not occupied by the citizens of any other government, and shall take possession thereof, and occupy the same," such persons should be protected in such possession by the United States. On the 14th of May, 1860, Jeremiah S. Black was Secretary of State under James Buchanan; on that day W. J. Kendall, of Baltimore, Maryland, notified the department that he had discovered a deposit of guano on the island of Alto Velo, that he had taken possession, and asked to have his claim acknowledged. On the same day, Patterson and Murguiendo, also of Baltimore, filed a similar notice; Kendall dated his discovery on the 19th of March, 1860, and Patterson and Murguiendo dated theirs on the 23d of February. Nothing farther appears on record respecting Kendall's claim, but Patterson and Murguiendo continued to press the department for a recognition of theirs, but the Secretary, Mr. Jerry Black, refused to grant it in the absence of proof that the island was not within the lawful jurisdiction of any other government.

In the meantime the parties proceeded to work on the island shipping home the guano, and were in possession of it some seven months. At the end of that time, a war schooner of the Dominican navy visited it, broke up the business and took the occupants to St. Domingo, but subsequently released and sent them home.

The parties thereupon made claim for damages, laying there entire claim at about $23,000. They also sought to make a contract with the Dominican, St. Domingo, Government for permission to ship guano from the island. The American Secretary of State, Mr. Jerry Black, refused to take notice of any claim for damages until after the agent of the United States at St. Domingo should have ascertained and reported the grounds upon which the Dominican Government had asserted its jurisdiction. Before the answer was received, Mr. Black had ceased to be Secretary of State and had been succeeded by Mr. Seward. The rebellion and the war, various revolutions in the Dominican Republic, and war between that government and Hayti, the Mexican occupation, and various other national and international complications postponed any consideration of the claims of Patterson and Murguiendo. In February, 1866, one Henry G. Root, of New York, wrote to the department asking for information respecting the rights of Americans to take guano from Alto Velo, and the department answered that two parties of Americans had claimed the island as discoverers and that St. Domingo had asserted a title and ejected the claimants. Pending the consideration of the matter, the department declined giving any encouragement to Americans to resort there for guano without first obtaining the consent of St. Domingo. Alto Velo dropped out of notice again until January, 1867, when Mr. J. W. Shaffer, of Illinois, who signs himself as attorney, and Messrs. J. S. Black and Ward H. Lamon, who sign themselves "of counsel" representing Patterson & Murguiendo, filed a paper in the State Department renewing the claim for damages, and for restoration of the island. The State Department referred the case to the proper officer for examination, who reported that the claim of the Dominican Government to the island was clear and unquestionable. He traced its recorded history from the discovery, and found that in all the political and geographical definitions of the boundaries and divisions of the mainland, Alto Velo had been invariably assigned to that portion of the mainland now included within the Dominican Republic. It was an integral part of the country. The rule of law applicable to the case, as laid down by Lord Stowell, was that an island whose occupancy by a foreign power would be inconvenient or dangerous to the government having jurisdiction of the contiguous mainland, must be held to belong to the mainland, whether actually occupied or not.

This report was made in June, 1867. In July, Mr. Black had managed to work the small damages claimed by Patterson & Murguiendo, of $23,000, up to nearly two millions of dollars. He addressed a furious letter to the President demanding that a naval force be sent to Alto Velo to put his clients in possession. Black was allowed to make an elaborate argument, and the case was re-examined by the Department.

In the meantime a new light appeared on the case. From some casual reference in the newspapers to Jerry Black's gigantic claim, Messrs. Thomas R. Webster & Co., of New York, in September, 1867, informed the Department that as long ago as April, 1866, they had purchased from the Dominican Government the right to take guano from the Island of Alto Velo, and that they had expended large sums of money and were in regular receipt of cargoes from the island. Thus it appears that the island is, and has been for years, in the possession and occupancy of American citizens. Mr. Seward made a report of these additional facts, affirming the previous judgment of the Department, and calling the attention of the President to the fact, that Black's clients and the present occupants of the island being American citizens, the remedy for any injury sustained by either should be sought by legal proceedings in the United States Courts. Those courts had full jurisdiction of the matter, and it was no part of the Executive duty to interfere. Black appealed from this decision of Secretary Seward, made in January, 1868. He addressed his arguments in language of the most vehement character. He demanded that the navy be immediately employed to put his clients in possession and to oust everybody else. He denounced Mr. Seward personally in the most indecorous language.

A letter dated March 9, was signed by Messrs. Butler, Logan and Stevens, Managers of the Impeachment, requesting the President to send the vessel of war as requested by Black. This extraordinary letter, bearing date two weeks after impeachment, was read to the Senate by the President's counsel, Mr. Nelson. Mr. Butler denied the accuracy of its date, alleging that it was signed before the impeachment took place. The paper, no matter when signed, is not creditable to those whose names are attached to it. Mr. Seward deserves the thanks of the country for his refusal to lend the naval and military power of the government to aid a private speculation, and for his firmness in resisting the powerful and unscrupulous lobby recruited to carry it through.
