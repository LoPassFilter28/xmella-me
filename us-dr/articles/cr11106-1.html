WILSON CHEERED AS CONFERENCE OPENS Pan-Americans Acclaim President as He Voices Hope for Better Trade.

WILSON CHEERED AS CONFERENCE OPENS

Pan-Americans Acclaim President as He Voices Hope for Better Trade.

(Continued from First Page.)

Americans feel at home. The delegates met in the big assembly room of the Pan-American building, whose corridors contain exhibits of the industries of Central and South America, and whose walls are lined with the marble busts of the great men of these republics.

Plants Wave Welcome.

Palms and tropical plants seemingly waved a welcome; the flags of all the Americas floated in fellowship under a soft breeze, and flowers familiar to this and other lands lent their fragrance and beauty to a truly inspiring and important gathering.

Just a bit of humor was given an otherwise dignified assemblage by the noise-making activities of four Brazilian parrots, perched upon their perches in the patio, or courtyard, of the building. Occasionally, as matters of state and finance were being discussed a few feet away, these birds squawked most disrespectfully.

John Barrett, director of the Pan-American bureau, finally assigned a stern and exacting colored servant to stand guard over the offending birds and by persuasion, or otherwise, prevent them from intruding upon the proceedings.

Thereafter, whenever a parrot grew mindful of the importance of the speeches being made within and emitted unseemly noises, the disrespectful bird would be "shooed" into silence. Nevertheless, the colored guard aforementioned had no easy task.

McAdoo To Speak.

Secretary McAdoo, who was scheduled to make the principal speech of the afternoon session, opened the conference, which will extend throughout the week. Secretaries Bryan, Daniels, and Lane occupied seats on the platform and as the conference opened the members of the Resere Board filed in and took seats to the right of the speakers' stand.

The President and Cabinet officers were generously acclaimed as they entered the hall and the addresses of Mr. Wilson and Secretary Bryan were exceedingly well received. Following the speech of Secretary Bryan a representative from each of the countries represented was called to the platform and delivered a short speech in reply.

All thees utterances forecasted an improvement of the commercial relations among the American countries, and there were frequent references to the European war. The peace endeavors of the Administration were lauded by successive speakers from the Central and South American countries.

President Remains.

President Wilson remained throughout the morning ceremonies, greeted each speaker as he reached the platform and frequently noding approval of the expressions relating to the necessity of closer business relations between the United States and its sister republics.

Shortly before 1 o'clock the President, returning to the White House, held a reception for the visiting members of the conference and their families. At the Pan-American building this evening the visitors will be officially received by the Secretary of State and the Secretary of the Treasury.

Three Cabinet officers—Secretaries McAdoo and Redfield and Postmaster General Burleson—are on the program for the afternoon session of the conference today.

In his address today, Secretary Bryan said:

Like Great Tree.

"We have taken upon ourselves the responsibility of developing this territory for ourselves, and for the world. It is dedicated to a system of government—may I describe these republics as representing a great banyan tree? The United States is the parent stem, but the branches extended have taken root and are now permanent supports of the tree, and parts of that great tree, linked together by identity of political purpose and by a common political ideal, we cannot but be interested in each other. And being here, so situated that we must live side by side, certain expectations are aroused by the fact that we are neighbors.

"The first is that we shall know each other. Neighbors must get acquainted, and this epoch meeting must result in an extension of the acquaintance, which is not only desirable, but necessary.

"The second expectation aroused by the fact that we are neighbors is that we shall be friends. And I am assured that this feeling of friendship is a growing feeling. Time has tended to make it grow, and words spoken by the representatives of the different nations have promoted its growth.

War Hardly Possible.

"And may I refer to one other thing which has had an influence. It is the fact that this nation is now united to the Spanish-speaking republics by treaties that provide that there is no cause for which we shall go to war until after there has been a year's time for investigation, and for passion to subside.

"This country has shown its good will by offering to all these republics, without respect to size, such a treaty, and these republics have responded in the spirit in which the offer was made.

"And we therefore may feel certain, that, in the years to come, there will be no hasty rushing into armed conflict, and we have such faith in the power of reason, when controlled by friendship, that we believe that a year's deliberation will enable us to find peaceful means of settling any dispute that may arise between us."

Eyes Opened.

"The war in Europe has opened our eyes more than anything else to the necessity of strengthening our business interests," said Senor Dr. Ignacio Calderon, the minister from Bolivia, in his brief speech of reply. "Mankind does not advance by selfishness, violence, or hatred," he said later, referring, evidently, to the conflict abroad.

Other speeches dwelling upon the potentialities of the international conference and the prospect of a community of commercial and moral influences among the Americas were made by the chosen representatives of the republics as follows:

Argentina, Senor Dr. Samuel Hale Pearson; Brazil, Senor Dr. Amaro Cavalcanti; Chile, Senor Dr. Luis Izquierdo; Colombia, Dr. Santiago Perez Triana; Costa Rica, Senor Dr. John M. Keith; Cuba, Senor Dr. Pablo Desvernine y Galdos; Dominican Republic, Senor Dr. Francisco J. Peynado; Ecuador, Senor Dr. Juan Cueva Garcia; Guatemala, Senor Dr. Victor Sanchez Ocana; Honduras, Senor Dr. Leopoldo Cordova; Nicaragua, Senor Dr. Pedro Rafael Cuadra; Panama, Senor Dr. Ramon F. Acevedo; Paraguay, Senor Dr. Hector Velasquez; Peru, Senor Dr. Isaac Alzamora; Salvadore, Senor Dr. Alphonso Quinones; Uruguay, Senor Dr. Pedro Cosio; Venezuela, Senor Dr. Pedro Rafael Rincones.

"If you want to know what the United States means to all the Americas," said Dr. Galdos, of Cuba, "look at the Pearl of the Antilles; see what they have done for Cuba." This sentiment was applauded.

Dr. Triana, of Colombia, is suffering from defective eyesight, and President Wilson assisted him down the steps after his address. He made an eloquent address, contrasting the blessings of peace and the horrors of an almost world-wide war now raging.
