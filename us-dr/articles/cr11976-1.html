New Soviet Policy Appeal

When the General Assembly is called to order Tuesday, there will be present in the great hall an impressive roster of the world's most important statesmen.

Each year since 1945, the world's only organized effort for international comity and order has been an impressive occasion when the delegations of more than 80 nations have gathered for the annual session of the General Assembly of the United Nations.

No one present at this "routine" opening session has ever failed to be intrigued by the multilingual, cosmopolitan and universal character of this international parliament of mankind. Even the dread shadow of the hydrogen bomb and the ever-present reality of a power struggle between two blocs armed to the very teeth with nuclear weapons capable of absolute destruction, fade into the realm of science fiction in the reassuring atmosphere of the congregation of nations. The sweepingly-spacious chamber is designed for global endeavour which is at once a testimony to man's confidence in his responsibilities and a continuing revelation of his apparently insurmountable kinship to his Neanderthal ancestors.

When the gavel of the 1959 President of the General Assembly, Dr. Victor Andres Belaunde of Peru, opens the 1960 session at 3 o'clock Tuesday afternoon, there will be present in the great amphitheater the most impressive aggregation of world statesmen that has ever been seen in a single room since the Congress of Vienna in 1815.

In any normal year, the Foreign Ministers of the member nations are in attendance, as heads of their delegations, at the opening sessions of the Assembly. After a few days, depending on the business at hand, these gentlemen return to their capitals, leaving their delegations under the leadership of their permanent representatives (ambassadors) at the U. N.

Year of the Bear

But this year ("the year of the Bear" as one U. N. delegate described it with scant attention to the Chinese calendar), the announced intention of Soviet Premier Khrushchev to head his country's mission to the Assembly sparked a veritable pilgrimage from all over the world to the sprawling glass-and-steel world capital on the shores of New York's East River.

Dr. Belaunde, opening the Tuesday session, will look down from his rostrum on Mr. Khrushchev, Yugoslav President Josip Broz Tito, Cuba's Premier Fidel Castro, Indonesia's President Sukarno, Generalissimo Rafael Trujillo Molina of the Dominican Republic, President Gamal Abdel Nasser of the United Arab Republic, First Secretary Gheorghe Gheorghiu-dej of Romania, Wladyslaw Gomulka, First Secretary of Poland; Antonin Novotny, President of Czechoslovakia; Janos Kadar, Minister of State of Hungary, and many other men noted for their roles in the conduct of world affairs. Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru of India is expected to be present later.

If Dr. Belaunde indulges his penchant for making an appropriate opening speech, he will certainly have a blue-ribbon audience. Indeed, the significant absentees will be few, notably Prime Minister Macmillan of the United Kingdom, President de Gaulle of France, and President Eisenhower. One delegate observed laconically yesterday that he "would not be at all surprised to see Generalissimo Francisco Franco" in the Assembly chamber, even though Franco Spain is not a member of the U. N.

Deeper Interest

Why the interest of these heads-of-state in the 1960 session of the General Assembly?

The answer lies somewhere in the portents of the times, the collapse of the summit conference last June, and the dismaying stiffening of the icicles of the cold war. And it lies even more in the fact that the world today is already across the threshold of a new era—the era of independence and national sovereignty of an almost bewildering roster of new nations which only days, weeks, or months ago were colonial dependencies of European powers.

In the opinion of many able diplomats, Mr. Khrushchev has determined to recover as much as possible his program for an East-West detente and a long period of "peaceful co-existence" which he was forced, in this view, to jettison after the incident of the U-2 United States espionage plane last May.

And he has chosen this time, and this international forum, it is being said, because the addition of so many new nations to the membership roster of the United Nations automatically strengthens his hand and the power position of the "socialist camp" in world affairs.

The applicants for membership in the international organization are impressive in their devotion to "neutralism." None of them are expected to be allied with either of the major power blocs of the cold war—which fact is cited as a major reason for the attendance at the Assembly of President Tito of Yugoslavia and the probable attendance of Prime Minister Nehru.

Neutralist Nations

But even though the new member nations are "neutralist" rather than committed to the Communist bloc, Mr. Khrushchev is believed to feel that their presence in the U. N. will serve inevitably to weaken the domination of the international organization by members of the Western bloc. Although the West will continue to dominate the powerful Security Council for the foreseeable future, the General Assembly itself can act, in accordance with the U. N. Charter, on substantive matters.

Thus the Soviet Premier believes the time ripe for making a major move on the international stage. While it is almost entirely unlikely that he will accomplish any significant success in the field of disarmament or on other outstanding issues of the cold war, he can and probably will establish Soviet policy before the U. N. in such a way as to make a universal appeal to the uncommitted nations that now make up the real balance-of-power in the world forum.

Additionally, the Soviet leader is said to believe that now is the time to demonstrate to the world what he assesses as a major shift in the balance-of-power between East and West. In many comments, conversations and remarks, Mr. Khrushchev has made it clear that his confidence in his Soviet Union and in the "socialist camp" has grown to the point where he feels able to challenge the West at any point along the long line of divergence.

Eisenhower Plans

President Eisenhower plans to address the General Assembly on Thursday, and, the White House said, "will have specific proposals to make to the United Nations delegates at that time". Since Mr. Khrushchev is not scheduled to address the Assembly before Thursday (he is on hand officially as head of the Soviet delegation and therefore must take his proper turn at the rostrum), Mr. Eisenhower will apparently be able to state his case in advance of the Soviet gambit.

Although no details of the President's proposals were being divulged at the White House last week, it is generally expected that he will issue a challenge to Moscow to "do something" rather than continue to talk about disarmament, that he will insist that the U. N. must be the final arbiter in the confusion prevailing in the Congo, and that he will suggest again that the American and Soviet output of fissionable material, such as uranium and plutonium, be either halted or earmarked for "peaceful purposes".

There was nothing new in these forecasts, but many observers were predicting that Mr. Khrushchev would find ample opportunity to get in a few weighty licks in advance of the Eisenhower address. The best and most likely occasion, it appears, will be either on the first or the second day of the Assembly session, when the regular agenda provides that the matter of admission of new nations be considered.

Mr. Khrushchev, as head of the Soviet delegation, will be free to argue the case for the admission of Communist China, certain to be brought up again as it is every year. In the arguing, the Soviet Premier will have all the leeway in the world to make a full-scale speech on world affairs.

Brighter Note

From the Soviet liner Baltika in mid-Atlantic, there came on Friday a few words of reassurance. Mr. Khrushchev, replying to an invitation from the Harvard Law School, regretted that United States orders restricting him to Manhattan during his U. N. stay would prevent his accepting the bid from the Forum of the law school for a lecture. But the Soviet Premier said in his radioed message that he believed "better times will come in the relations between our countries, the sun will rise at last, the dark clouds will scatter, and all people will realize the need for friendship and international co-operation."

The message was given top play on Radio Moscow's international service, and was put on the world wires by Tass, the official Soviet news agency. And in a note to the State Department, the Soviet Union called "for the subordination of personal ill feeling" in the interest of better international relations. Persistent but unconfirmed reports, called "speculative" by the White House, said Mr. Khrushchev had been quietly intimating, through Soviet diplomatic channels, that he would welcome an opportunity to meet with President Eisenhower and presumably "bury the hatchet" in the personal feud existing between the two men since the affair of the U-2 last May.

In London, the word from No. 10 Downing Street was that Prime Minister Macmillan would fly to New York immediately if there was any prospect of serious talks between Mr. Khrushchev and Mr. Eisenhower.
