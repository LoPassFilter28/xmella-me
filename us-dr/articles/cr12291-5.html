The New York Freeman · November 28, 188528 de noviembre de 1885

Original in English.Original en inglés.

HON. JOHN MERCER LANGSTON.

COMMERCIAL RELATIONS BETWEEN SAN DOMINGO AND THE UNITED STATES.

Hon. John M. Langston Invited to Address a Large Gathering at the African Methodist Episcopal Church, San Domingo City, October 28, 1885, upon the Commercial Relations Between San Domingo and the United States and the Commercial Treaty now Pending for Ratification Between the Two Countries.

To the Editor of THE NEW YORK FREEMAN:

Upon the announcement of the arrival in this city of Hon. John Mercer Langston, ex-Minister Resident and Consul General to the government of Hayti and ex-Charge' de Affaires near the Dominican government, the matter of the reciprocal treaty was at once agitated.

Hon. H. C. C. Astwood, United States Consul, was requested to call a meeting impromptu at the A. M. E. Protestant Church and on the night of the 28th inst. one of the largest and most important gatherings ever seen in this city assembled to hear the renowned speaker. At 7 o'clock P. M., the hall was crowded to its uttermost capacity and the sidewalk and street was literally jammed with people. Prominent members of the government and representatives of commercial interest were present. Consul Astwood spoke as follows:

LADIES AND GENTLEMEN:—This is in my judgment the most important meeting ever held in this city. It seems to be the beginning of a great end, the influence of which will doubtless tend to the extension of our commercial relation and the ratification of our commercial treaty now pending. Mr. Langston's visit here is purely accidental, and it might be said providential, being one of our great National leaders and the foremost man of his race in the United States, with the ability and the opportunity to speak, not only to the American people, but to our President, Secretary of State and Senatorial Committee, if necessary, having been identified so long with the diplomatic relations of the countries. For this reason Mr. Langston can speak as no one else can upon this important question. Ladies and gentlemen, my object is not to make a speech, but simply to organize this meeting, so that you might have an opportunity to hear our matchless orator and able diplomat.

I find myself fortunate in being able to prevail upon Mr. Langston to speak. Not anticipating such a call, he was not prepared for a task of such vast magnitude, but Mr. Langston is a patriot and a lover of progress and has consented to make an off hand talk, and I know you will be pleased to hear him. Without further remarks I will state that I am requested to call the Hon. Lucas Gibbs, Minister of Finance and Commerce of the Dominican Republic, to preside over the meeting, and ex-Administrator of Customs and ex-member of the National Congress, Hon. Richard Alexander Gross, as secretary and the following vice-presidents: Hon. J. W. Farrand, U. S. Vice Consul; Hon. J. M. Leyber, Consul for the Netherlands and Acting B. V. Consul; Hon. J. B. Vicini, merchant and planter; Hon. Alexander Bass, plumber; Hon. J. M. Calera, Dominican citizen; Hon. George Davis, engineer; Hon. M. L. Pardo, Agent Maritime Exchange, New York; Mr. C. H. Williams, Secretary of the A. M. E. Church; Mr. F. Claudia, Trustee; Isaiah Hamilton, Trustee.

Mr. Gibbs, upon taking the chair, introduced the speaker of the evening, the Hon. John Mercer Langston. Mr. Langston rose and said in substance:

LADIES AND GENTLEMEN:—I have been called upon during my life time to make a great many speeches, but I do not remember ever being surprised in a manner like this. To treat a question of so much importance, it needs time and reflection; but I am not an apologist; I am an American citizen, and I am proud of being an American citizen. As such, I am always actuated by considerations of patriotism, whenever the interest of my country and the welfare of men in general are involved; therefore I will attempt to speak briefly upon the commercial relations and the reciprocal treaty.

But I may not begin without complimenting great men who are in part the fathers of this great and heroic Republic, with whose history I have made myself familiar. The inspirer of your independence, Duarte, and the heroic Sanches, Mello, Santana and others who realized the great work and then your restorers, Luperon, Palanco and Lafit, from among the heroes of Capotilla, Henreaux, Marino and Gil of the New Era, whose fame is known abroad. I am proud of all these men and from what I have heard and see to-night, I feel you voice the liberal and noble sentiments of these dead and living heroes in the utterances of this meeting.

I am possitively interested in the commercial and reciprocal treaty pending for ratification between the Republics of the United States of America and San Domingo.

My relations with the Dominican Government and that of the Republic of Hayti; serving at the former for more than two years as Charge' de Affaires, and quite eight years as Minister Resident and Consul General, has made it possible for me to be somewhat familiar with the commercial needs of these two young Republics, and I would like to see them so identified with the United States, commercially, as to render their their future prosperous and happy.

I consider the United States the greatest country upon the globe. I do do not say this from egotism or false pride, but it is a fact. The progress of our country has been wonderful, wonderful because the voice of the people is sovereign. Political parties have no significance in our country only in so far as they obey the will of the people and contribute to the general prosperity of all its citizens.

We have just given to the world an example never before recorded by any nation or any people, and it is worthy of imitation by all the Republics of the earth. We have recently chosen a Chief Magistrate under the fundamental principles of our unalterable Constitution, preserved for more than one hundred years as handed down to us by the fathers.

The two great parties were contending for the supremacy. The contest was intense and rigid and vexed in the extreme. The one party made strong by twenty-four years of power; it had endeared itself to the people by the great principles it had enunciated; it had the wealth of the country within its grasp; the army and navy were in its hands; it had the entire civil service and all of the machinery of the government; but it chose an unfortunate candidate as its standard-bearer, and on election day the people rebuked the pretensions of those who would force upon the party a candidate not in keeping with its time-honored name and character. The candidate was rejected, not the party, by the smallest vote possible, relieving it of power in the executive branch of the government. But there was no conflict, or tumult, or revolution, or uprising of the people, no pretensions by the party in power to seize the reins of the government upon technical objections, but a quiet resignation to the will of the people and the surrender of the government to the victors without a jar in our government affairs.

I have had the pleasure of shaking hands with President Cleveland, a typical American, a man who can stand above party prejudices, in administering the affairs of his great office. It is my desire to see that all of the republics upon this continent imitate the great Republic of the United States in this respect. And to do this I would have the United States to extend its strong arms and draw all of these republics toward it as a great centre of moral influence and power. San Domingo, Hayti, Venezuela and all of the South American Republics; not to divest them of their sovereignty and nationalities, but to make them commercially great and prosperous, so that the commercial and diplomatic relations of our continent be American in triumph as against European competition. And to this end I would have a foreign diplomatic and consular policy with these countries broad enough to comprehend all of this in celebrating such treaties as the one now pending between the Dominican Republic and the United States.

We want your products, and in exchange we want you to have ours exclusive of all others upon a reciprocal basis. Your country is ripe for this and there is no better time to reach a good understanding with the United States thereupon than to-day.

A new administration has just been inaugurated and it is about to construct and enunciate its foreign policy and I am certain that it will be especially interested in a measure like this, having such vast results within its campass.

Your country is largely undeveloped and needs help. It is yet in its infancy and one who has not visited it can scarely conceive the extent of the vast probable commercial field that might be developed; not only your forests of valuable woods, yet unexplored, for want of transportation facilities, but your immense virgin fields not now approachable for the same reason and for the lack of capital and labor.

On my way up here, as the steamboat plied in easy distance of your beautiful coast, my attention was called to an immense field of cane. It was a grand sight. The eye could scarcely take in the immense sugar works. These could be seen in a distance, the tall chimneys of which reaching far up above the landscape and the fields. The pretty out houses and the miles of railroad, reaching from the beautiful “Finka Italia” down to the coast,—it all looks like progress. On reaching the city, I wanted to see the owner of this enterprise. My friend, the consul said to me, “come, and I will show you the man who never tires, but is always at his desk, night and day, not even excusing the Sabbath to rest.” I was taken into the presence of a big fine tooking Italian, working away at his desk. He is the owner of six other estates and I saw so many other things to please that I have become especially interested in your people. The business like appearance of your city, the order and decorum observed in your public offices, the fine appearance of your public men, etc., all these pleased me.

I called with Mr. Astwood, the consul, at the home of your great diplomat, Mr. Galvan. who signed the treaty with Mr. Frelinghuysen. I saw in him a great man, with large and broad ideas as to the requirements of his country. I also saw your young and handsome President, General O'Alexander Woz y Gil, and a great many of his Ministers, and I was greatly pleased with all I saw.

When I look around me here and see this large gathering, and the representatives of such large interests it makes me feel that you are in earnest about this treaty matter, and I shall take great pleasure to impress this upon the officials of my government encharged with this important subject, who I know will listen to me because the subject is worthy of profound, serious consideration and action.

It would be vain to attempt to give in full Mr. Langston's great speech. He spoke for more than an hour, dealing in facts and figures, showing the necessity, importance and probable advantages resulting to both countries concerned. He was listened to with rapted attention, every sentiment expressed by him being received by enthusiastic applause and approval. It was a great speech and the effect will be long remembered.

J. B. Vicini, Esq., the largest sugar planter on the island, rose and in a neat speech complemented Mr. Langston for his great effort and the good it would do to the general interest here.

Hon. J. M. Leyber, consul for the Netherlands and acting British vice-consul, congratulated Mr. Langston and said: “I am pleased with your eloquent speech and its good effect, although I am not a planter, yet the realization of this enterprise has my full sympathy and support.”

Alexander Bass, Esq., an American citizen, a large and experienced planter and president of the Pioneer Iron Works, at Brooklyn, New York, also complimented Mr. Langston, and said that words failed him to express his thanks and gratitude to the speaker for his noble effort in behalf of the sugar and other interest here, but that he would always have the good will of the entire commonwealth for his great work.

Mr. Gross, the secretary, took Mr. Langston's hand and said: “As a Dominican, I cannot fail to shake your hand and thank you in behalf of my people for the good things you have said in honor of the great men of my country and the general prosperity.” Mr. Gross made a happy speech in Spanish which was well received.

Consul Astwood, for the benefit of the many Dominicans present, not understanding the English language, interpreted Mr. Langston's speech in a happy, effective manner, it being received in the most approving earnest way. After a unanimous vote of thanks to Mr. Langston, the choir sang a choice selection, whereupon the meeting adjourned with a general hand shakings and congratulations to the speaker. Mr. Langston has endeared himself to the people here and will be long remembered.

R. MEJANDRO GROSS, Secretary.

San Domingo City, Nov. 1.
