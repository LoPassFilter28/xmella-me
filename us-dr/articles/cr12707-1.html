Dominican President Goes Mr. Coolidge One Better

By EVELYN PEYTON GORDON

Well, once we had a President who "did not choose to run." For a good many years that pronouncement of the late Calvin Coolidge has been rather unique. Presidents today don't so easily give up the job. But down in the Dominican Republic another man has made the same statement—only in many more words.

The other day Rafael Trujillo Molina, who has served two terms as President and dictator of the island republic, announced to "the nation" that he "would renounce public office in order to enjoy once more the peaceful quiet of private life." Now there's a smart man. He has had all the glory—if there be any—in twice serving as President of his country. He has—according to all accounts—accomplished many things during his eight-year tenure of office. Even the capital of the republic is now called Trujillo City—Ciudad Trujillo in Spanish. But unlike Mr. Coolidge, who merely wrote those six historical words on a slip of paper. Mr. Trujillo delivered a 12-page speech to his fellow citizens.

WILL RETAIN POWER

So on Aug. 16, Strongman Trujillo will retire from office. He will retire to his fabulous home in the mountains near Ciudad Trujillo "to the peaceful quiet of private life." But the bets are even that he will still remain the strong man of Dominican politics.

Here in Washington we know a good deal about Mr. Trujillo. Some years back there came to the Capital a pretty brunet, smartly gowned and gay of smile—Mme. Trujillo. She was officially received at the White House as the wife of a foreign president. She was officially called upon by State Department big-wigs. And then she settled down to enjoy the remainder of her stay—it was some weeks—as the guest of Mr. and Mrs. Joseph E. Davies. At the time Mr. Davies, who is now our Ambassador to Brussels, was a local attorney and among other things, counsel to the President of the Dominican Republic—that was Mme. Trujillo's husband.

Now Mr. Trujillo has another wife and so has Mr. Davies, but there is still a close friendship between the two families. Mrs. Aldace Walker, a daughter of Mr. Davies, spent some weeks in Trujillo City and was thrilled with the charm of the place. Of course she stayed at the Presidential Palace, and that does make a trip just a little different.

MINISTER RETURNING

Today the Minister of the Dominican Republic, Andres Pastoriza, will return from New York where he has been to speed his wife and family on their way to Trujillo City—the Minister himself forgets sometimes, and speaks of Santo Domingo City, but hastily corrects himself. And on his return Mr. Pastoriza will begin to make plans for an imminent visit of the erstwhile President.

Just when Mr. Trujillo will come to this country is not yet known, but it will be some time shortly after his retirement. He has never been here and is probably agog at the thrilling tales his wife and Ministers have brought back to him. So Mr. Pastoriza is preparing to "give him the works"; take him thru the country on a coast-to-coast tour; show him how things are run in our modest little republic.

Meantime down in the Dominican Republic there is none of the uproar that would be attendant to the emphatic retirement of our Chief Executive. There is no scurrying around looking for two noble candidates to fight, fight, fight, for the coveted office.

While President Roosevelt has been touring the country dropping a word here and a smile there by way of endorsement for his candidates for various public offices, Mr. Trujillo simply said "to the nation" that he wished to recommend to his fellow citizens the mutual advantages of unifying electoral action by nominating Dr. J. B. Peynado for the Presidency. The retiring Chief Executive continued "This is my candidate and as such I recommend him favorably to my fellow citizens."

HE'S QUITE FRANK

No beating around the bush there. Mr. Trujillo just told 'em and they went to work and elected Mr. Peynado, a distinguished lawyer. Didn't even bother to nominate an opponent. Simple as that.

Now, of course, Mr. Trujillo may return to Trujillo City after his visit in this country, so filled with new ideas of government—many of the Latin American republics have been patterned after ours—that he will want to execute them himself again. That remains to be seen four years from now when Mr. Peynado has done his job.
