Caribbean Feud Blame Laid to Three States By Rio Pact Probers

By the Associated Press

Three Latin American countries were accused last night by a five-nation committee of full blame for a heated feud which for months has shaken the Caribbean area.

A fact-finding group set up by the Organization of American States charged Cuba, Guatemala and the Dominican Republic with responsibility for a series of plots, conspiracies and attempted invasions.

The committee cleared Haiti—which also had been involved in the charges and countercharges—and bluntly recommended stiff action under the Rio mutual defense treaty if necessary to prevent further plotting by the other three nations.

The investigators also suggested creation of a five-man watchdog committee to keep a continuing check on the situation.

The committee submitted an 18,000-word report to the council of the Organization of American States—the central body of the 21 American nations, which meets at the Pan American Union.

Inquiry Ordered January 11.

The committee was appointed on January 11 by the OAS council to investigate the whole Caribbean situation. That was after Haiti complained that the neighboring Dominican government participated in a plot to overthrow her government.

The Dominican Republic immediately retaliated by hurling similar charges against Cuba, Guatemala and Haiti. Both countries asked that the RIO mutual defense treaty be invoked.

Exasperated by several fruitless attempts to deal with the problem and faced with loss of prestige, the council unanimously set the treaty in motion and appointed a fact-finding committee.

Headed by Jose Mora of Uruguay, the committee members were Paul C. Daniels of the United States, Guillermo Gutierrez of Bolivia, Alfonso Moscoso of Ecuador and Eduardo Zuleta Angel of Colombia.

In general, the committee found that Dominican officials and particularly Anselmo Paulino, a self-styled close friend of President Trujillo, not only tolerated but also gave aid to Haitian conspirators.

Aided by Cuba.

Also the committee declared that armed groups of exiles and nationals—popularly known as the "Caribbean Legion"—had been given aid and facilities by government officials of Cuba and Guatemala for the purpose of overthrowing President Trujillo.

The committee also stated that the facts alleged to the Cuban, Guatemalan and Dominican governments are "contrary" to various inter-American pacts binding nations to prevent revolutionary activities on their territory aimed at others.

In a frank effort to keep the peace in the Caribbean, the group recommended:

1. That the council warn that any repetition of the disturbances "would give place to application of the procedures of the Rio treaty to protect the principle of non-intervention and to guarantee the inviolability or integrity of the territory and political independence of any American State."

2. That the Dominican government take "immediate and effective steps to prevent official functionaries from tolerating, instigating, stimulating, aiding or fomenting subversive and seditious movements against other governments."

Asks Strict Compliance.

3. That the Dominican government give strict compliance to the joint declaration of 1949 with Haiti.

4. That the Cuban and Guatemalan governments take "adequate steps so there will not exist in their territory groups of nationals or foreigners, militarily organized, for the purpose of conspiring against the security of other countries."

5. That the two governments take "effective steps to control war materiel in the hands of revolutionary elements and to avoid illegal arms traffic."

The committee broke up its report into two main sections. The first deals with Haiti's charges that the Dominican government "effectively participated" with Haitian conspirators in a plot to overthrow her government last November-December.

The second relates to the Dominican Republic's charges that Haiti, Cuba and Guatemala gave aid and assistance to the "Caribbean Legion."

Finding in First Case.

In the first case, the committee found:

1. That the Dominican government did not take the "necessary steps to prevent Astrel Roland and Alfred Viau from carrying out on its territory activities designed to alter the peace of Haiti in conformity with the joint declaration of 1949." In the latter pact the two countries agreed anew to prevent revolutionary activities on their soil aimed at one another. Roland and Viau are Haitian exiles living in the Dominican Republic. Roland was fired from the Haitian government two years ago in the aftermath of a plot which Haiti said was aimed at her and involved President Trujillo.

2. That "some Dominican authorities not only tolerated the activities of Astrel Roland but also gave assistance in the conspiracy of November and December."

3. That "this co-operation and assistance was given principally through the intermediary of Anselmo Paulino." The committee said Paulino described himself as an "intimate friend" of President Trujillo.
