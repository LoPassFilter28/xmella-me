Watch on the Potomac

Washington.—THE MISTREATMENT OF MR. NIXON—Vice President Nixon has had a rough time on his tour of Latin America. First, a big American meat-packing company (Armour) tried to get Nixon to intervene with Uruguayan officials to have one of their large plants returned after it was seized by the government. This plea came in an open letter and embarrassed the Vice President.

Then came the unfortunate episode in Lima, Peru where a group of so-called students pelted Nixon with stones until he was forced to cancel his visit at San Marcos University.

Editorial writers have already made the point that this latter episode was probably inspired by young Communists. But it's a little more than that.

There is no doubt that the popular attitude in many Latin American countries towards this nation has hardened. Many Latins no longer share the "Good Neighbor" feelings of an earlier era.

Nixon's tour of Latin America was undertaken because the State Dept. has come to realize that we have been taking for granted the unity of the Western Hemisphere against Soviet imperialism. We have blandly assummed that all Latins, being strong individualists, would automatically be on our side and against Communist totalitarianism.

That's where it turns out we may be very wrong.

The Communists have made serious inroads in several Latin countries we are told, because our government has deluded itself into thinking that when it gets pledges of support from Dictators like Batista in Cuba or Trujillo in the Dominican Republic it has won the affection of the people they govern.

HIGH TARIFFS AND BAD RELATIONS — If Communists have been able to make any headway in Latin America, there must be some reason for it. If people are fully employed, well-fed and happy Communist propaganda isn't likely to make much impression. But if they are miserable they'll listen to demagogues of any stripe—the Perons or the Latin disciples of Khruschev.

That seems to be the situation in Peru and at least a part of the difficulty can be traced to the American lead and zinc industry. We don't want to oversimplify the problem, because many U.S. mine workers in these industries are now out of work themselves, hard hit by falling prices.

On the other hand, we doubt that the American workers will go back to work or that Peruvians will be re-employed if the government yields to demands from the industry for a sharp increase on duties of lead and zinc exports.

Nixon may not appreciate this fact but the stoning he took in Peru is merely a long distance reaction to the U.S. recession. The government has stopped stock-piling lead and zinc. Now the U.S. auto industry, which uses 31 per cent of all the lead processed in this country in storage batteries has also cut down sharply.

So in a way it can be said Mr. Nixon's troubles are "Made in Detroit."
