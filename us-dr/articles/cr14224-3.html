THE SAN DOMINGO JOB.

The Negotiations for the Purchase of the Island and its Debt.

Correspondence of the Cincinnati Commercial.

So far as I can learn, this San Domingo business is a job and a swindle. I suppose that at any time in the last fifty years we could have made treaties with the transient, corrupt governments of these revolutionary half-breeds of the Indies, or Central or South America, and such treaties would probably give us the right of enforcement through arms. But whether their possession so obtained would pay, is further along. The wiser heads controlling our government heretofore have had such tempting offers made them, but, counting the cost, they have concluded that the additional territory would come to more than it was worth. We can ratify this treaty, based as it is said, upon about two thousand votes—and the Devil himself knows how many of these are bogus, and how many represent genuine citizens—but for the rest of the enlightened population, we should have to reduce them with the bayonet, and, after materially increasing our already unpayable indebtedness, at the end of half a century find ourselves possessed of another State calling for continued reconstruction and armed occupation.

It seems that this affair originated with our great real estate agent, Mr. Soward, who began negotiating for the purchase of the Bay of Samana. Baez proposed to sell to diplomatic Billy very much as the devil offered large quantities of real estate to our Saviour, Baez's title being about as good as that of his Satanic Majesty. Diplomatic William was more cautious and far shrewder than the venerable sturgeon we now have in the State Department. Looking into the constitution under which Baez held office, he found in Article 7 that all sale or alienation of territory, or any part thereof, was prohibited; and on the statute books, a law passed by the Congress of the Republic in 1867, which declared that man a traitor to his country who should propose a sale or alienation of any part of the soil.

On the 29th of November last a treaty was signed for the annexation of San Domingo to the United States, without the people of San Domingo knowing anything whatever about it. It is provided for in the treaty that the people of San Domingo "shall, in the shortest possible time, express in a manner conformable to the laws, their will concerning the cession herein provided for; and the United States shall, until such expression shall be had, protect the Dominican Republic against foreign intervention."

The foreign intervention here referred to means a powerful party, headed by Cabral, that does not favor such annexation. And it is believed that it will, so far as the present Administration is concerned, successfully suppress any such movement. We have not only given the protection proposed, but there has been an active intervention in the struggle going on. The President and Mr. Secretary Fish have contributed $150,000 without any authority from Congress to the Baez party, and favored our protege with ships of war, and artillery and other material. Admiral Poor has notified the Haytiens who are friends of Cabral that they must do nothing whatever toward aiding that party. This is a precious piece of business. While we are throwing members-elect to Congress out of their seats because of intimidation of voters, here is the government calling for a popular expression from the people of San Domingo, while Admiral Poor, with his shotted guns, warns them off.

Secretary Fish appears in magnificent contrast to his cunning predecessor. He takes no precaution whatever to protect the government from swindlers before signing two treaties, one for the leasing of Samana for fifty years at an annual rent of $150,000, and the other for the annexation of the Republic to the United States.

THE DEBT.

By the terms of this treaty, I am assured that, in addition to the payment of over a million, the government of the United States assumes the indebtedness of the republic. To exhibit this in a clear light, we have only to refer to the report of Ricardo Curiel, Minister of the Dominican Treasury, made to President Baez, dated March 29, 1869, and published in the Bulletin Official of the Republic (No. 63), of the 8th day of May, 1869.

Conformably to the dates, says the Minister, which are found registered in the General Accounting office of the Treasury, there was in circulation in this Territory on the 1st of February, 1868, paper money to the amount of $2,628,300, issued as follows: Under the government of the Protectorate (decree of the Assembly Constituent of 22d October, 1865)............ $100,000.00 Under the administration of Baez, conformably to decree of National Congress, of 12th of March, 1866............ 100,000.00 Under decree of April of same year............ 100,000.00 Under the Triumvirate, decree of subdelegation of 29th July, 1866............ 200,000.00 Under the administration of Cabral, emission of 31st of May, 1867............ 250,000.00 Bank bills issued August, 1867............ 100,000.00 Emission of bank notes, 30th of October, 1867............ 143,500.00 Total............ $998,500.00 Emission of notes, November 30, 1867............ 1,629,800.00 Total............ $2,628,300.00

The Minister of the Treasury in the same report specifies divers other debts, which are called the National Loan, amounting to............ 117,395.90 Due bills (vales) [see doc. No. 6]............ 57,032.54 Drafts drawn by the general accounting officers of the Treasury............ 10,126.35 Due bills issued by the general accounting office under convention of November 27, 1867............ 29,265.50 Other due bills............ 5,465.75 Total............ $2,947,898.34

To this sum must be added due bills drawn by the Treasury............ $5,102.15 The Minister then speaks of another class of debt, called public debt, which was incurred in the war of the Restoration; the total of this he places, January 31, 1869, at............ 315,400.00

"From the 1st of February, 1868," the same officer continues, "the country was overwhelmed with an immense debt, and this was the case, without taking into account the salaries of public officers, due and unpaid, and the expenses incurred during the revolution which brought Baez back into power. Other due bills had to be issued, which, by the by, gave a greater guarantee to commerce."

Due to Jessurun & Son............ 14,000.00 Other debts to same parties............ 39,000.00

The Minister then alludes to the loan made by Hartmont, Herzberg & Co. to the government for £1,995,000, less commission, £100,000 at $4.75: £475,000, net $1,520,000.

This loan is to run for twenty-five years, and 8 per cent interest is to be paid upon the same. To pay the interest and provide a sinking fund £56,900 per annum are required.
