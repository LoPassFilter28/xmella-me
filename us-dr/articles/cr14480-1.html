CAPTAIN GREENO'S STORY.

A Timely Rescue When Threatened by Dominican Desperadoes.

[New York Times.]

According to the story told by Captain W. H. Greeno, who formerly commanded the Nova Scotian brig Martha J. Brady, and who with his wife, mate, and six seamen arrived on the steamer Saginaw yesterday, the protection of American men-of-war is needed somewhere else than in Hayti. Captain Greeno left Annapolis, Nova Scotia, in the Martha J. Brady, bound for the West Indies. On the night of October 28th the brig was blown ashore on the southeast coast of San Domingo at a place called San Luis Petit Trou.

The vessel had struck a reef and lay in a very dangerous position, so Captain Greeno got his wife and crew into the boat and succeeded in getting ashore. They had only the clothes on their backs and a few articles and shipstores they could lay their hands on. Next morning, after passing a night exposed to the elements, Captain Greeno saw that it would be possible to reach the stranded brig and obtain from it the effects of the crew, the sails, and other property. A boat went over to her and succeeded in getting a quantity of useful things.

On its return and while the Captain was engaged in putting up a tent to shield the party somewhat from the rays of the tropical sun a band of ferocious-looking Dominicans, armed to the teeth, descended on the camp. The crew grasped what arms they had and warned their piratical-looking visitors to keep at a respectful distance. There were about fifty men in the armed party, and they might easily have slaughtered the handful of sailors, but their respect for Anglo-Saxon bravery kept them at a distance, while one of Captain Greeno's crew, who was a Spaniard, held a parley with them.

The commander of the desperadoes—a tall, rascally-looking fellow of Spanish descent, whose waist was garnished with revolvers and Bowie knives, while he carried in his arms a long, murderous-looking rifle—said that he would give Captain Greeno until the following morning to decide what he would do. If in that time all the property of the party—money, watches, and anything else of any value—were not given up to the armed band they would forthwith proceed to kill all hands and take what they could lay their hands on.

Captain Greeno resolved to fight, but thought also that he could do so to a better advantage if he had some help. He saw a brig anchored about four miles distant, and to this he sent the mate and one of the crew for help. The brig's captain, with four of his men returned, fully armed, and the next morning the band of Dominicans, seeing that Captain Greeno had been reinforced, left the neighborhood.

The Captain took his wife without further difficulty to a small village near by, and from there word was sent to San Domingo City for a vessel to bring the shipwrecked party to that port. A schooner arrived in a few days and all hands reached San Domingo safely, where they embarked on the Saginaw.
