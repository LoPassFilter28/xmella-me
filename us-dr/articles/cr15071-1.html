Agrarian Reform or Death

HAVANA, Cuba.—There is a sense of waiting here which strikes one who is no expert in the Caribbean, but who knows enough of its past and present to feel its moods and gauge its tempo.

It still is the great and beautiful city. Its night life awaits the tourists as of yore, and if they are not here in the same old numbers there is no reason why they should not be.

But, to one who watched out the Machado Revolution of 1933, and who has looked in during the intervening years, this one of Fidel Castro's presents a different face. His situation may be worsening. But whether that is temporary or not remains to be seen. It astonishes one, for example, to see Havana, a city given over to the tourist and the gentle art of extracting his fresh money, putting so much emphasis on tractors and reform. But it is. There are tractors on many corners outside the heart of the city.

And there are banners proclaiming "Reforma Agraria o Muerte." This is a banner with a strange device compared with those of the past. Then the phrase had been "Liberty or Death."

But, if one buys the newspapers and puzzles out the Spanish with a mind rusty from little use of that language, one finds the officials have noted that difference. There is liberty, they say. But they declare with the fervor of Latin oratory and writing that agrarian reform is the revolution, and that without it liberty cannot endure.

The fundamental ills of the new republic, created in 1902, with its overtones of a colonial system, produced the Machado and Batista regimes, says the Castro revolution. This time there will be an immediate move to overturn the old obstacles.

They cite many. One will serve. There are, for example, 780 large farms and ranches. These alone control almost 20 per cent of all the arable land in Cuba.

So, Castro has moved. There is angry insistence that this is not a Communist-type plan. There is emphatic denial that there is a Communist anywhere in the government. They point a finger at the statistics which they do not hide—namely, that 10 per cent of the people are unemployed and that 50 per cent of the workers and farmers live in worse conditions than an unemployed worker in the United States.

This condition could not be hidden insofar as the Cubans are concerned. But this government flings it out as a part of its justification and its irritation with critics.

So the visitor who returns to a land he has always liked feels the currents of it. This is a new sort of revolution. It has its roots in the rural areas and with the masses of the people. It is not a revolution of cities and troops. The soldiers, who had no heart to fight for Batista, are themselves, for the most part, sons of these same farmers and unemployed. The unemployed of the city contained some Communist elements. How much, none may say for sure. But they are outnumbered by the many thousands of peasants who have for so many years been bypassed and without a voice.

It would be most unwise, I believe, to underestimate this weight in the scales. The revolution is worsening insofar as its economics are concerned. And the businessmen, and the big exporters of sugar and tobacco, are unhappy. The millionaire Cuban exiles in Miami are without question plotting and privately urging Trujillo of the Dominican Republic to become tough. The city may be growing disenchanted. But, the observer does a little arithmetic. If the 200-or-so owners of large cattle ranches are enemies, there are ten times that number made staunch followers by the breaking up of the huge holdings into small farms.

But, still, there must be money to run a country. And there is not enough of it here to carry out all the needed measures to alleviate unemployment in the cities. The Castro revolution is not yet won or lost.
