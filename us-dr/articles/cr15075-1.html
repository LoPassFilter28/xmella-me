The Telegraph-Courier · July 12, 191712 de julio de 1917

Original in English.Original en inglés.

AMERICAN TOURISTS THRONG CARIBBEAN WONDERLANDS Former European Travellers Find Rival to the Mediterranean; Marvels of the Cool Table Lands of the American Tropics; Magnificent Old Spanish Architecture; Strange Prehistoric Ruins; Modern Capitals Humming With Activity Are Among the Unique and Novel Sights of the Picturesque Countries Fringing the Caribbean Sea.

By HAMILTON M. WRIGHT.

The splendors of the universe, the glories of the world are this year unfolded to American travellers in the picturesque wonderlands that fringe the Caribbean sea. But it is a new world in a new universe that they find. No more marvelous changes of climes, scenes and customs can be imagined than those which greet the tourist, business agent or seeker after health who, from the snow and rigors of an American winter, plunges into the luxuriant semi-tropical environment that characterizes the former colonies of Spain at her zenith.

It is a new chapter from a new book. Modernly equipped buildings, broad paved streets, pure water systems, electric lighting systems, movies, theaters, automobiles, and good hotels are features of the Latin-America of today. With an observance of sanitation and new standards of hygiene, the cities with their all year out-door life are among the healthiest in the world.

And the Caribbean has its temper-ate zone. The beautiful table lands and mountain regions form almost a half circle rimming the Caribbean sea upon the south and west. In the higher reaches the air is cool, even cold sometimes, at night and you will be uncomfortable without a warm coverlet. The snow-capped Sierras to be seen from the Santa Marta foothills, nearby, are taller than the loftiest peaks of the Alps. In a short day you can ride from Santa Marta on a mule to the "Cincinnati" coffee plantation, elevation 4,500 feet. Three hours more and you reach an elevation of 9,500 feet. For hundreds of miles extends the glorious panorama of the snow-covered Sierra Nevadas of South America. Some of these peaks reach heights of 1,800 feet or more. Here is a vision for Americans well worth seeing. The Colombian foothills abound with flowering trees and shrubs all abloom at this season.

So, too, on the eastern fringe of the Caribbean will the traveller find temperate plateaus, as, for example, the elevated table lands of Guatemala and of Costa Rica, the Switzerland of the American tropics. No more wonderful railroad journey in the world can be had than that from Port Limon across Costa Rica to San Jose, the capital city. From a luxuriant profusion of tropical growths the railroad ascends often by mile-high canyons to the balmy table lands. For miles and miles one revels in the glories of a virgin forest at its best. The line, 103 miles in length, was built by an American, Mr. Minor C. Keith, a great constructive executive in the development of the American tropics. San Jose, the capital, is a modern city, elevation 3,800 feet. A feature is the Teatro Nacional (National theatre), a gem of architecture, modelled, in part, after the Grand Opera House in Paris. It cost nearly $1,000,000 in gold. Guatemala City, the capital of Guatemala, has a population of 100,000. It is five thousand feet above sea level. Its climate is fine and varies little throughout the year.

And these countries are near at hand! One reaches them far more easily and cheaply than the Mediterranean was reached by tourists before the war. One may reach Colombia in a little over a week from New York, stopping at Jamaica and the Panama Canal on the way. Get aboard a steamer and try a trip to the American tropics. Finest accommodations may be had. The Mediterranean steamers could not excel them. Take a United Fruit boat, for instance, one of the "Great White Fleet," the largest fleet of steamers flying the American flag. The boats are, literally, floating palaces. They are built especially for tropical travel. Their spacious cabins are cooled with refrigerated air. Fine a la carte meals are included with the ticket and by many routes, the steamers of the fleet reach practically every port along the Caribbean sea.

The American tourist finds that the slogan "See America First" may well include the tropical Americas, discovered by Columbus and his followers. Here at Cartagena, once called "Queen of the Indies," fortified by King Philip II of Spain at a cost of $59,000,000, a fabulous sum in those days, one finds the finest example of a fortified city of the seventeenth century to be found in the new world. Founded in 1533, once it was sacked by Sir Francis Drake. Behind its heavy walls that face the water on three points of the compass, rise its great churches and picturesque public edifices, tinted in pink and blue, and brown and pale yellow and gleaming under the brilliant tropical sun like some mystic city of the far Orient. Here, too, is heard the hum of the auto, for Cartagena is crowded with machines.

Santa Marta, a short distance along the Colombian coast, is the oldest city still standing on either of the American continents. In age it is excelled only by San Domingo in the Dominican republic on the Island of Hayti. No town was more pillaged by pirates. And between Santa Marta and Cartagena is Barranquilla, the bustling export city of Colombia, located in the delta of the mighty Magdalena river that flows northward for 1,000 miles through the great republic. Giant dug-outs burned hollow from huge trees and capable of carrying a score or more of persons, sail the river and lagoons with great bellying sails. Swarms of lazy crocodiles lie sunning on the sand banks. Flocks of parrots and brilliantly colored birds that both sing and whistle are seen in almost every woodland and thicket.

Compared to New York in summer time the Caribbean lands are cool. The mean average temperature, throughout the West Indies, at sea level is 78.8; the maximum 87.5; the minimum 70.8, a range of 16.7. At 5,000 feet above sea level the mean average is 62.4, cooler than many city offices. The maximum at this elevation is 68.8 degrees Farenheit; the minimum 56.8.

Thus it is in the American tropics Americans are more and more finding new goals of interest. Whether it be in the capitals with their architectural monuments of old Spanish rule, amid ancient ruins, as the old evidences of the Mayan civilization in Guatemala, or in the busy sea-ports which are in close touch with the United States, the Caribbean lands exert an irresistible lure.
