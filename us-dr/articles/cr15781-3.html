In an exchange printed by the paper, a Dominican speaker pleads for recognition by the United States, calling the enjoyment of American favor essential to Dominica's stability and peace and hoping that the proclamation of general liberty in the United States had removed the obstacles to recognition. Secretary Seward replies by recounting how the United States established itself as a republic on the principle of the equality of men while African slavery still existed under state laws.En un intercambio publicado por el periódico, un orador dominicano suplica el reconocimiento de Estados Unidos, y califica el disfrute del favor estadounidense de esencial para la estabilidad y la paz de Dominica, con la esperanza de que la proclamación de la libertad general en Estados Unidos hubiera eliminado los obstáculos para el reconocimiento. El secretario Seward responde relatando cómo Estados Unidos se estableció como república sobre el principio de la igualdad de los hombres mientras la esclavitud africana aún existía bajo las leyes estatales.

Daily National Republican · February 6, 18666 de febrero de 1866

Original in English.Original en inglés.

President Baez said: I was in France; I came to America on my way to my country; I heard that the Spanish rule was cast off and that the Republic was proclaimed and successfully established by the people in arms. I was prepared to accept, and heard with satisfaction the condition of things. General Serrano had been proclaimed President (provisional President). I was prepared to acquiesce in his administration. The people considered again, and General Pimental was chosen Provisional President. I was ready to acquiesce in that. While I still remained abroad the people considered again, and called General Cabral to the presidency. I was content with that arrangement; but there came a declaration of the popular will calling me to the presidency; that call was sustained by General Serrano, General Pimental, and General Cabral, and it came in a form so solemn that I was obliged to regard it as the will of the people. I caused the Congress of the Republic to be convened. They have ratified that desire, and I have accepted the administration under the guarantees of organic law, and with a view of cementing the harmony of the Government I have invited General Pimental and General Cabral to be members of my cabinet. I am still holding a similar trust for General Serrano, which I hope will be accepted by him

Mr. Seward. President, your policy seems to me to have been eminently judicious. I trust that it will prove as successful as it was manifestly wise.

President Baez said: I hope that all parties will acquiesce in a complete return of peace and order. But it would complete our good fortune and gives us the only guarantee that remains to consolidate the Government, if the great Republic of North America would give us its recognition. When Dominica separated, in 1824, from Hayti, many other States recognized Dominica; but the United States, for reasons, doubtless, of their own, have never recognized Dominica. The enjoyment of their favor is essential to our stability and peace, and I have always thought it a hardship that it could not be secured Now, however, since I learn that the United States have proclaimed general liberty for all classes of men, I hope that the obstacles which have prevented our recognition may be deemed to have been removed.

Mr. Seward. President, when the United States separated from Great Britain and established themselves an independent nation, they organized themselves into a Republic, and laid for its foundation the principle of the equality of men, a principle with which any form of human slavery is incompatible. But African slavery existed in the country; and while in theory and in form our condition was one based upon the equality of all the people of the nation, slavery nevertheless existed, and had guarantees in local or State laws. The first great effort of the friends of freedom and humanity was to eliminate slavery, with its influences in every form, from the Federal Republic, and to give to the nation the character and policy of a nation opposed to human bondage. Every step that we advanced in attempting to impress this character upon the nation, we were met by the slaveholding portion of the Republic with the menace of disruption and dismemberment of the empire.

It was not possible at first to prevent the extension of slavery in our own borders. It was not possible to prevent the acquisition of foreign territory for the purpose of extension of slavery, because of the popular apprehension that disunion must follow the policy of restriction thus proposed. Thus, in fact, under previous Administrations the slaveholding power largely and long determined the policy of the country at home and abroad. Thus it happened that when Hayti established a republic upon the basis of universal emancipation and constituted in all its forms after our own, the United States did not recognize Hayti, and throughout all her existence, now not a short one, of near fifty years, that sister republic was never recognized by the United States. It was so with Dominica, in 1824, when she separated herself from Hayti and established an American sister republic.

The United States did not acknowledge Dominica as a State, and she has remained out of relations with us until the present time. Our own anti-slavery people of all classes, with great labor and great cost, many years ago established a Republic on the coast of Africa, Liberia, intended to operate successfully for the suppression of the African slave trade. The founders of Liberia were American citizens, the settlers were a portion of the American people; but the slaveholding policy prevailed in the councils of the United States, and the United States did not recognize Liberia, though virtually a creation of its own. All this continued until 1861, when the slaveholding policy of the United States was for the first time effectually reversed—not without the necessary cost of suppressing the attempted dismemberment so long [illegible]. How great that cost has been I am not now to speak; but it is a source of satisfaction that the reversal of the objectionable policy is complete and radical. We have now no slaves in the United States.

Simultaneously with declaring the doom of slavery at home, the President recommended to Congress a recognition of the republics of Hayti and Liberia, and Congress promptly and with great unanimity expressed its concurrence. This hand, which by direction of the President wrote those acts, was at that moment equally prepared and anxious to recognize the republic of Dominica. But, unfortunately, just at that moment the republic had gone down in Dominica, and this fair and beautiful land had become, with the apparent consent of its people, a portion of the kingdom of Spain. We could not deny to the people of Dominica the right to be subjects of Spain, if such was their choice.

We could not intervene by force to prevent their exercising that choice, or to compel them to reconsider it. We nowhere intervene to establish republican institutions by force. We deny the right of any State to intervene by force to establish monarchical, imperial or any other form of Government in our own country. Foreign intervention by force is always ruinous to a State, no matter under what pretence it is effected or by what agency it is invoked. No foreign State can establish or guarantee republican institutions in any other State by force. A State that will permit and endure successful intervention in its political affairs is unworthy, and therefore incapable of being free. A brave people need no intervention. To a timid or cowardly nation foreign intervention, though in friendly guise, is no blessing.

President Baez. That is true. But the people of Dominica have a right that when they have re-established their republican system they should be acknowledged; and if I could only give to our Congress, when it comes together, the assurance that we should be recognized by the United States, all our troubles would be passed.

Mr. Seward. President: Out of the capital, out of my own country, without the authority of Congress or President, I can say nothing official, and therefore nothing that can guarantee the action of the American Republic.

President Baez. Certainly. I understand that your visit is personal and unofficial.

Mr. Seward. Yes, it is so. Nevertheless I feel assured that I shall speak in harmony with the sentiments of the entire American Government and people, when I say all that the condition of Dominica, as you have explained it to me, seems to me to require. We have built up in the northern part of the American continent a Republic. We have laid for it a broad foundation. It has grown upon our hands to be an imposing, possibly a majestic empire. Its stability and permanence are essential to prosperity, liberty, peace, and happiness. Like every other structure of large proportions, it requires outward buttresses—those buttresses will arise in the development of civilization on this hemisphere. They will consist of republics founded like our own in adjacent countries and islands, upon the principle of the equal rights of men. To us it matters not of what race or lineage those republics shall be. They are necessary for our security against aggression, external forces and perhaps for the security of our internal peace. We desire those buttresses to be multiplied and strengthened as fast as it can be done, without the exercise of fraud or force upon our own part.

You are quick to perceive the use of the main edifice in protecting the buttress you have established; and thus it happens that the republics around us only impart to us the strength which we in turn extend to them. We have, therefore, no choice but to recognize the Republic of Dominica so soon as it shall afford to us the necessary guarantee of its own stability. We have only been waiting at Washington for the report of our Consul here, giving us satisfactory evidence of its stability and permanence.

President Baez. It is desirable for Dominica that the United States should enter into a permanent treaty with us. But it is a question whether we should now send a minister, by executive authority alone, or wait until the assembling of our national Congress to give the necessary authority. I should like to know if you are at liberty to give me your opinion on that question.
