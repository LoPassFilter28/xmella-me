Our Protectorate in the West Indies

No standard American book on Santo Domingo has been published since Mr. Hazard's work (Santo Domingo, Past and Present, with a Glance at Haiti, by Samuel Hazard) which appeared in 1873 and was reviewed by the present writer in the columns of The Sun. A new one was therefore much needed to bring our knowledge of the country up to date; and this we have in Santo Domingo: A Country with a Future, by Otto Schoenrich, secretary to the United States Commissioner who investigated the financial condition of Santo Domingo in 1905, and subsequently secretary to the Dominican Minister of Finance.

His frequent visits to the island and long sojourn there have made him familiar with the political, social and business conditions which prevail in this "republic of revolutions," as Santo Domingo has aptly been denominated; a title which is becoming less appropriate than it was, however, since the United States Government has consented to supervise the collection of the customs and payment of the foreign debt and has thus really established a sort of American protectorate over two-thirds of the island of Hayti.

America's Stake.

It is vital to the preservation of political liberty in the United States that the countries about the Caribbean Sea should be kept free from the domination of any hostile autocratic power. This truth has been realized more intensely than ever before since Germany has revealed her true attitude toward the rest of the world in the course of the present great war. When therefore the Dominicans found themselves bankrupt and hopelessly at a loss how to adjust their indebtedness it became a matter of the first importance to us that Santo Domingo should seek aid from this country rather than from a Government which might exploit the island not merely to the advantage of Germany but greatly to the detriment of the United States.

By its treatment of Spain after the war of 1898 the United States had shown that a great nation was capable of acting unselfishly and in accordance with altruistic ideals. When Elihu Root was Secretary of State the Dominicans appealed to him to help them settle their national debt. Fearing that any intervention might lead to a scheme for annexation, many members of the Senate opposed any and every scheme for rendering aid. An agreement was finally made, however, between the Government of Santo Domingo and its creditors, whereby the latter agreed to accept specified sums in satisfaction of their claims to be paid out of the customs in instalments, provided the United States would consent to appoint a receiver general to collect the duties and apply them to the liquidation of the indebtedness.

The Senate confirmed a treaty sanctioning this agreement, and since 1907 the customs income of the Dominican Republic has been collected by an American General Receiver, appointed by the President. "The customs receivership," says Mr. Schoenrich, "has continued to render invaluable service. In peace and war its officials have distinguished themselves by a highly efficient, tactful and fearless discharge of their duties. Up to 1913 appointments to the service were determined by the fitness and experience of the appointee rather than by his political antecedents, and the officials appointed possessed unusual qualifications." This reference to 1913, however, must be regarded as an intimation that there has been a falling off in the character of the appointees since that date.

Our Delicate Task.

The relations established by such a fiscal treaty as ours with Santo Domingo are so delicate as to require very careful handling. The purpose of the United States is, or should be, to serve the Dominicans without dominating their Government or wounding their susceptibilities. The revolutionary tendencies of the people render this an exceedingly difficult task.

In 1916 a civil war threatened such disaster to the country that a force of marines was landed from the American cruiser squadron in Dominican waters and order was restored after a brief engagement, in which the Americans suffered a loss of one killed and eight wounded. The Admiral subsequently issued a proclamation declaring Santo Domingo to be under the military administration of the United States, but stating "that the object of the occupation was not to destroy Dominican sovereignty but to restore order; that Dominican laws were to continue in effect, so far as they did not conflict with the objects of the occupation or the decrees of the military Government; that the Dominican courts were to continue in their functions, except that offences against the military Government were to be judged by military courts, and that all the revenues of the Dominican Government were to be paid over to the military Government, which would administer the same."

This was the status quo when the present volume went to press in January, 1918. The affairs of Santo Domingo were being administered by American naval officers, pending a reorganization of the civil Government of the republic.

Columbus's Hispaniola.

Just as all Gaul, in the time of Julius Cæsar, was divided into three parts, so all of the great West Indian island of Hayti is divided into two parts: The French republic of Hayti in the west, dominated by French negroes, and the Spanish republic of Santo Domingo in the east, populated by whites and mulattoes of Spanish descent.

"The area of the island is generally stated at 28,249 square miles, of which Hayti is credited with 10,204 square miles and the Dominican republic with 18,045 square miles." No part of the country, however, has ever been carefully surveyed except the railroad lines and the chief harbors, so these estimates are only approximately correct.

We may fairly consider Santo Domingo as being about as large as Vermont and New Hampshire taken together. The island was discovered on his first voyage by Columbus, who called it La Española (in English, Hispaniola), because the scenery reminded him of Andalusia in Spain; and the Spanish colony which was established there enjoyed great prosperity for about half a century, especially under the administration of Diego Columbus, the son of the great Admiral, who married Maria de Toledo, the beautiful niece of the Duke of Alba, and maintained a sort of vice-regal court in the castle which he built at Santo Domingo city, and which is described as an imposing structure even in its ruins.

France's Foothold.

"About 1508 the island began to be called Santo Domingo, but for almost three centuries royal decrees continued to refer to it as Española. So flourishing was its state at this time that thirteen of its towns were granted coats of arms and three were declared cities. The colony was and for many years continued to be a starting point for voyages of discovery and conquest in the islands and along the shores of the Caribbean Sea." By the peace of Ryswick in 1697 Spain ceded the western part of the island to France, whose colonists had long claimed it, and soon made it the richest country of West Indies.

"Great plantations of tobacco, indigo, cacao, coffee and sugar were established. The country came to be known as the paradise of the West Indies and the wealth of the planters became proverbial." The French Revolution, however, turned this paradise into a veritable hell. The half million of slaves rose against their 30,000 white masters and negro rule was permanently established. "The great plantation homes were burned, the wide estates desolated, white women were ravished and murdered and white men put to death with horrible tortures, while the liberated slaves indulged in orgies at which the beverage was rum mixed with human blood. It was a fearful day of reckoning."

Under Negro Rule.

From this holocaust of blood arose the negro nationality which now constitutes the Republic of Hayti. The Spanish portion of the island fell under the domination of this black republic for twenty-two years, in the early part of the nineteenth century; but the Dominicans declared their independence in 1844, and have maintained it ever since except for a short period (1861-1865), during which they were nominally under the rule of the crown of Spain.

Under the presidency of Gen. Buenaventura Baez in 1871 a strong effort was made to induce the United States to annex the Dominican Republic, but the Senate would not sanction the proposed acquisition, although it was strongly advocated by President Grant. In speculating on what the condition of the country would have been, if annexed to the United States, Mr. Schoenrich says: "The power of the United States would have maintained peace; salutary laws would have educated the people in self-government; liberal tariff concessions would have stimulated agriculture and industry; the influx of a good stock of immigrants would have developed and settled the interior; honest administration would have provided roads and schools; and soon the country would have attained a high degree of development and prosperity."

In the title of his book our author characterizes Santo Domingo as "a country with a future." It is evident that he believes its future will be closely associated with the United States. Irrespective of any question of annexation, he insists it is our moral duty to foster the development of the country and promote the happiness of the people by granting suitable tariff concessions.

Much Natural Wealth.

The lack of a free but stable Government capable of maintaining law and order and thus promoting the general welfare of the inhabitants is the chief reason for the non-development of the rich natural resources of Santo Domingo. With attractive scenery, good harbors, an abundant water supply, a fertile soil, magnificent forests and a healthful climate, the Dominican Republic ought to be one of the most prosperous regions of the New World, as it was one of the earliest to be discovered. What it has needed is "men, high minded men," such as the poet says are necessary to constitute a State. Santo Domingo city, the capital, "had begun to decline almost a hundred years before the Pilgrims landed on Plymouth Rock."

"There are only 144 miles of railroad in the republic, and the other lines of communication overland can be called roads only as a matter of courtesy, being in fact little better than mere trails. This explains why most of the important towns are on the sea coast, where they can be reached by water. One day when travelling on the high road near La Vega the author encountered an old man who was in actual danger of being drowned in the liquid mud; he took a snapshot of the victim while he was being extricated by a good samaritan who was passing by.

Santo Domingo is not only proud of having been discovered and acquired by Christopher Columbus; it also claims to be his burial place. "The same honor is claimed by Spain, but a Dominican would consider it almost treasonable to doubt the justice of the Dominican claim." That the body of Columbus was brought from Spain, where he died, and deposited in the cathedral in Santo Domingo city, where it remained until 1795, is unquestionable.

The doubt arises in reference to the claim of the Spaniards that they removed the remains to Cuba in 1795, whence they were taken back to Spain after the Spanish-American war of 1898. Excavations made in the Dominican Cathedral in 1877 satisfied the Dominicans that the body removed by the Spaniards was really that of Diego Columbus and that the remains of his father were still in the cathedral vault.

Mr. Schoenrich reviews the evidence in detail and concludes that the Dominican contention is correct. It is sustained by a majority of those who have investigated the question, especially those who have personally visited Santo Domingo. "It seems that in spite of the acts of men, fate has permitted the remains of the discoverer of America to repose in the principal cathedral of the island he loved."

23—Revolutions—23.

We have spoken of Santo Domingo as the republic of revolutions. During the seventy years of Dominican independence the country has witnessed twenty-three successful revolutions. Fortunately they have not been very sanguinary as a rule. "In Puerto Plata, it is said that when the Government troops attacked the city in 1904 a fierce battle ensued, which continued from morning till the town was taken by storm in the evening; yet only one man was killed and his death was due to his own carelessness, for he appeared not far from where soldiers of the other side were training a cannon and refused to obey their warning to get out of the way, whereupon the cannon was discharged and his arm shot off, causing a mortal wound." Nevertheless the graves of victims of the numerous revolutions are everywhere to be seen, testifying to the curse of internecine warfare.

Mr. Schoenrich's interesting book shows that Santo Domingo under wise and friendly aid and guidance from the United States may become the most prosperous State in the West Indies.

SANTO DOMINGO: A COUNTRY WITH A FUTURE. By Otto Schoenrich. The Macmillan Company. $3.
