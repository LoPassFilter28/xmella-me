Albuquerque Morning Journal · January 29, 191729 de enero de 1917

Original in English.Original en inglés.

ANNEXATION OF CUBA TO U. S. IS MUCH OPPOSED BY POLITICIANS Americans Living on Island Predict It and Spanish Population Hopes for It, Says Correspondent. DOMINICAN REFUGEES MAKE STRONG PROTEST Denounce Policy of President Wilson in Santo Domingo as Diabolical Plan Aimed at Independence.

Havana, Cuba, Jan. 17 (by Mail).—Following is a translation of a "resolution of protest" adopted by Dominicans in Havana at a meeting presided over by their charge d'affaires for Cuba, Manel M. Morillo.

"The undersigned Dominicans, residents of the Republic of Cuba, convoked by the charge d'affaires of the Dominican republic in Cuba, who also signs this, to deliberate on the extraordinary happenings which have occurred in our country, where the government of the United States of America, committing a great crime, has destroyed the constitutional government which then existed, presided over by that illustrious citizen, Dr. Francisco Henriquez Carvajal, and established in its stead a military American government, proclaiming besides martial law and ordering the censorship of all mails, telegrams and cables, hereby resolve:

"To protest against the crime committed by the United States of America in our country with the establishing there of a military despotism and the destruction of an existing constitutional government. We would protest also against the scandalous and barbarous conduct of the American troops in our country since May 14, when, against all laws, they invaded the republic by force of arms and put it under the domination of the sword, committing all kinds of assaults, open violence, outrages and crimes against the persons and interests of our compatriots, and carrying their cruelty even to the assassination of old men, women and children and the burning of innocent towns.

"We denounce before America and before the world these crimes against modern civilization, which constitute a threat against all weak peoples of America, and, lastly, we ask all American republics in the name of American solidarity, for their moral and diplomatic assistance for our country that she may be saved from the subjugation to which the United States of America is submitting her for imperialistic ends."

Among those who were present and signed the resolutions was a son of the deposed president and a son of the revolutionary general, Mella.

Annexation Question.

Will Cuba be annexed to the United States?

"Certainly," says the American. "It's only a question of time."

"I hope so, and the sooner the better," declares the Spaniard.

"Never!" cries the Cuban professional politician — and the American and the Spaniard smile.

The American does not venture to predict when annexation will be consummated — five years, ten years, twenty years—but he thinks it inevitable.

In support of his view the American points to what he thinks is the trend of sentiment in Cuba today. He mentions the hundreds of Cuban boys who leave each autumn for schools and colleges in the United States, to return imbued with American ideas and ideals.

Politics Closely Watched.

He indicates the increase in commerce with the United States and the advantages Cuba would derive from a removal of the tariff barrier. By treaty, goods are exchanged between the two countries at 20 per cent below the regular tariff duties.

There is also the ever-increasing amount of American capital invested in Cuban enterprises. Both the American and the Spaniard feel that with an assuredly stable and well-administered government Cuba will become one of the richest places in the world.

While intensely loyal to their republic, some of the better educated Cubans are watching their national politics with anxious eyes, fearful that the result may make American intervention and annexation advisable.

Taking Army Out of Politics.

The present government by decree furnishes an ever-handy excuse for somebody to become alarmed for the welfare of the sacred constitution. Because the president is a conservative the congress, which is controlled by the liberals, refuses stubbornly to exercise its functions. Even appropriation legislation is ignored.

As a result the president governs by decree in order that a government may continue to exist.

Under President Menocal an attempt has been made to take the army out of politics. Officers and men have been thoroughly trained under the supervision of Lieut. Col. Edmund Wittenmyer, military attache to the American legation, and the army has been developed to a point of high efficiency.

9,000 Men Organized as Police.

Colonel Wittenmyer, incidentally, is credited with knowing more than any other man in the world about Cuba and Cubans. This knowledge is believed to explain why the United States keeps one of its best colonels as military attache to a comparatively obscure legation.

The Cuban government now has under consideration a plan for universal service for the training of all citizens. This step would not be taken for national defense against a foreign foe. Unlike any other army in the world, the Cuban troops are trained not to repel invasion—that is left to the United States—but to police the country. The army is composed of 9,000 men.

Wilson Puzzles Liberals.

It must be admitted President Wilson has the liberal leaders sadly puzzled. They look to the west and see Mexico conducting revolutions to its heart's content, on the theory that its people are fighting for liberty. They look to the east and see Haiti and the Dominican republic occupied by United States marines.

In which class would Mr. Wilson place a Cuban revolution? If the liberals could answer that question to their own satisfaction they would be able to decide definitely on a plan of action. Above all other Cubans, they dread an American intervention and the subsequent famine of public offices.

During the last few months political leaders have devoted much attention to the policy of the United States in Haiti, the Dominican republic and Nicaragua.

The Diabolical Wilson Plan.

Here is a quotation from an article which appeared in La Reforma Social, a monthly magazine published by Sr. Ferrara:

"A third meddling intervention of the American government assumed the form of a plan, afterward proverbially called the Wilson plan. They (the Dominicans) appear never to have perceived that they were marching blindly toward the abyss. Only thus is explained their acceptance of the diabolic Wilson plan in all its traitorous and suicidal completeness."

The "diabolic Wilson plan" referred to is the one proposed and accepted in August, 1914, whereby the United States agreed to protect constitutional government in the Dominican republic. This same article states that "with the crimes of its forces in Santo Domingo, which remind us of the crimes of the Germans in Belgium and France, the United States has outraged America and the world."

Feeling Runs High.

Feeling over the action of the United States in the Dominican republic runs especially high in the eastern end of Cuba, which is more closely connected with the neighboring island. La Independencia, an independent daily newspaper in Santiago, has devoted columns to comment on the American intervention.

El Mundo of Havana, a conservative newspaper, which, incidentally, is controlled by Americans, points to the Dominican republic and Mexico both as object lessons for Cuba.
