SAN DOMINGO. Doubtful Condition of the Debt and the Public Lands—The Trickery of Baez—How he Forces Public Opinion.

A correspondent of the Boston Advertiser, who accompanied the Commissioners to San Domingo, writes an interesting letter from Port-au-Prince, under date of March 7, and his account of the condition of affairs in San Domingo is by no means flattering. He says:

"The Tennessee, with two of the Commissioners, Mr. Wade and Dr. Howe, arrived here from Azua on the 2d instant. Here they will await the arrival of Mr. Wylie, who rode across by land from San Domingo to Puerta Plata. The work of the commission was really finished before it came to Hayti, as it had been decided, since ascertaining the temper of the people and government here, not to ask any direct questions in regard to their claims against or relative to San Domingo. The investigation has probably been as thorough as was possible in so short a time, though much of this brief period has been consumed by over a dozen changes in plans, each necessarily involving delay. The harmony which marked the first week of the work at the capital was unfortunately almost entirely destroyed toward the close of the stay there; and this, of course, detracted much from the efficiency of the inquiry and the value of the results.

"The last days at the capital were chiefly devoted to ascertaining the full extent of the debt, and of the concessions made to private individuals. The terms upon which Baez was now willing to negotiate a treaty were also agreed upon. The authorities repeatedly insisted that the one million and a half dollars named in the last treaty would discharge the entire public debt, including all claims of whatever kind. The arrival of a merchant from Curacoa with a claim of over half a million dollars, aroused Mr. Wade, and he insisted that every claim, whether recognized as valid or not, should be furnished. After several days of persistent effort, a list of claims pending before the Senate to the amount of $1,400,000 was prepared and handed to him. This made the debt nearly equal to the sum which several of the officials and a number of prominent merchants had asserted that it was—namely, $3,000,000, as a minimum. According to a Senator present at the capital, this low limit had been reached by a repudiation of near half a million of what had been considered valid debt.

"By the terms of the treaty which Baez is willing to make, the public lands, not already disposed of, are to be held to settle all claims outside of those it is intended to cover with the $1,500,000 which the United States is to pay. The effect of this will be to absorb all that is now left of the public lands. As near as could be ascertained, the condition of the public domain is as follows: Its extent is from 16,000 to 19,000 square miles. One fifth has been granted to the New York Surveying Company, and one fifth to all other parties. These two portions will cover by far the most valuable tracts in the republic. The remaining parts will be liable for these irregular claims which have been discovered.

"Baez thought it would be right and fair for the United States to pay the expense of the Dominican Government during the long delay since annexation was first proposed. This was not put in any formal shape, but was left for the consideration of the Commissioners.

"There is no avoiding the belief on the part of those who looked on from the outside that Baez from first to last attempted to arrange everything for the commission. So far as he could, he put the play of annexation on the stage for them and furnished the scenery. A number, if not all the societies which came to assure the commission that they were in favor of annexation, came at his request; and in several instances men of position, who thus went and declared, for the project, when they dared to talk, gave strong reasons why they were opposed to it, and why in their opinion, it would surely lead to trouble. It is a matter of extreme difficulty to ascertain public opinion under the severe military rule which exists. In a government where the Minister of War and the Vice-President, both men of standing, are obliged to take refuge in flight because of their disagreement with Baez, mainly on the subject of annexation, it is no easy task to get at the whole truth.

"Another example of trickery was the cool preparation of what was reported to the Commissioners as a revolt in the Higuey District (south of Samana) in favor of the United States. This was accomplished by sending a General of the army up to that section a week before, and in due time the cut and dried revolt came off with great enthusiasm. Dr. Howe was even induced to delay the time fixed for sailing several days in order to ride into that section to witness the wonderful enthusiasm for the United States.

"Baez's use of the American flag is not such as to inspire great pleasure among American observers. He carried it in his boat when shipping troops to Azua, and with them on the way, after reaching that place he sent it at the head of his forces against Cabral. It was simply taking advantage of the existing circumstances to impress his own soldiers and those of the enemy with the idea that he was actually entitled to the full benefits of that flag. It was the course of a 'jockey' from first to last, and no one could observe him without thanking Mr. Sumner for selecting a word that described the man and all his operations so well.

"It has been impossible to get at any correct estimate of Cabral's strength, except as it has been unwittingly and indirectly furnished by Baez himself. From the first he has asserted that Cabral amounted to nothing, and yet all his actions have been those of a man who had constant and grave fears of what Cabral might accomplish. The commission were promised every facility for visiting him, but each party sent out to find him were stopped at Azua by the Governor, a brother of Baez. When the Tennessee reached Azua several efforts were made to start a party through the lines, but the attempts were fruitless. No one was allowed to go west of that place, on the plea that Baez could not protect them. This was a virtual admission that Cabral controlled that part of the republic west of the meridian of Azua. In extent it is almost one-quarter of it. It seems pretty certain, however, that Cabral has comparatively little Dominican strength, though if late accounts are true he is gaining in that respect. Three of Baez's Generals, whose names are given, are announced here as deserters to Cabral.

"Mr. Wade and Dr. Howe were received here on Saturday by President Saget and his Cabinet. The interview had no reference to the question of annexation, but was only to exchange expressions of friendship. The authorities, however, as well as the leading citizens, are very hostile to the idea of the United States acquiring a foothold in the island; while the masses are represented as intensely bitter in their opposition. To such an extent does this exist that anything more than common courtesy extended to the commission from the President would be very likely to stir up a revolution."
