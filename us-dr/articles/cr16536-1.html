THE LIBERTY GUARD.

[From the Chicago Tribune.]

Mr. Sumner struck "San Domingo" a mortal blow when he arrayed the old Liberty Guard against it. It may have been a foul blow, but it was none the less a fatal one. When we find Lloyd Garrison, Gerrit Smith, and their school of political agitators opposing the annexation project on the ground that it is an act of injustice to the colored race, we may be sure that there are breakers ahead. We have done something in our time to create and sustain the anti-slavery sentiment in this country, and to break down the senseless and cruel distinction that once prevailed among us on account of color, and we know how strong that old abolition sentiment is. We did not suppose that it would be yoked into the harness to pull against the President's San Domingo project. But it seems very likely that that will be the upshot of it. And if it does turn out that the Liberty Guard look upon it as a measure of oppression to the Republic of Hayti, as Mr. Sumner contends, and a measure of injustice to the colored race collectively, as Mr. Gerrit Smith contends, then,

"Not all the King's horses nor all the King's men.

Can pull Humpty Dumpty up again."

It is evident that nothing could have been further from the President's thoughts than the idea of committing any wrong upon the colored race anywhere by the annexation of San Domingo. It is not very clear what idea he had in his mind, unless, as the Nation suggests, he fancied that to import $100,000 worth of products from the Republic of Dominica would be a loss to us, while to import the same products from the State of Dominica would be a loss to us—that the former would tend to increase the national debt, while the latter would tend to diminish it. But it is certain that he did not have any such idea as the injury or oppression of any race of men, or depriving them of any chances they may have acquired, or may fairly acquire, for vindicating their capacity for self government. Mr. Sumner alleged that the proceedings taken by the President in reference to San Domingo are a menace to the Republic of Hayti, which embraces four-fifths of the population of the island; that threats of violence have been openly made against Hayti by our naval forces in those waters; that the President, in his annual message, referred no less than nine times to the Dominican Republic as the whole island, thus entirely ignoring the existence of Hayti. Senator Morton says that this latter was an inadvertence, and we think very likely it was; but it strongly suggests that the annexation of San Domingo would be only a step to the annexation of Hayti, especially since the commercial advantages which the President sees in the extension of the island could not be attained with the thrifty portion of the island unannexed. That the Haytiens are strongly opposed to the annexation of Dominica, and still more strongly opposed to the annexation of themselves, has been made manifest in many ways, and is not disputed.

Mr. Gerrit Smith argues that the West India Islands have been designated by nature as the home of the black man; that he has a divine right to govern in the tropics; that the annexation of San Domingo is the first step towards dispossessing him of his home and his zone, and that "it would be a robbery in itself, and would lead to one far greater." We cannot, perhaps, take in the whole force of this argument; but we can perceive that if that portion of the Republican party which has usually followed Mr. Sumner as an infallible guide in anti-slavery morals, come to take the same views, or substantially the same, as those which he and Gerrit Smith hold, San Domingo will not be annexed during the present generation. And, so far from Mr. Sumner being read out of the party on account of his opposition to San Domingo, it is more probable that he will read out everybody who is in favor of it.
