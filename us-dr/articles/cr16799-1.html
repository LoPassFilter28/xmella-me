Bryan Had Sullivan Under Rigid Inquiry Secretary's Investigation So Far Unfavorable to Minister. Fills Dominican Post Appoints Man Unknown to Envoy Instead of Latter's Own Selection.

WASHINGTON, Dec. 10.—That the official conduct of James M. Sullivan, Minister to Santo Domingo, has already been the subject of investigation by Secretary Bryan was established beyond doubt here to-day.

The result was not at all favorable to Minister Sullivan. As a result the man selected by the State Department to be recommended to the Dominican Government for appointment as Director of Public Works under that Government is one who never has seen Minister Sullivan and is not acquainted with those with whom the Minister has been closely associated in political matters in New York and vicinity.

Mr. Bryan will recommend for the place A. J. Collett, son-in-law of J. C. Dahlman, former Mayor of Omaha, Neb. Mr. Collett is an engineer. He has been with the Union Pacific Railroad for thirty-three years and has been indorsed for the Dominican post. The salary is $6,000 a year.

This appointment was brought about when a report reached Mr. Bryan of the activities of Mr. Sullivan on behalf of a friend whom he was supporting for the Dominican post. It was represented to Mr. Bryan that the activities of Minister Sullivan since his arrival at Santo Domingo city last summer had been largely responsible for the resignation of J. L. Mann, who was then Director of Public Works.

Mr. Mann was regarded as highly efficient. He had been appointed in 1911, after serving for some time as assistant to his predecessor. He has had a varied experience as an engineer in general practice, and also in the irrigation service of the United States.

Blame Sullivan for Resignation.

According to the information brought to Mr. Bryan, Mr. Mann's resignation was the result of activities by Sullivan, which made it impossible for him to retain his post. The programme then was for Minister Sullivan's friend to get the post, which carries with it great influence over the awarding of contracts for Government work in the Dominican republic.

It was learned to-day from an authoritative source that Mr. Bryan did not select Mr. Sullivan for the post, as had been supposed, but that the decision to appoint him was reached at the White House without any recommendation from the State Department.

It was learned also that Senator Hughes of New Jersey was not his political sponsor for the Dominican post.

Representative Hamill of New Jersey was Sullivan's strongest backer for political preferment. In fact it was said here to-day that the White House named Sullivan chiefly for the purpose of pleasing Mr. Hamill. Senator Martine also spoke a word for Sullivan.

One interesting fact about the selection was that Sullivan was named without any active indorsement of his candidacy by Senator O'Gorman from Sullivan's own State, New York.

It was learned to-day that the reported award of a railroad concession in Santo Domingo to T. J. Sullivan was far from an accomplished fact. The project in question is one which has been under discussion in the Dominican republic for years, but still is a long way from realization. Before any concession can be granted under the proposed plan the approval of the State Department will be necessary, as the matter involves an increase of the indebtedness of the Dominican Government, over which the United States Government as receiver exercises full control.

In official quarters it was stated to-day that the unfortunate tendencies of Minister Sullivan had been noted since the assumption of his official duties. He had impressed many of those brought into touch with his official acts as one who seemed determined to prove that every American who had preceded him to Santo Domingo had been guilty of irregularities and gross improprieties.

Minister's Actions Criticised.

It was this tendency upon the part of the Minister, it was said here to-day, which was largely responsible for Director Mann's resignation. It has been expected by other Americans that the Minister's attitude would have unfortunate results, not only to those directly concerned, but to the prestige of the participation of the United States in the internal affairs of the Dominican republic.

The Dominican protest against the despatch of a large party of American officials to Santo Domingo to "observe" next Monday's election still stands without modification despite Secretary Bryan's message of explanation to President Bordas. There is no doubt, however, that the Dominican Government will accept the inevitable and make no effort to prevent the American officials from carrying out their instructions to watch carefully the proceedings.

Officials here who have had much experience with Dominican affairs and Caribbean politics in general are pessimistic over the outlook in Santo Domingo. In many quarters a revolution is predicted as the result of next Monday's election, regardless of which side is declared the victor. It would be unprecedented, it is said here, for the results of the election to be accepted by both sides without protest from the loser.

This view is based upon the conviction that there can be no such thing as an absolutely fair and free election in Santo Domingo and general acceptance of the result by both sides without the interposition of a third strong force.
