IMPORTANT FROM ST. DOMINGO.

Our St. Domingo City Correspondence.

St. Domingo City, May 17, 1862.

Grand Industrial Progress Under the New Rule of Spain—Enterprise of Every Description Fostered—Openings for American Capital—The Tariff—Fine Chance for the Negro Contrabands of the United States—Slavery in the Spanish Colonies Nearly at an End—Advance in Dominican Property—Trade Report, &c., &c.

Although our news is tame and trivial compared with your grand march of events, yet I will send you the few items afloat.

Spain is making her mark here. By her liberal and enlightened policy she has silenced the few remaining malcontents. The Dominicans have certainly no reason to complain of her occupation. Indeed, the benefits bestowed have been thus far like the jug handle—all on one side—for poor St. Domingo has had nothing but acquiescence and gratitude to render on its part. The officials of her Majesty's government are free to express their avowed desire to foster enterprise, and especially to welcome and protect foreign capital and labor. There will be plenty of fat contracts to be had, and as Spain pays up in solid rocks we may expect to see the "Yanks" along here in the natural course of things.

The railroad from Santiago to Angelina, on the Yuna, is decided upon. This will give an outlet for the productions of the splendid valley of the Yuna, known as the Vega Real, to the Bay of Samana. Already propositions for navigating the Yuna from its mouth, at Samana Bay, to Angelina, have been presented by an American company and favorably considered.

The new tariff, reducing the rate of duties from thirty to twenty per cent ad valorem, it is said, will take effect in July. All articles necessary for the personal use of the colonists, except provisions, are to be admitted free of duty. I enclose a copy of the new decree regulating the terms for the payment of duties.

Our accounts from Hayti as to the practical working of the colonization of American blacks there are favorable, and Spain is not unwilling to take a leaf out of Hayti's book. Indeed the authorities here have already taken the initiative in the matter, and I understand expect shortly large arrivals of "contrabands." There is no fear that they will be other than well treated. There is no country on the face of the earth where the irrepressible darkey blooms as he does here. They hold high offices, both in the civil and military departments, and possess the same political and religious, if not social, status as the whites. This is a peculiar feature of Spanish policy. It is well known that the home government favors emancipation in the colonies, and every year sees a new condition imposed on slavery. The institution in the Spanish West Indies is now so hampered with restrictions, bearing heavily upon the master and operating to the proportionate advantage of the slave, that the planters of Cuba and Porto Rico are seriously considering the propriety of proposing to the home government the policy of emancipation, with compensation.

Spain evidently intends to build up in these waters a powerful free black empire. She distrusts the loyalty of her white Creole subjects. They are a little too much exposed to the captivating influences of Secessia; and, seeing what the progress of the age demands, and will not be cheated of, she wisely determines to have her best bower out to windward. So her policy is towards emancipation, and not only this, but she treats free blacks with marked respect and partiality. In Havana you will see a regiment of a thousand free black volunteers parading with the troops of the line or keeping guard in the Obra Pia. This service, it is to be borne in mind, is not allowed to the whites of Creole birth, except as special cases of favor. Thus the blacks are educated to the use of arms, while the whites grow up in ignorance of this accomplishment—a wrinkle which the non-slaveholding States of the United States may make a note of. In point of civil privileges the free blacks are in every respect the equal of whites. In courts of law their testimony is considered quite as good, and they have the same rights as to the holding of lands and other property. The government reserves to itself the right to dissolve the connection of master and slave on proof of cruel treatment, &c. One quarter of the blacks in Cuba are free, and many of them are rich. It may be mortifying to acknowledge that Spain is leading us in wisdom and progress; but such is the melancholy fact.

Since the annexation, real estate in and about the city has increased in value several hundred per cent. Houses rent at Fifth avenue rates. The large property of Gen. Cazneau, which adjoins the city walls, is now a splendid fortune to its lucky owner. Land in the interior has not perceptibly increased in value, as but few agriculturists have yet arrived.

At Samana great improvements are being made. The Royal West India Mail Company's steamships will probably make this port their terminus, in lieu of St. Thomas. If the coal is as good and abundant as represented, it will doubtless be the favorite resort for steamships plying in these waters. All such vessels may enter and depart free of duties and port charges. It is probable that Samana will be declared a free port for all nations in July. Then farewell to the glory and prosperity of St. Thomas. It would be well for Uncle Sam to secure a good location for a coal and naval depot at this port before the speculators get their clutches on every point.

Business is lively. House carpenters, masons and painters command good wages. Lumber continues in demand. No American vessels now in port. Health of the city good.
