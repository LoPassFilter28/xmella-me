Stamps That Plunged Rival Countries Into War

POSTAGE stamps, if anyone takes the trouble to make a study of them, are much more than little gummy-backed pieces of paper intended to pay for transporting letters and packages from one place to another. To serious-minded philatelists they are works of art to be collected like old prints or antique furniture—and to the jittery citizens of adjacent and unfriendly countries they can be, and have been, pretty little reasons for going to war.

It was back in 1927 that Paraguay issued the stamp reproduced at the extreme upper right of this page. To the casual observer it seemed a proper stamp bearing a map of the country. Hundreds of similar stamps had been printed before and no fuss ensued. But this stamp was a trouble-maker, and it made the Bolivians, next door to the Paraguayans, so mad that they grabbed their muskets and gathered in the plazas. In the capital at La Paz, high government officials glowered at the stamp put out by their neighbors and drafted curt notes that threatened reprisals.

The reason for all this sound and fury was this: In 1895 a piece of fever-infested jungle known as the Gran Chaco was divided between Paraguay, Bolivia and Argentina. The southwestern parts of the territory were, by treaty, given to Argentina, and the rest was divided about equally between Bolivia and Paraguay. That little stamp, issued thirty-two years after the signing of the treaty, showed all of Bolivia's chunk of the Gran Chaco as Paraguayan property. No wonder the Bolivians blew up and laid hands on their rifles.

The heads of the Bolivian government counted ten, as the saying goes, and did not immediately march against their neighbors. First they conferred with the post office department and the official engravers and countered with a stamp of their own—the larger of the two stamps shown at the left of the page. The result was a stamp that depicted all of the Gran Chaco that did not belong to Argentina as Bolivian ground.

That was the last straw. Frontier guards shot at each other and these clashes brought on an expensive and bitter war that finally was settled by the League of Nations. The explosion set off by that little exchange of stamps cost hundreds of lives and drained both Bolivia and Paraguay of millions of dollars' worth of resources.

The stamp at the extreme upper left of the page, a peso issue by Argentina, was much more fairly and diplomatically drawn. The artist who made the map was careful not to extend the northern boundary of his country a fraction of an inch into the disputed Gran Chaco section.

Back in 1900 a stamp put out by the government of the Dominican Republic—San Domingo, as it is more commonly known—so enraged the Haitians, who occupy the western half of the same West Indies island, that they reached for their shooting irons and marched from a hundred jungle villages into Port au Prince.

The Haitians cried out for San Dominguan blood because of the stamp shown at the lower right. Actually, the land of the island is about equally divided between the two countries. As this stamp placed the frontier, the Haitians were squeezed into the western tip and all but dispossessed.

Cool heads prevailed upon the black warriors to wait a day or two until San Domingo had been given a chance to explain what the stamp meant. A courier was rushed to the other end of the island and his orders were plain. If San Domingo was telling the Haitians that it intended to claim all that land, then the answer was war and several thousand soldiers were all set for action.

Fortunately for all concerned, San Domingo apologized and blamed the misunderstanding on an artist who just wasn't up on his geography.
