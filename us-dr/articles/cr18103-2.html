Grant's San Domingo Message—He Explains His Conduct, but Doubts His own Judgment

The President to-day sent to Congress a message transmitting the report of the San Domingo Commission. The President in his message claims the report justifies all he has said on the subject, but regards it due to himself to give the story of his connection with the annexation scheme. When inaugurated he did not anticipate insular acquisitions, but believed we should not permit any independent government within the limit of North America to pass from the condition of independence to one of anarchy or protection under European power. The agents of Baez had represented to him the great capacity of the island and the helpless condition of the people and spoke of the alternative of applying to other powers. In the light of the Monroe doctrine he felt bound to ascertain facts. He thought the proposed friends of the rights of man would be his first assailants if he neglected so clear a duty. The report of the first commissioner showed it be his duty, in regard to the national interest, to negotiate a treaty. The debates of the Senate on the treaty being secret, the people could not know how much its rejection was influenced by misrepresentation. Therefore, he deemed it due to the character of his office and his agents to have such proceedings as would enable the people to know the truth. Hence the second commission. His personal solicitude in the matter is now ended, and the whole matter handed over to Congress and the people, "against whose will I have no policy to enforce." "I have ever believed that all men are wiser than any one man." The President suggests no action this session beyond the dissemination of the report, and concludes with the remark that he "can bear with patience the censure of disappointed men."

The Commissioner's Report—The

Land of Promise

The Commissioners say they made a thorough inspection of the territory, an investigation of official records and examination of the popular feeling. They give an extended statement of the form and institutions of the Dominican Government. Not a Justice of the Peace was found whose character did not inspire respect. They found all the departments of the Government in full operation, and Baez in full and peaceable possession of all parts of the Republic, except on the Haytien border, which is disturbed by insurrectionary leaders, aided by the Haytiens and political intriguers. These are incited to extraordinary activity now by the fear of annexation. Baez, chief in the esteem of the people, has been signally successful in his administration and has the best title of any to the chief magistracy of the Republic. For the last two years the resources of the Government have not been adequate to meet the expenses. Luperon and Cabral head the insurrections. Cabral is the most important, but neither has a flag nor an organized army. Cabral only claims to be chief of a revolution. Luperon is simply a bandit stained with crimes. His chief exploits are robberies. Cabral has only a few hundred men, who in an emergency press in all the men they can lay hands on. The Commissioners obtained evidence of Haytien assistance to Cabral from many sources. Had the revolutionists wielded only their own force, they would long ago have been suppressed. Their importance is derived from the fact that behind them stands the Haytien nation, which has never relented in its aggressive policy, and at whose head is a President elevated by a bloody insurrection involving the murder of his predecessor, but the local governments have been greatly obstructed by internal disturbances. The Spanish occupation of 1861 was consummated by fraud. All classes of people found have thoroughly discussed annexation and agreed it is their only hope. Pauperism, beggars and high crimes especially are unknown among the people, though the Spaniards will gamble. There is no religious intolerance, nor dislike of labor when sure to be paid. The people are proverbially shrewd and capable of self-government. The resources of the country are great and the production limited only by labor.

In the first part of the report of the Commissioners it is said the Spanish soldiers died by wholesale. At this point they say the losses of Spaniards are greatly exaggerated.

Samana Bay is a powerful naval and military station. Supposing a great commercial city there, the inland communication by water and by rail would be easy and the future of the place under the authority of the United States of national importance.

The total debt and claims were $156,583,159, including much that the Dominican government refuses to acknowledge as just. The resources were $79,268,475.

The Commissioners discredit the claim of Baez for property destroyed. They say Dominica is not liable for any portion of the French claims on Hayti. Dominica had representatives and made treaties only with the United States.

The Scientific Report—Mahogany

Houses—Little Whales!—Do Tell!

Geologists with the expedition report the existence of ores of iron, copper and gold, with deposits of lignite, rock, salt and petroleum. The iron is abundant and easy of access. There are five classes of lands. The most of the land is rich, well timbered and admirable for pasture or profitable cultivation. The regions where no rain falls can be made fertile by artificial irrigation. As a whole it is one of the most fertile regions on the face of the earth. The sugar cane is a very profitable crop; coffee, fibrous plants, wax and honey in the mountain regions. Ginger and indigo grow wild in the greatest profusion, and cotton and cinchana are among the cultivated products. Three crops of maize are raised annually. The country combines the products of the tropical and temperate zones. Immense herds of cattle feed on the great savannas. Cattle are sold for a cent per pound on the hoof. Great numbers of swine thrive in the forests. There is the greatest diversity of natural growths, which will give rise to extensive internal commerce. Insects and "noxious" reptiles are scarce. There is an astonishing variety of forest productions, including the choicest cabinet woods, and an immense stock of medical and commercial woods. Many houses are built of mahogany. The fisheries are neglected, but fish are abundant, especially grampus and little whales. Several little whales are caught every year. The country is generally healthy. The yellow fever is chiefly confined to Hayti.

Acclimatization was not difficult. There was but one case of mortal sickness
