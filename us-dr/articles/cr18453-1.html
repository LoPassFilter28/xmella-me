OAS Is Slated to End Dominican Sanctions

The Council of the Organization of the American States is to meet here tomorrow to lift the hemispheric boycott in force against the Dominican Republic since August, 1960.

Qualified sources at OAS headquarters say it is virtually certain the necessary two-thirds majority of 21 members will vote to lift the economic and diplomatic sanctions completely.

They originally were imposed against the dictatorship of late Generalissimo Rafael Trujillo Molina for plotting the assassination of Venezuelan President Romulo Betancourt.

New Regime in Office

Consensus in Latin and United States diplomatic circles here is that the sanctions are no longer justified since a representative Dominican Council of State took office on January 1 with the mandate to wipe out the last vestiges of the Trujillo era.

The special nine-member Sanctions Committee of the OAS met last Friday and is understood to have approved a report which recommends ending the Dominican boycott. The council will vote on this report tomorrow.

Dominican President Joaquin Balaguer, who is the one remaining hangover of the old regime, has said he will resign when OAS action is completed.

Reports from the Dominican capital of Santo Domingo say he is expected to resign from the seven-man State Council later this week. The interim President will then be Rafael Bonnelly, member of the National Civic Union—the leading democratic opposition group.

The hemispheric sanctions include a complete diplomatic break with the Dominican Republic by all OAS members and an embargo on shipments of arms, petroleum, trucks and spare parts.

But officials here note that the greatest boon the Caribbean nation will receive from the ending of sanctions will be a share in the United States sugar quota from Cuba and distributed among other foreign sugar producers.

President Kennedy said before Christmas that the Dominican Republic would qualify immediately for its share of this Cuban windfall once the United States resumes diplomatic relations. State Department officials expect Dominican inclusion in the February quotas.

Department of Agriculture officials estimate the added Dominican sugar sales could amount to $35 million in the first six months of 1962.

At United States premium prices, that figure amounts to a profit of $15.5 million over what the Dominicans would receive for the same amount of sugar at depressed world prices.

"What the new government needs is money and confidence," said one State Department official, "and it needs them fast." The United States would try to give both the money and the confidence, he added.

Panel to Study Needs

A top-level panel of three or four experts from the new AID agency and the State Department is scheduled to leave for the Dominican Republic later this week.

This panel is labeled a "pre-planning mission" out to investigate the needs of the country under the inter-American Alliance for Progress. The hope is, officials say, that these needs can be studied and plans drawn at a "more leisurely" pace after new dollar receipts for the Dominican sugar sales start pouring into the economy.

United States officials estimate the Dominican Republic lost $31 million during 1961 because it was excluded from the Cuban "bonus" by the break in diplomatic relations.
