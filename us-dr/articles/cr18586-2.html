Tom Paine Really Started Something When He Set Up the 'French Lobby'

By Henry B. Lee

When France hired Thomas Paine in 1778 for $1,000 a year to be propaganda agent in the United States, a chain reaction was established that is still being built up. Now, after 175 years, the foreign-agent industry is a multimillion-dollar proposition.

When Tom Paine set up shop the United States had just instituted a new type of government in which public opinion could sway public policy. Though the Government is much bigger and more powerful now, public opinion is still a big thing.

Worth Millions

Foreign agents have spent $33,330,558 here in the past five years seeking to influence United States foreign policy in behalf of the foreign governments they represent. This figure does not include about $30 million spent in this period by official foreign propaganda agencies. Nor does it include propaganda disseminated by foreign embassies, where most foreign propaganda originates.

The latter are exempted by the 1938 Foreign Agents Registration Act and do not have to register with the Department of Justice and record their activities. But agents hired by foreign governments to operate outside diplomatic immunity must register and report their activities in detail.

Two Types

Current registration discloses two major types of propaganda. One is the Communist-line variety, designed to subvert public opinion to the expansion of international communism. It is usually disseminated through Communist embassies and under the cloak of diplomatic immunity. The other is a more respectable activity and is generally conducted openly by friendly countries who hire American public relations firms and lobbyists to influence United States foreign policy in their behalf.

Much of this latter type of foreign propaganda involves underdeveloped countries whose economies are dependent on producing raw materials and selling them in United States markets. These might seek favorable legislation that would give them a bigger cut in United States import quotas. Or they might seek other economic concessions.

This activity is becoming increasingly important in world affairs. The economic survival of a nation or the life of a particular government can turn on the success or failure of this endeavor.

Venezuela's economy, for example, depends largely on exporting crude oil to the United States. When Congress thought about cutting imports of this oil last spring, it was rumored that the Venezuelan government would not survive such action.

Taft's Brother Involved

The Venezuelan government went to work on American public opinion. Its $40,000-a-year foreign agent—the Washington law firm of Cleary, Gottlieb, Friendly & Ball—mapped an extensive propaganda drive against the proposed legislation. This firm retained a network of additional foreign agents throughout the Nation to lobby against the legislation.

This network of powerful political figures and civic leaders included Charles P. Taft, brother of the late Senate majority leader and a prominent Ohio Republican himself. A syndicated columnist, too, was retained as a foreign agent. He was Dudley Harmon of Boston, who syndicates a column to nine New England newspapers.

Venezuela's investment in this propaganda drive has paid off thus far. Legislation to restrict that country's oil imports was not passed by Congress, despite support of the oil and coal lobbies here.

Another interesting propaganda drive involved Bolivian tin. When Bolivia nationalized its tin mines last year, it incited much hostility and what amounted to an economic boycott in the United States. This Latin American country was criticized for not compensating the expropriated tin companies, part of whose stock was owned by United States citizens.

Battle of Lobbies

Then Bolivia retained the public relations firm of Selvage, Lee & Chase for $48,000, plus expenses, to make the expropriation more palatable to public opinion and promote a long-term purchase contract for Bolivian tin. Press comment and public opinion on Bolivia began to improve.

The expropriated tin companies, meanwhile, retained the powerful Washington law firm of Davies, Richberg, Tydings & Beebe to lobby for adequate compensation. Former Senator Millard A. Tydings of Maryland is a member of this firm, which is to get $20,000, plus 1 per cent of any compensation the tin companies obtain.

The tin companies also retained the New York public relations firm of Win Nathanson & Associates for $75,000 yearly, plus expenses. "Canned" editorials began appearing in weekly and small city newspapers throughout the Nation, condemning the use of what was termed "hot" Bolivian tin.

This propaganda battle is apparently headed for a happy ending. Bolivia recently acquired a one-year tin purchase contract from the United States. And it has agreed to compensate the expropriated tin companies, according to reports.

Sugar Propaganda

Sugar is another international commodity involved in competing propaganda campaigns. The one-crop economies of the Dominican Republic and Cuba are dependent on United States sugar markets and there is intense rivalry between them over assignment of import quotas.

Rafael Trujillo Molina, strong man of the Dominican Republic, has dispensed much money and propaganda through several foreign agents here in the past several years. Meanwhile, he gained an increase for his sugar in the United States import quotas—an important concession for Trujillo because he and his family own much of the country's sugar industry.

Trujillo's gain was made at the expense of Cuba, whose quota was reduced. Apparently bent on shoring up its large quota, the Cuban sugar industry retained former Senators James P. Kem of Missouri and Joseph C. O'Mahoney of Wyoming as its foreign agents.

Canal Fight

But big-time lobbying is not new. Foreign propaganda agents played an interesting part in the history of the Panama Canal.

When the Frenchman, De Lesseps, undertook the excavation of the canal in 1879, the United States expressed fears about an infringement of the Monroe Doctrine. De Lesseps then spent $2 million trying to improve American public opinion.

Then, in 1902, the United States was about to construct a canal route through Nicaragua, but the successor of De Lesseps scared the United States Senate away from this project by pointing out a Nicaraguan volcano that had become active. One of the country's stamps had a picture of the volcano engraved on it. The day after it became active, one of these stamps was placed on each senator's desk with this message:

"An official witness of the volcanic activity of Nicaragua."

The canal, finally built in Panama, is still a subject of foreign propaganda. Panama is now engaged in an extensive drive to get more rent and other economic concessions. We now pay Panama $430,000 yearly for "use, occupation, and control" of the 10-mile-wide Canal Zone.

The canal is operated by the Army. So when Panama wanted help, it hired a couple of former generals. Brig. Gen. Julius Klein. and Maj. Gen. Kenneth Buchanan, both public relations counsels, will get $40,000 for conducting a press campaign.
