The piece emphasizes that existing American financial supervision over the Dominican Republic and Nicaragua is regarded across the region as a disguised, creeping advance upon local territory. Latin-Americans Don't Trust Us. Our Monroe Doctrine May Protect Them From Europe, But What, They Ask, Protects Them From Us! By Albert Bushnell Hart, Professor of Government in Harvard University

The people of the United States like to be liked, and think they deserve to be liked. Whenever they travel they are proud of their country and willing to register from their home town, whether it be Podunk or Philadelphia. Especially we feel like being liked by our near neighbors in Mexico, Central America and South America. We are good republicans and we taught their infant foot steps to move in the path of popular government. We are good business men and are ready to sell to them on safe and profitable terms to buy whatever we can take from them cheaper than we can get it elsewhere. It is, therefore, a grief to discover that many Latin-Americans do not reciprocate these kindly feelings.

Were we to judge only from the citizens of these neighboring republics whom we see in the states, we might believe that we were accepted as warm friends. Several hundred Latin-American students have come to our schools and colleges and technical institutions, they like us well enough to come and spend several years with us and they expect to make their results useful by becoming leaders among their own people. These students are a lively, wide-awake, friendly lot of young folks, and we should like to have more of them come. Then there are the diplomats some of whom spend years in Washington as the envoys of their states. Senor Romero, for many years minister from Mexico, was a warm personal friend of General Grant. Naon of Brazil, and Perez of Peru, have been favorites wherever they have gone in the United States, and nobody can doubt their sincere desire for friendship between their home countries and this country.

Out in South America the Yankees are not so popular as they are at home. Of late years there has been a succession of travelers who have gone down to learn the spirit of the countries, many of them hoping to sell goods. No Latin-American is willing to express his full mind to a visitor; but many of these travelers have brought back the strong impression that the North Americans are disliked by the South Americans. James Bryce, who was certainly impartial on this subject, found a prejudice against the people of the United States. Explorers and scientific men like Hiram Bingham, who had every wish to think otherwise, are convinced that the overwhelming point of view is that the Yankees are selfish and are looking for national as well as private advantage.

We could get on well even if we felt that the visiting New Yorker or Californian was not made an intimate friend by the Chileans or Mexicans whom he met. It is, however, a serious matter that the Latin-Americans suspect us of designs against their welfare and liberty. The Monroe doctrine which, they understand, ninety years ago was meant for their protection, has directly protected them only two or three times: the French were turned out of Mexico in 1867, and the English, Germans and Italians were refused the opportunity to take possession of Venezuelan territory in 1902. Probably other states might have been attacked but for the belief that Uncle Sam was waiting around the corner, ready to defend his weaker neighbors. All this the Latin-Americans are willing to accept; but they want a stronger doctrine which will protect them against the United States.

Every Latin-American knows that the strip of Mexico north of the Rio Grande, New Mexico and California, was taken by force from a Latin-American state. They do not trouble themselves to go back to the history of the Mexican war and see who was at fault. The thing that interests them is, who got the territory? In the last 17 years the United States has annexed the Latin-American community of Porto Rico, has annexed the canal zone, made a protectorate of Cuba and is now taking steps which seem likely to lead to the annexation of the whole island of Santo Domingo and of the Central American states. The present financial control of the Dominican republic and of Nicaragua looks to other Latin-American states like a slow advance upon their territory. They are not in the least affected by the argument that these are miserable, disturbed countries which can be made happier by the benevolent guidance of Uncle Sam. The Latin-Americans claim the right to be poor and disturbed; they do not ask or desire us to give them a better government. Whatever our motives or our kindly feelings, the Latin-Americans do not trust us.
