Latin Says U. S. Press 'Fabricated' Castro

By GARNETT D. HORNER

Star Staff Writer

Former President Jose Figueres of Costa Rica, himself an old guerrilla fighter against dictatorships, needled the United States press today with a charge that Cuban Premier Fidel Casro is its "fabrication."

Mr. Figueres, talking with newsmen at the White House after a conference with President Kennedy, also said there is widespread resentment in Latin America against the United States because this country ignored for too long the struggle of Latin American liberals against dictatorships and corruption.

But he emphasized that the task now is to "bury resentment—to go forward, not to look back."

The former Costa Rica President was peppered with questions regarding Cuba and Mr. Castro by newsmen when he emerged from Mr. Kennedy's office.

Cites McCarthy Publicity

Finally, he said he felt the American press was suffering from "Castroitis." He went on to say that "the United States press fabricated Castro—to the extent that it did Senator McCarthy earlier." He added that Mr. Castro's publicity in the press here when he was fighting former Cuban Dictator Fulgencio Batista a little more than two years ago "sold him to Latin America."

All over Latin America, he said, there is justified resentment against the United States for ignoring the fight in Latin America against dictatorships over the last several years "because you were too busy with other things."

Asked if he felt the United States had helped keep dictatorships in power in Latin America, Mr. Figueres said, "I think not deliberately, but certainly in fact." He mentioned American support in past years of Batista in Cuba and Generallissimo Trujillo in the Dominican Republic as examples.

"If it hadn't been for the United States," he added, "we would have got rid of dictatorships and dynasties a long time ago."

Offers Aid to Kennedy

Conceding that Latin America had many mistakes and misconceptions on its own side, he said he had come to the White House to offer any help he could to President Kennedy's "call to arms" for an inter-American "alliance for progress" to promote economic and social development throughout the hemisphere.

As for the situation in Cuba now, Mr. Figueres said his sympathies are with the anti-Castro invaders, but he has no information for appraising success of their move.

Asked if he had any ideas about what the United States should be doing in Cuba now, he smiled and said, "Wait and see."
