THE WEST INDIES. Facts About The Group of Islands Which Are Again The Scene of a Naval War. WORTH READING NOW.

Boston Post.

The West India Islands, the probable scene of many great naval battles in a war between the United States and Spain, have had more than their share of fighting since the romantic days of the Spanish Main, when England wrested from Spain supremacy on the sea. Nearly every one of the islands which curve about the Caribbean Sea played a part in the struggles between Spain and England in the seventeenth, and England and France in the eighteenth centuries.

Besides Havana and Matanzas, in Cuba, Spain has but one fortified harbor among the Antilles, San Juan Borrista, or St. John's, in Porto Rico. It is an excellent harbor, and could be strongly defended by the Vizcaya and Oquendo, with the torpedo boats, against our squadron. But the latter alone could do nothing in St. John's against our battleships. The fortifications are old-fashioned and the guns are useless against iron ships.

In Southern Cuba, the harbor of Santiago de Cuba could be defended with a weak force, as the entrance is narrow and deep, with forts naturally strong. The forts are not modern, however, and the Spanish government holds but a limited area around the city, so that the Spanish will probably make the main defense at Havana and everything be made subservient to holding the capital. In this case, with the Oquendo and Vizcaya reaching Havana the great battle of the war will be fought at the entrance to the harbor, or the American fleet will blockade and starve the city into surrender, aided by the Cuban army.

The sympathy of both of the black republics in Hayti is with the United States as against any European power. A moment's consideration of their history will explain this. The two republics, Hayti in the West and San Domingo in the East, are the only countries in the West Indies whose form of government is like our own except, of course, the republic of Cuba. Toussaint l'Ouverture's successful struggle against France and his capture by treachery, followed by the complete expulsion of the French, is one of liberty's favorite tales. The Spanish invaded San Domingo in 1861 and their defeat left the islanders free.

There are quite a million people on the island, but they dally on the way to civilization. There is no army, so Hayti's only use to us would be in lending us a harbor. There are no harbors on the north coast, but Port au Prince, in the republic of Hayti, is a well-protected port lying behind the island of Gonave. In the Dominican republic there are the harbors of Santo Domingo and Port Plate, which would make convenient bases for operations in the Caribbean.

The Mona Passage, only sixty miles wide, separates Hayti from the beautiful island of Porto Rico. Porto Rico is a valuable possession and Spain is putting up a rich stake on a turn of the war card when she risks her control of that fertile island.

St. John's is an ancient town. The old buildings are curious and interesting, especially the palace which was erected by Ponce de Leon, who conquered the island for Spain. The principal city on the southern coast is Ponce, with 40,000 inhabitants, situated three miles inland. Mayaguez, on the west coast, contains 30,000 people. It is separated from its port by a river spanned by an iron bridge. There is a barracks here, but no fort. The four other seaports of Porto Rico do not deserve notice. Their harbors—as in fact is the case with all the harbors of the island—are rapidly filling up with silt and are dangerous to heavy vessels.

In the group of the Virgin Islands, to the east, only the two Danish islands, St. Thomas and St. Croix, are probable factors in the present situation. Their importance would be considerably greater if they had been secured by the United States, as was at one time suggested by our State Department. The harbor of St. Croix is unfortified and not more than sixteen feet deep, so that it cannot be of much service as a base. The island is a hill that rises 1100 feet out of the water. Denmark bought it from France for $145,000 in 1733.

St. Thomas is but 100 miles away from St. John's. The harbor is nearly land-locked and has a depth of thirty-six feet. A dry dock is here and a steam-slip for small vessels. There is no more pleasing sight in the Indies than the harbor of Charlotte Amalie, the capital, and the towering peak fifteen hundred feet high, which forms the bulk of the island.

Point a Pitre, in Guadaloupe, is an important French station. That it has a fine military harbor may be concluded from its soubriquet, "The Gibraltar of the Antilles." Nevertheless, this Gibraltar is not as strongly fortified as it might be. It resisted the English successfully in 1665, '91 and 1703, but was taken by General Barrington and Admiral Moore in 1759. The celebrated victory of Rodney over Admiral de Grasse occurred here. A curious feature of the island is La Riviere Sale (salt stream), 110 to 400 feet wide, which divides it.

Martinique is not a great strategic point like Guadaloupe, but there are some interesting things about it. Mt. Pele, tallest of its volcanic mountain peaks, rises 4480 feet in the air. The population is nearly as dense as that of Belgium, there being 165,000 people to an island with greatest width and length forty-three and nineteen miles respectively. The place enjoys greater immunity from hurricanes than the other West India Islands, and the houses may be built there to a height of several stories.

The British possession, Dominica, is a large island, without a harbor.

The beautiful island of Trinidad, the land of humming birds, whence the asphalt of commerce comes, is not one of the Antilles, but it should be mentioned for its harbor. Port-of-Spain is one of the finest cities in the West Indies and a favorite anchorage for United States ships. In 1895 the crews of the New York, Cincinnati and Raleigh saved the city from total destruction by fire.
