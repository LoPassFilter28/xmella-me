CONSTANTINE BROWN Left-of-Center Regimes Preferred Leaders of the Right Found Concerned At Policy Trend in Washington

ROME.—A hitherto rather foggy picture of American foreign policies as seen from Europe is finally beginning to come into somewhat better focus. It was put clearly by the well-known London Daily Telegraph journalist Peregrine Worsthorne, on March 24. "Incredible as it may seem," he wrote, "Washington is beginning itself to use the well-known phrase, 'an opening to the left,' not with fear and trembling but with hope and approval . . . the men who today talk Washington's language (are) Fanfani of Italy, Spaak of Belgium and Brandt of West Germany."

This preference for left-of-center governments by Washington is basically what gravely concerns such leaders of the right as De Gaulle, Adenauer, Franco and Salazar. If an "opening to the left" brought strength and stability there would be no worry. But in the last few years there have been tragic examples where the United States has thrown its weight against conservative governments. Syngman Rhee's South Korea is an example. Today South Korea is in a state of dangerous ferment and again Washington is letting it be known that anti-Communist strongman Gen. Chung Hee Park is out of favor.

In rapid succession, other conservative governments began to fall after 1960. Menderes in Turkey fell before rioters, and today the Turkish political volcano is about to erupt. President Betancourt of Venezuela, greatly favored in Washington, is fighting for his life against Castro infiltration. Leftist President Goulart of Brazil is on the brink. We assisted indirectly the overthrow of the Trujillo regime in the Dominican Republic only to create a left-of-center government headed by President Juan Bosch.

We spent millions in aid to Leftist Sukarno in Indonesia and that country is in the Soviet orbit. Laos was certainly not a successful diplomatic achievement for us, nor were many other areas around the globe where disaster has struck following an opening to the left. Even without these examples, Cuba should have been sufficient to prove the danger of the policy.

What seems to be the greatest significance to many European leaders is that since bare-faced communism has come to Cuba and threatens to spread like a cancer throughout the hemisphere, Washington seems only to worry about American public opinion and nothing else. And if Washington prefers the choice of trustful negotiations in regard to the Soviet military might in Cuba, would it, perchance, prefer the choice of negotiations rather than retaliation in the event Europe should be threatened with the Kremlin's ambitions?

Latin America has become more our first line of defense than Asia and Europe. And the main reason for Europe's skeptical evaluation of our expressed determination to make the NATO a strong force, is based on our Latin American policies. They realize that they have not changed from those of President Eisenhower when he stated that he had no intention of using armed intervention against Mr. Castro so long as he did not attack Guantanamo. It is also obvious that the policy stated by Vice President Nixon in South America — a warm abrazo for democracies and a cold shoulder for dictators —is even more favored by the New Frontier. Since 1959 Latin American dictators of the right have been either ousted or assassinated and the Soviet Union has established a strong beachhead in our hemisphere as a result.

It is clear to the Europeans that communism is closing the ring around the mighty American giant while Washington naively calls it an "opening to the left, not with fear and trembling but with hope and approval."

A thin red pencil line is slowly being drawn from the Hudson Straits in Canada to the Caribbean around South America to Central America and up our coast to Alaska where recently Soviet planes flew on spy flights. And the core of the Red cancer is in the Caribbean from Cuba off the tip of Florida to the Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico, the West Indies, British Guiana, Venezuela, Colombia, Panama, Central America and on to the Rio Grande.

Only a faint Red dot appears in Canada where the conservatives are steadily losing and Communists have announced that "Canada needs another Castro," claiming that there will be a Canadian Castro within a few years.

The living examples of too large a number of leftist governments which have become boiling caldrons of political turmoil and eventually taken over by the Communists or pro-Soviets are the main reasons European rightists fear Washington's policies.
