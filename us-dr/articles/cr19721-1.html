ST. DOMINGO.

“The American Gibraltar of the Antilles.”

History, Geography and Local Importance to the United States:

Samana Bay, American Trade and Military and Naval Positions.

ST. DOMINGO, W. I., July 12, 1869.

The presence of Luperon’s piratical craft, the Telegrafo, in the waters of this bay not only excludes the commerce under the Dominican flag, but also interferes with the pursuits of vessels of other nations. The fact of Luperon having no papers, except those which he obtained from the late American Consul at St. Thomas, and by virtue of which, after several failures through other sources, he was enabled to depart on his illicit mission under the American flag, was in itself sufficient warning against trusting to the caprices of this unrecognized belligerent individual. It was, therefore, deemed prudent not to venture beyond the Cay Levantado. It was hardly presumed that Luperon would dare to take absolute possession of an American vessel, but it was certain that he would not hesitate to detain her.

My despatch, written in advance of this, gives details of the brilliant little engagement between the Dominican war schooners Capotilla and Alta Gracia and the pirate Telegrafo, and the defeat of the latter. At the same time I have been able, through actual observation and from various sources, to obtain much information respecting this magnificent bay. The pirate, lying far in the bay, succeeded in reaching that part stretching from the headlands at the entrance to the Cay Levantado. This portion constitutes the main bay, and in event of the United States becoming the fortunate possessor of this powerful and convenient naval station, would form the anchorage of the fleet, and the islands and the adjoining shores the sites of the government fortifications, docks, warehouses, magazines, workshops and other necessary buildings and improvements.

THE BAY OF SAMANA.

Standing in a northwesterly direction from Cape Engaño, the most eastern point of the island of St. Domingo, and passing Cape Raphael, distance about fifty miles, we arrived off the entrance to the Bay of Samana. The coast is bold, and in many places rises perpendicularly out of the sea. The approach is well defined by high headlands on the coast, and mountain ranges, prolific with vegetation, in the interior. On the north side the conical crest of Azucar de Pilou, or Sugar Loaf Mountain, nearly 2,000 feet in height, and La Montana del Diablo, with an altitude of 1,300 feet, form excellent landmarks to the approaching navigator. The entering channel lying on the north, along the peninsula of Samaná, vessels bound into the bay move up close to the north shores.

At the eastern end of the peninsula are two capes, the most northern Cape Cabron, a conspicuous white cliff, and Cape Samana, a bold, rocky promontory near the channels. Between these two capes stretches a high perpendicular curtain of red cliffs with blue water under their very shadows. Penetrating the entrance between two less prominent projections—Vaca Point and Balandra Head—the shore is still bold and rocky. The latter Head is of red formation, apparently sandstone. Passing Cape Samana the main bay has the appearance of a capacious bight sinking deep into the neighboring shore. Upon closer examination, however, this sheet of water is well encircled by land and perfectly protected. Between Cape Samana on the north and Cape Raphael on the south the mouth measures twenty-one miles north, west three-fourths north, reckoned from Cape Raphael. From this line, including the inner harbor, as we may term it, the bay extends thirty miles to the mouth of the Yuna river. This stream is one of the largest on the island of St. Domingo and enters the bay at the extreme western shore. The breadth of the bay about midway its depth from the towns of Samana on its northern to Savannah la Mar on its southern shore is eight miles. Ten miles west of Cape Raphael, near Punta Jicaco, begins a reef which stretches northwestward to a number of islets or rocks, called Cays (Keys) Pascual, Levantado and Carenero, better known as the Bamsier Cays. This reef protects the basin of the bay against the heavy seas which otherwise a gale from the north and east would send into it. The passage for the entrance and exit of vessels is thus contracted to one mile between Punta Cacao and Cayo Pascual. In the middle of this channel is a small reef, marked on the charts and called Jean Bart, having three and three-fourths fathoms, or twenty-four feet, of water. This could be indicated by a buoy. At other points not less than six fathoms, or thirty-six feet, of water is found, while a channel exists, well defined on the charts, showing nowhere less than eight fathoms, or forty-eight feet. The reef Jean Bart lies northwest six cable lengths from the north point of Cayo Pascual, which would make it hazardous for sailing vessels of large size to leave with the prevailing sea breeze. It is advisable to stand out by the land breeze only. Beside the principal bay there are several side anchorages or coves (surge depots), extending into the land, the principal of which is the Bahia de San Lorenzo, or De las Perlas, on the southern shore, west of Savannah la Mar.

This bay forms an excellent port. From its eastern point a shoal stretches about half a cable’s length. The entrance to the bay presents from six and a half to eighteen fathoms. In 1854, during negotiations then going on between the United States and the republic of Dominica in relation to the acquisition of the Bay of Samaná and a portion of the peninsula, General (then Captain) George B. McClellan was sent out by the United States government to make some preliminary surveys. In regard to the result he wrote the following letter:— UNITED STATES SHIP COLUMBIA,} BAY OF SAMANA, August 5, 1854. } WILLIAM L. CAZNEAU, United States Commissioner:—

SIR—I enclose herewith a sketch of the entrance of the bay of Samana, on which you will perceive marked in red lines the place I have selected as a coal depot, &c.; the bays that it is necessary to possess are marked with red crosses. You will observe that the tract selected includes Ponito Carenero and most of that of Santa Clara, as well as all the Levantado Cays. In a military point of view and for the purpose of forming an important naval depot it would be highly desirable to obtain the whole peninsula of Samana and Levantado Cays.

If this cannot be done, or is not contemplated by your instructions, the tract I have alluded to above and marked on the sketch is the next best thing that can be obtained. With regard to this I cannot too strongly urge the necessity of obtaining the Levantado Cays. If any depot established here is to be fortified and held in time of war the possession of them is a sine qua non; they control the entire bay, so that without them our position would scarcely be tenable, while with them we should be entirely secure. The possession of Point Cacao, although not absolutely necessary if we hold the cays and Carenero Point, is highly desirable and should by all means be obtained if possible. The points within the specified tract marked “B” and “C” are commanding points, necessary to cover the depot against any land attack. It would answer every purpose if our line included the crest and a portion of the farther slope of the ridges of which they are a part. * * *

Should the worst come to worst the Levantado Cays afford a good anchorage, a strong defensive position and room enough for a tolerable depot, the possession of these cays alone should be a last resort, although we should have them in connection with any tract on the peninsula. * * * I am, sir, very respectfully, your obedient servant, GEO. B. McCLELLAN, Brevet Captain Engineers, U. S. A.

L. Howard Newman, passed midshipman, United States Navy, on board the Columbia, at the same time having received orders to co-operate in the survey by taking soundings, in a communication of August 5, 1854, made the following report:—

In obedience to an order from Commodore Newton directing me to ascertain, by sounding, the suitableness of the site selected by Captain McClellan, United States Army, for a coal depot, &c., and to report thereon, I have the honor to inform you that, entering the small bay between Points Lirio and Carenero from the eastward you have ten and eleven fathoms water at the entrance and then about six fathoms in the middle of the bay, gradually shoaling to the beach on the northward, and to the southward keeping about the same depth till you approach the reef. At the western extremity there is a small coral reef or shoal, with from six to nine feet of water upon it, and around it about five and six fathoms. This, however, might be serviceable in constructing a wharf. The water at this end of the bay is deep enough for steamers of the largest class, and a wharf of between 200 and 300 feet in length would allow vessels to lay alongside in four fathoms water. There is also a passage into this bay from the southward between the small island of Carenero Chiquito and the western point of the reef. This entrance has from seven to thirteen fathoms of water. To the westward of Point Lirio, in the Bahia de Clara, there is also an anchorage and a convenient place for building a wharf, alongside of which vessels might lay in security.

On the northward of the larger Banniser Cay there is a very good anchorage, the smaller cay and the shoal between the cays and mainland forming a good breakwater against the heavy swell which might set in from the eastward. The depth of water varies from six to ten fathoms, and quite close to the beach there are four fathoms of water, so that in case it was found practicable to establish a small depot upon the northwestern part of the cay a wharf of little expense would afford facilities for coaling steamers. The approach to this latter anchorage is probably the least difficult of the three, although none present any very serious obstacles, particularly to vessels propelled by steam.

A letter from Commodore John Thomas Newton, commanding the home squadron, dated on board the United States flagship Columbia, Samaná Bay, July 26, 1854, says:—“I arrived here yesterday afternoon, entering this magnificent bay without meeting with any difficulty. We commenced watering the ships this morning from a delightful stream half a mile distant from our anchorage. It is pure, excellent water, and we manage to get it off with very little trouble. The surrounding country is beautiful as we view it from the ship. There is a fine anchorage in every direction.”

From these facts the commodious character, as well as the remarkable natural advantages possessed by the bay of Samaná, may be judged. Under the protection of the Cay Levantado and communicating reefs the largest fleet in the world may lie perfectly secured from the sea and weather. This portion of the bay would answer admirably for an anchorage for ships out of commission, while the spacious bay outside of Levantado might be appropriated for vessels in active service. The shores around the bay are abrupt, and the largest vessels can lie in close. Around the Cay Levantado there is a sufficient depth of water to admit of vessels getting in so close as to communicate with the island by a common gang plank, by which coal could be wheeled upon coaling steamers. The expansive surface of the bay would also admit of manœuvring vessels with an abundance of room.
