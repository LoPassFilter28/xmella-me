ST. DOMINGO.

“The American Gibraltar of the Antilles.”

History, Geography and Local Importance to the United States:

Samana Bay, American Trade and Military and Naval Positions.

ST. DOMINGO, W. I., July 12, 1869.

The presence of Luperon’s piratical craft, the Telegrafo, in the waters of this bay not only excludes the commerce under the Dominican flag, but also interferes with the pursuits of vessels of other nations. The fact of Luperon having no papers, except those which he obtained from the late American Consul at St. Thomas, and by virtue of which, after several failures through other sources, he was enabled to depart on his illicit mission under the American flag, was in itself sufficient warning against trusting to the caprices of this unrecognized belligerent individual. It was, therefore, deemed prudent not to venture beyond the Cay Levantado. It was hardly presumed that Luperon would dare to take absolute possession of an American vessel, but it was certain that he would not hesitate to detain her.

My despatch, written in advance of this, gives details of the brilliant little engagement between the Dominican war schooners Capotilla and Alta Gracia and the pirate Telegrafo, and the defeat of the latter. At the same time I have been able, through actual observation and from various sources, to obtain much information respecting this magnificent bay. The pirate, lying far in the bay, succeeded in reaching that part stretching from the headlands at the entrance to the Cay Levantado. This portion constitutes the main bay, and in event of the United States becoming the fortunate possessor of this powerful and convenient naval station, would form the anchorage of the fleet, and the islands and the adjoining shores the sites of the government fortifications, docks, warehouses, magazines, workshops and other necessary buildings and improvements.

THE BAY OF SAMANA.

Standing in a northwesterly direction from Cape Engaño, the most eastern point of the island of St. Domingo, and passing Cape Raphael, distance about fifty miles, we arrived off the entrance to the Bay of Samana. The coast is bold, and in many places rises perpendicularly out of the sea. The approach is well defined by high headlands on the coast, and mountain ranges, prolific with vegetation, in the interior. On the north side the conical crest of Azucar de Pilou, or Sugar Loaf Mountain, nearly 2,000 feet in height, and La Montana del Diablo, with an altitude of 1,300 feet, form excellent landmarks to the approaching navigator. The entering channel lying on the north, along the peninsula of Samaná, vessels bound into the bay move up close to the north shores.

At the eastern end of the peninsula are two capes, the most northern Cape Cabron, a conspicuous white cliff, and Cape Samana, a bold, rocky promontory near the channels. Between these two capes stretches a high perpendicular curtain of red cliffs with blue water under their very shadows. Penetrating the entrance between two less prominent projections—Vaca Point and Balandra Head—the shore is still bold and rocky. The latter Head is of red formation, apparently sandstone. Passing Cape Samana the main bay has the appearance of a capacious bight sinking deep into the neighboring shore. Upon closer examination, however, this sheet of water is well encircled by land and perfectly protected. Between Cape Samana on the north and Cape Raphael on the south the mouth measures twenty-one miles north, west three-fourths north, reckoned from Cape Raphael. From this line, including the inner harbor, as we may term it, the bay extends thirty miles to the mouth of the Yuna river. This stream is one of the largest on the island of St. Domingo and enters the bay at the extreme western shore. The breadth of the bay about midway its depth from the towns of Samana on its northern to Savannah la Mar on its southern shore is eight miles. Ten miles west of Cape Raphael, near Punta Jicaco, begins a reef which stretches northwestward to a number of islets or rocks, called Cays (Keys) Pascual, Levantado and Carenero, better known as the Bamsier Cays. This reef protects the basin of the bay against the heavy seas which otherwise a gale from the north and east would send into it. The passage for the entrance and exit of vessels is thus contracted to one mile between Punta Cacao and Cayo Pascual. In the middle of this channel is a small reef, marked on the charts and called Jean Bart, having three and three-fourths fathoms, or twenty-four feet, of water. This could be indicated by a buoy. At other points not less than six fathoms, or thirty-six feet, of water is found, while a channel exists, well defined on the charts, showing nowhere less than eight fathoms, or forty-eight feet. The reef Jean Bart lies northwest six cable lengths from the north point of Cayo Pascual, which would make it hazardous for sailing vessels of large size to leave with the prevailing sea breeze. It is advisable to stand out by the land breeze only. Beside the principal bay there are several side anchorages or coves (surge depots), extending into the land, the principal of which is the Bahia de San Lorenzo, or De las Perlas, on the southern shore, west of Savannah la Mar.

This bay forms an excellent port. From its eastern point a shoal stretches about half a cable’s length. The entrance to the bay presents from six and a half to eighteen fathoms. In 1854, during negotiations then going on between the United States and the republic of Dominica in relation to the acquisition of the Bay of Samaná and a portion of the peninsula, General (then Captain) George B. McClellan was sent out by the United States government to make some preliminary surveys. In regard to the result he wrote the following letter:— UNITED STATES SHIP COLUMBIA,} BAY OF SAMANA, August 5, 1854. } WILLIAM L. CAZNEAU, United States Commissioner:—

SIR—I enclose herewith a sketch of the entrance of the bay of Samana, on which you will perceive marked in red lines the place I have selected as a coal depot, &c.; the bays that it is necessary to possess are marked with red crosses. You will observe that the tract selected includes Ponito Carenero and most of that of Santa Clara, as well as all the Levantado Cays. In a military point of view and for the purpose of forming an important naval depot it would be highly desirable to obtain the whole peninsula of Samana and Levantado Cays.

If this cannot be done, or is not contemplated by your instructions, the tract I have alluded to above and marked on the sketch is the next best thing that can be obtained. With regard to this I cannot too strongly urge the necessity of obtaining the Levantado Cays. If any depot established here is to be fortified and held in time of war the possession of them is a sine qua non; they control the entire bay, so that without them our position would scarcely be tenable, while with them we should be entirely secure. The possession of Point Cacao, although not absolutely necessary if we hold the cays and Carenero Point, is highly desirable and should by all means be obtained if possible. The points within the specified tract marked “B” and “C” are commanding points, necessary to cover the depot against any land attack. It would answer every purpose if our line included the crest and a portion of the farther slope of the ridges of which they are a part. * * *

Should the worst come to worst the Levantado Cays afford a good anchorage, a strong defensive position and room enough for a tolerable depot, the possession of these cays alone should be a last resort, although we should have them in connection with any tract on the peninsula. * * * I am, sir, very respectfully, your obedient servant, GEO. B. McCLELLAN, Brevet Captain Engineers, U. S. A.

L. Howard Newman, passed midshipman, United States Navy, on board the Columbia, at the same time having received orders to co-operate in the survey by taking soundings, in a communication of August 5, 1854, made the following report:—

In obedience to an order from Commodore Newton directing me to ascertain, by sounding, the suitableness of the site selected by Captain McClellan, United States Army, for a coal depot, &c., and to report thereon, I have the honor to inform you that, entering the small bay between Points Lirio and Carenero from the eastward you have ten and eleven fathoms water at the entrance and then about six fathoms in the middle of the bay, gradually shoaling to the beach on the northward, and to the southward keeping about the same depth till you approach the reef. At the western extremity there is a small coral reef or shoal, with from six to nine feet of water upon it, and around it about five and six fathoms. This, however, might be serviceable in constructing a wharf. The water at this end of the bay is deep enough for steamers of the largest class, and a wharf of between 200 and 300 feet in length would allow vessels to lay alongside in four fathoms water. There is also a passage into this bay from the southward between the small island of Carenero Chiquito and the western point of the reef. This entrance has from seven to thirteen fathoms of water. To the westward of Point Lirio, in the Bahia de Clara, there is also an anchorage and a convenient place for building a wharf, alongside of which vessels might lay in security.

On the northward of the larger Banniser Cay there is a very good anchorage, the smaller cay and the shoal between the cays and mainland forming a good breakwater against the heavy swell which might set in from the eastward. The depth of water varies from six to ten fathoms, and quite close to the beach there are four fathoms of water, so that in case it was found practicable to establish a small depot upon the northwestern part of the cay a wharf of little expense would afford facilities for coaling steamers. The approach to this latter anchorage is probably the least difficult of the three, although none present any very serious obstacles, particularly to vessels propelled by steam.

A letter from Commodore John Thomas Newton, commanding the home squadron, dated on board the United States flagship Columbia, Samaná Bay, July 26, 1854, says:—“I arrived here yesterday afternoon, entering this magnificent bay without meeting with any difficulty. We commenced watering the ships this morning from a delightful stream half a mile distant from our anchorage. It is pure, excellent water, and we manage to get it off with very little trouble. The surrounding country is beautiful as we view it from the ship. There is a fine anchorage in every direction.”

From these facts the commodious character, as well as the remarkable natural advantages possessed by the bay of Samaná, may be judged. Under the protection of the Cay Levantado and communicating reefs the largest fleet in the world may lie perfectly secured from the sea and weather. This portion of the bay would answer admirably for an anchorage for ships out of commission, while the spacious bay outside of Levantado might be appropriated for vessels in active service. The shores around the bay are abrupt, and the largest vessels can lie in close. Around the Cay Levantado there is a sufficient depth of water to admit of vessels getting in so close as to communicate with the island by a common gang plank, by which coal could be wheeled upon coaling steamers. The expansive surface of the bay would also admit of manœuvring vessels with an abundance of room.

PHYSICAL FEATURES OF THE PENINSULA.

At the northeastern point of the island of St. Domingo, from the mainland along the northern shore of the Bay of Samana, stretches a narrow tongue of land thirty-two miles due east. The most eastern point of this peninsula, forming a bold and abrupt promontory standing high out of the sea, is known as Cape Samana or Cape Rezon, called Cabo de San Feramo by Columbus. Formerly at the western extremity of the peninsula existed an open passage from sea to sea, used by the boatmen of Samana. This passage is still used, though it is now much filled up with sand. The isthmus is low. The lofty summits of the Monte Christi chain cease on approaching it and rise again on the eastern bank of the Gran Estero, the western extremity of the peninsula. From west to east, as I have said, Samana is thirty-two miles. The low land near the isthmus is about five miles wide, with the greatest breadth between Punta Balandra and Cape Cabron, where it is eleven miles. Samaná is traversed by a central chain, rising highest at the eastern part. Pilon de Azucar, or Sugar Loaf Mountain, near Cape Cabron, by measurement is 1,936 feet in height, and La Montana del Diablo 1,300 feet. The area of Samana is 225 square miles, with a circumference of ninety-five miles, following the bays.

Several rivers find their outlet into Samana bay, which affords an abundance of fresh water for the use of vessels. The principal of these is the river Yuna, which reaches the bay at its western extremity. The Yuna rises in the central mountain chain on the highest peak of the Cibao, and having passed close to the town of Cotuy receives on its left the Camu. The Yuna has two mouths in the bay and one northward into the Atlantic. In each there exists a bar with about three to four and a half feet of water. I learn from Arthur Pennel, a young English engineer, to whom I am indebted for much personal information respecting the physical features of St. Domingo, and who made a special exploration of the Yuna, that its course is very tortuous and during the rainy season the current is exceedingly strong. Boats formerly ascended the river some miles. The stream is navigable inside the bars for boats drawing four to five feet of water as far as the junction of the Quaba.

The Yuna, with a large population on its banks, would prove a valuable tributary of supplies by native craft to a population on the shores of Samana. The stream, with its branches, courses through La Vega Real (the Royal Meadow), one of the most fertile valleys in the world, and also with the mineral districts of Maymon and the pine forests of the Cibao. In 1867 the trade of the greater portion of the valley, which stretches from the Bay of Samana to Cotuy on the Yuna and Conception on the Camu, averaging eight miles in width, was carried on by native canoes of about two tons burden.

A railroad is in contemplation from the head of the bay to the towns of Conception and Santiago, which will go far to open the country.

Towards the southward from the Yuna are the anchorages of Barranca Chica and Barranca Grande, both anchorages not now used. The Naranjo river here enters the Bay of Samana, passing under a hill of calcareous rock, through which it has forced an outlet.

The central chain of mountains traversing the peninsula of Samaná from west to east in its geographical formation consists principally of mountain limestone, schistose rocks, with veins of calcareous spar and sandstone. Quartz occurs frequently, and at the eastern end of the peninsula there is found an abundance of mica slates. A large quantity of fossil shells in great variety are met with. Near the rivulet of Almençon veins of bituminous coal have been discovered. The specimens already produced indicate too much bitumen to be conveniently employed on steamers. The veins, however, have not been developed, and it is possible that a better quality exists. The coal deposits, according to information I have derived from Mr. Pennel, commence at a point called Los Robelas, about ten miles to the westward of the town of Santa Barbara, and crops out at intervals as far as Las Canitas, at the head of the bay, and it is thought extends to the base of the mountains separating La Vega Real from the sea.

The soil of the peninsula of Samaná, like all other portions of the island of St. Domingo, is highly productive. Its principal woods are extensive forests of mahogany, espinello, caya, cavina and other cabinet woods; roble and asaroble wood, suitable for shipbuilding; capa, valuable for bottoms of vessels, and lignumvitæ. The soil and climate have also produced indigo, cacao, cotton and coffee. The present inhabitants are satisfied with cultivating yams, India corn and fruits, chiefly bananas, plantains, pineapples, oranges, mangoes, alligator pears and cocoanuts, while from the jungle and the forest they gather, as the cravings of taste may dictate, other fruits in endless variety and inexhaustible abundance.

The bay of Samaná abounds with fish, which is an important article of diet with the people on its shores. In spring whales visit the adjacent sea. Near the mouth of the Yuna river oysters are found in large quantities. Aquatic birds visit the bay in great nummers, chiefly flamingoes, spoonbills, scarlet ibis, snipe and many other varieties.

THE POPULATION

subsisting on the shores of the great bay of Samana. The primitive people, who are said by Columbus and others to have been very numerous, were the Ciguayens, and were a fierce and warlike people. The population now consists of the descendants of the Canarians or Islenos, the French refugees from Hayti, creoles from the same country and the Spaniards of St. Domingo. In 1824 another element was added in the arrival of a few free colored people from the States of Pennsylvania and Maryland. About 800 of these people and their children are still living, the majority at Samana. These people rank among the most intelligent and industrious on the island. In 1851 the population of the town of Samana was about 1,800 souls, of which nearly 300 were colored Americans. The chief commerce of Samana is with Turk’s Island, shipping provisions to that barren spot, and receiving in return quantities of salt.

The small town of Santa Barbara is situated on the acclivities bordering a small bight on the north side of the bay, about six miles northwest of Cay Levantado. It was founded in 1756 by Don Francisco Rubroy Peñarando, then Governor of St. Domingo.

On the southern shore, near the mouth of the Yabau, is the village of Savanna La Mar, containing a few hundred inhabitants.

EARLY HISTORY AND IMPORTANCE OF SAMANA.

The commodious and unparalleled bay of Samaná was one of the earliest attractions in the physical features of ancient Hispaniola. Since that time its convenience of geographical position from a strategic point of view and the accommodations afforded by its well sheltered and placid waters have associated it with all the warlike operations of the Antilles. A brief review of these historical associations at this time will not be inappropriate, and will point out at once the value placed upon the position by other Powers. After his discovery of the country, which he named Hispaniola, now St. Domingo, and giving some attention to a preliminary exploration of its coast, the great navigator, Columbus, set out on his return to old Spain. In January, 1493, he passed a high headland, which he named Cabo del Enamorado (the Lover’s Cape), now Cape Cabron. Further eastward he saw another, which he named Cabo San Feramo, now Cape Samaná, which formed the most eastern point of the peninsula of the same name. Doubling this bold headland, Columbus saw opened before him an expansive gulf which he supposed to be an arm of the sea. Charmed by the beauties of the place he cast anchor in the bay, determined to give some attention to a further examination of its advantages. A large native population, called Ciguayens, a fierce race, inhabited the shores. Columbus landed some of his men with the desire to open friendly relations, when the natives assailed the party. A lively fight ensued, in which the first blood between European and the primitive people of the New World was shed. In consequence of this difficulty Columbus named the portion of the bay nearest the scene of the fight El Golfe de las Fleechas (the Gulf of Arrows). After passing three days in the bay Columbus departed for the Old World, carrying with him glowing accounts not only of the lands he had discovered but also of the great bay. In the subsequent operations of the Spanish, the bay of Samana was frequently visited and was a place of refuge in times of storm for vessels sailing along the coast.

Fully appreciating the advantages of Samaná upon the nuns of the French community in the beginning of the eighteenth century, the Spanish government established a settlement. A small town was built in 1756, together with a church, dedicated to Santa Barbara. A village on the southern side of the bay was called Savanna de la Mar. During these early operations the importance and value of Samana was fully recognized by the rival Powers in the New World. In 1763 Count d’Estaing, as Governor General of the French island of Martinique, conceived the idea of securing the cession of the bay by Spain to France, “and all the northern coast from Monte Christi to Samana, a district including the fertile valleys of the rivers Yaque and Yuna.” These negotiations were suddenly broken off by the court of Madrid. The rival interests of the two Powers on the island generally were finally adjusted by defining the boundary between the French possessions in the west and the Spanish in the east of Hispaniola or St. Domingo, according to the Treaty of Limits settled in 1777. By this arrangement Samana remained in the hands of Spain. When the insurrection of slaves broke out in the French colony a number of colonists and planters sought refuge in Spanish territory. Some of these fugitives joined the settlers at Samana. By the treaty of Basle, July 22, 1795, between France and Spain, the latter Power ceded to France the eastern part of the island of St. Domingo in consideration of France giving up her conquests in the Pyrenees. Toussaint l’Ouverture, then general-in-chief of the forces in St. Domingo, marched upon the city of St. Domingo to take possession of the territory in the name of France. In January, 1801, the tricolor waved over the whole island, from Cape des Trois to Cape Engano. Doubting Toussaint, the First Consul despatched General Le Clerc, his brother-in-law, to St. Domingo with a formidable fleet of sixty war vessels and 30,000 troops. This fleet rendezvoused in the Bay of Samana in January, 1802. Upon hearing of its arrival Toussaint, who was at the head of his forces at the capital, mounted his horse, and, with his customary energy, galloped to the scene of this threatening intrusion. From the bold promontory of Cape Samana he contemplated in the broad bay at his feet the power of France concentrated for an invasion of his country. While thus reconnoitring the fleet the manœuvres of the vessels preparatory to their mission of blood and devastation the negro warrior for a moment seemed struck with the magnitude of the war of opposition, which must inevitably ensue. He exclaimed to the officers who had accompanied him, “We must perish. All France is coming to St. Domingo. It has been deceived. It comes to take revenge and enslave the blacks.” Samana now received a French garrison.

In 1808, when Napoleon assumed the throne of Spain, the Supreme Junta of Seville delegated Don Torribio Montes, Governor of Porto Rico, to excite the colonists of St. Domingo against France. George III., by order in council July 4, 1808, made peace with Spain and united with that power in a war against Napoleon. The Spanish population on the eastern coast of the island, headed by Don Juan Sanchez Ramirez, formerly commandante of Cotuyrase, and at the battle of Palo Hincado defeated the French. Farrand, the French commander, shot himself in consequence of his misfortune. An English squadron, consisting of three frigates and two brigs from Jamaica, entered the Bay of Samaná in November of the same year, captured five vessels and took Fort St. Barbara. July 11, 1809, the city of St. Domingo capitulated to the English fleet and General Sanchez. Till the year 1821 Samaná was little heard of. Excited by movements in Mexico and Venezuela, St. Domingo declared herself independent of the mother country, and the insurgents hoisted the Colombian flag, calling the new State Spanish Hayti. General Boyer, President of the republic of Hayti, in 1822 made a sudden dash upon St. Domingo. The new government, little prepared to defend itself against this unexpected attack, surrendered to Boyer, who annexed the country to Hayti. In the same year Vice Admiral Jacob, with a French fleet of eleven vessels and 1,200 troops, anchored in the bay of Samana. The French landed, but the commander being informed by Boyer that upon any hostile act every Frenchman would be put to the sword, the French departed and abandoned whatever projects they may have then had in view in regard to Samana. For twenty-two years the Spanish portion of the Island of St. Domingo was held under subjection to the government of Hayti. In 1844, February 27, the Dominicans rose in their metropolis, and, overcoming the Haytien garrison, declared their liberty, under the name of the Dominican republic, with the motto, “God, our Country and Liberty” (Dios, Patria, Libertad). Great Britain first recognized the independence of the new republic, and in 1850 entered into a treaty with them. France and Denmark followed.

NEGOTIATIONS BY THE UNITED STATES.

In the year 1853 the advantages possessed by the bay and peninsula of Samana for a naval station were brought to the attention of the United States government by General William L. Cazneau. The plan suggested by him to Mr. Pierce, who was at that time President, was that our government should allow a fixed annual rent in perpetuity for the use and occupation of a selected position, but with the proviso that the bay and peninsula of Samana should be declared a free port and neutral of war forever. In 1854 the United States frigate Columbia, Commodore Newton, conveyed General Cazneau to St. Domingo, with powers to negotiate for a naval depot at Samana. Captain—now General— George B. McClellan accompanied the mission. Leaving General Cazneau at the capital of the island, the Columbia proceeded to Samana, where Captain McClellan made a survey of the coast on the north side, from Point Balandra to Fort Santa Barbara, about six miles, and the Levantado and adjacent keys and reefs. He also laid out a desirable tract of land, to be appropriated in event of success as the site for the station on shore. Passed Midshipman L. Howard Newman also took soundings of the entrance to the bay. The results of the labors of both these officers I have given in an appropriate place elsewhere in this despatch. Everything was favorable to a speedy consummation of the important project. The engineers were enthusiastic in their admiration of the bay and its facilities for a naval and commercial depot. The bargain was about to be closed at a mere nominal price, when the delicate negotiations leaked out, and the British and French Cabinets, alarmed at such a dangerous aggression, despatched at once two fleets, which concentrated before the capital of the island. The Dominican government was made to understand that no foothold on the island would be permitted to “such a dangerous Power as the United States.”

The Washington Cabinet, in a cowardly manner, summarily dropped all proceedings, not even supporting its negotiations with a dignified show of pride. The commissioner returned to the United States, not in the best humor, as much from the calmness of taking the insult which had been offered to the nation as from the failure of his project.

The question next came up in a new form. The government having failed, it was proposed to establish a free port on the principle and commercial basis of St. Thomas, with a United States mail and naval station on the site selected by McClellan. The plan was submitted to the consideration of some leading capitalists of New York—a plan for taking Samana on a permanent lease and subletting stations to the steam companies of any or all parts of the world. The capitalists and the Dominican government came into the arrangement, but made a condition that the United States government should agree by a formal treaty with the Dominican government to recognize the free and neutral status of Samana. This proposition was submitted to Mr. Buchanan, then President of the United States, in the early part of his administration. At the same time it was proposed by private parties that they would withdraw all individual interests if the government preferred acting on its own account. Buchanan at first hesitated, but was at length induced to inaugurate anew measures looking to the acquisition of Samana. Cazneau was again sent out, this time with conditional powers.

While the United States government was exhausting time and patience in stupid delays gangrenous Spain loomed up, seriously alarmed at the fresh dangers besetting her. The Castilian Envoy at Washington denounced the project as an act of war against his sovereign. The movers in the scheme were set down by the bewildered and alarmed plenipotentiary as Cuban filibusters, and the free port of Samana was in reality nothing more than a harbor and base of operations for revolutionists of the “ever faithful isle(?).” The press of France and Spain kept up a lively assault upon “the designs on Samana.” The United States government again receded, as an evidence of administration by weak hands and weaker heads.

In 1859 the United States government was advised of the hostile designs of Spain against St. Domingo, and again pressed the completion of the Samana question. While Mr. Buchanan was revolving the question in his unsettled mind the Dominican republic expired. A Spanish army occupied the cities of St. Domingo and a Spanish navy closely watched the coast. Under the new administration of Mr. Lincoln, Seward, whose main fault was a lack of clear and matured ideas upon the American question, left a wide field open to Spain by dropping the whole question. The Monroe doctrine, which he managed by force of circumstances to a more successful issue in Mexico, he entirely ignored in St. Domingo. Spain, taking advantage of the importance of Samana as developed in the negotiations of the United States for its possession, turned her attention to the establishment of a naval depot upon the very site laid out by McClellan in 1854, and had planned extensive fortifications to make it by the assistance of art what it was by nature—the Gibraltar of the Antilles. The timely uprising of the Dominican people, and the restoration of the republican constitution and flag, defeated the plans of Spain, and saved the United States, by no wisdom of her rulers, from having in the very line of her commerce in the Gulf of Mexico and the Caribbean sea a stronghold in the hands of a capricious and unfriendly nation.

The return of the legitimate form of government on the island opened afresh the question of Samana. The Dominican government lost no time, and in 1864 despatched an envoy to Washington to offer the bay and peninsula of Samana at a nominal rent on the basis of the original proposition. The envoy presented himself, and in return for the warm impulses which actuated the steps taken by the Dominican government, Mr. Seward uncourteously refused to receive the envoy or even to listen to him unofficially. Several senators were appealed to, and the advantages to accrue from the acquisition were so evident and met with so much favor that Mr. Seward, probably as an offset at this moment, began his negotiations with the Danish Court for the sterile peaks, diminutive harbor and undeveloped earthquakes of St. Thomas, at the same time the Secretary kept up a sort of awkward coquetry with St. Domingo. The Dominican Cabinet having been repeatedly put off, in turn exhibited great surprise at the sudden ardor of the American Minister, and, doubting the sincerity of his intentions, in view of the negotiations for St. Thomas, declined to treat. Again the question dropped.

In November, 1866, the negotiations were once more seriously opened between the two governments. President José Maria Cabral was in power, and looked favorably upon the prospects of a closer union between the United States and St. Domingo. After the repeated disgrace to which our flag and nation had been subjected by the conduct and common worthlessness of some of the commercial agents sent out by the United States to represent American interests, Mr. J. Somers Smith, a gentleman of character and social standing, was put in charge. Mr. Smith had had many years’ experience as consul in Spain, and was, therefore, fitted for the position. To him the new propositions were made for a lease of the coal mines of Samana and the Levantado and Carenero Keys. These facts Mr. Smith communicated to the department at Washington. Upon this letter Frederick Seward, Assistant Secretary of State, and Admiral D. D. Porter arrived at St. Domingo in January, 1867, in the United States steamer Gettysburg. Negotiations were at once opened, and continued five or six days. The representatives of the two governments could come to no terms. The Dominican representatives asked a joint occupation, which, on the part of the United States, Messrs. Seward and Porter declined to accept, on the ground that the United States would hold no property in conjunction with another Power. Here the question again terminated. The very next month, however, Consul Smith succeeded in consummating the first treaty between the two republics, termed “A General Convention of Amity, Commerce and Extradition,” which was exchanged October 5, 1867. This treaty was modelled on the plan of those with Venezuela and Hayti.

In February of the same year Consul Smith received from the State Department the full powers of a commissioner to negotiate the Samana matter. In August of the same year Hayti, becoming alarmed and evidently jealous of the benefits which would accrue to the Dominicans in the event of the United States possessing Samana, sent to the Dominican authorities a treaty which had in view nothing more than the prevention of the alienation of territory and a mutual agreement to put a stop to the revolutionary movements constantly fitting out in one country against the other.

Upon the downfall of Cabral and the accession of Bueneventura Baez, the present President, Samana once more became the topic of discussion. This was in March, 1868. The proposition was again to lease, to which the United States replied it was not willing to lease from any foreign Power.

PRESENT CONDITION OF THR SAMANA QUESTION.

Little if any credit belongs to the United States government for the efforts, at least, to secure to our people a possession which in the new relations of the United States with the rest of the world it most evidently requires. It would appear that humble American citizens have been more alive to the necessities of their government in the Gulf, than the imaginary statesmen who fail not to keep stirring, even if their labors are little more than worthless. To talk of the purchase of St. Thomas, when by a bold stroke of diplomacy, backed with a small amount of money and a gunboat or two, to sustain Dominican authorities, by defending the new acquisition, is suggestive of disordered reasoning. All the negotiations hitherto had upon the Samana question have failed, because our government has never come out with a boldly defined proposition and with the material to back it. But the trifling course of our government has had at least the appearance in case of emergency of a contemplated violation of faith. In such action a revolution here would be inevitable, for the failure would be thrown before the population as a sufficient ground of complaint. In a successful and sincere consummation of the transfer, in fee, for a small consideration, one-third the amount it was proposed to throw away on St. Thomas, it is unquestionable in my mind—and my conclusions are based upon free conversations with leading men of all parties—that there would be a unanimous expression of approbation on the part of that portion of the population desiring peace. The government would feel stronger and the island would progress. The revolutionists would of a certainty dissent, but this would prove an insignificant element if their outrageous and continued disturbance of the peace and prosperity of the island were to be met with summary and righteous punishment. It is also fair to suppose that even many of those who have no other occupation than to assail every effort to restore order on the island would be willing to keep quiet and avail themselves of an opportunity to enjoy the benefits of a security of person, liberty and property.

The acquisition of the bay and peninsula of Samana is now in a fairer way of consummation than at any previous moment. The government needs aid, and our own government will be never able better to grant it. Parties have appeared and are now in the United States who claim to be the agents of this government. In a conversation with M. Baez several days since I was informed by the President that he had given no powers to any one, neither secret nor public. It will, therefore, be understood by President Grant and Secretary Fish that all propositions coming from any source at present, according to M. Baez’s own statement, either upon the question of annexation or the acquisition of Samana are made upon individual responsibility. Baez has declared terms to no one. If anything is to be done on the Samana question it must come from the United States. Were Baez to propose to our government the sale of Samana that gentleman’s enemies would find plenty of food for their clamorous throats.

Should our government take hold of the question in the right shape I am at least able to say that our own terms would be accepted. I have not the least doubt, were our government to issue orders to the authorities at League Island to fit out two of the smaller monitors lying idle there, with say $500,000 on board as a first instalment, and send one of those vessels to the republican capital on the Ozama and the other to the secure anchorage by the coral keys of Levantado, in sixty days the first American flag unfurled in the Antilles would wave from the eastern peaks of the Cibao.

GEOGRAPHY AND STRATEGY.

A reference to a general map of the chain of islands separating the Gulf of Mexico from the Caribbean Sea will at once suggest the importance of the geographical position of the Island of St. Domingo in the Antilles with relation to its value as a strategic point, either in naval or commercial operations. It stands between the two principal Spanish possessions in America—Cuba and Porto Rico—and controls the English Island of Jamaica and the Windward Islands to the eastward. Samaná Bay and Peninsula, as an easily accessible and commodious station on the Island of St. Domingo, is certainly the central point at which all the advantages of a position in the tropical regions are to be realized. Its contiguity to the United States and other points of significance may be judged by the following table of distances:—

Miles.

From Samaná to New York.... 1,300

From Samana to Porto Rico (western end).... 80

From Samana to Turk’s Island.... 165

From Samana to Puerto Plata, St. Domingo.... 110

From Samana to Island of Saona.... 100

From Samaná to St. Domingo city.... 240

From Samana to the Spanish Main.... 600

From Samaná to Aspinwall.... 1,000

From Samaná to Key West, Fla..... 800

From northwest end of St. Domingo Island to Cuba.... 60

It will be seen that steamers stationed in the bay would have convenient access to all the surrounding islands and coasts, and for purposes of trade or as a coaling station the position is admirable.

The coal mines of Samana are as yet undeveloped, and therefore nothing can be said of their probable utility. With such flattering indications it is still encouraging that this valuable and indispensable material in modern navigation will be found in quantity and quality to answer every purpose. I have already alluded to the unexampled fertility of the adjacent country, its excellent building material of limestone and sandstone and valuable and inexhaustible forests of wood for cabinet and domestic uses and shipbuilding, its streams of pure water, its vegetable productions, its animal life. These natural resources would certainly attract and give subsistence to a teeming population and lead to a community of wealth, enterprise and prosperity unequalled in the tropics, a sort of outpost of the great republic of the North.

The territory and bay are entirely unencumbered. No concessions of land have been granted to any foreigners. President Baez has also expressed his resolution to listen to no persuasion to mar the title. Whatever efforts may have been made with a view to speculation they have thus far signally failed. If the property comes into the possession of the American government now, at least it will do so clear of obstructions and imposition. From the activity and anxiety of parties now in the United States it would appear that an effort is being made to control the Samaná business as a speculation. I would suggest to Mr. Fish to keep a sharp look out upon those who are annoying him, and if he has any negotiations to beware of outsiders.

Nature has also performed wonders for the defence of the position. The entrance by nature is already well defended and by the assistances of the appliances of human ingenuity could be rendered impregnable.

The mountain heights and dark passes intervening are sufficiently defensible and with the assistance of covering batteries, suggested by General McClellan, and perhaps on Azucar de Pilou and La Montana del Diablo could be easily defended against land attack.

The destiny of all the greater islands, particulary of the Antilles, is American. The centre of republicanism in the United States already imparts its influence to the adjacent islands and continent. The influence of American institutions, at least, in the Western hemisphere daily becomes more deeply felt, and as time moves on we observe its mysterious movements more openly exhibited. As the model of republican greatness, the small powers of America look to the United States for their lessons which shall direct and govern their development of new forms of government, independent and on the principles of liberty. The responsibility of the United States is, therefore, great. There is a necessity for a policy distinct from that employed in relations with the concerns of the Old World, a policy, American and republican, adapted to the demands of people and territories united with us in a community of interest and an identity of destiny. All these considerations demand, as a preliminary step, the presence of the republican flag of the United States, and nowhere could it better be planted than in the bay of Samaná, which would at once become “the American Gibraltar of the Antilles.”

The new Secretary will here find a field worthy of his labor and perpetuate the name and fame of his official career, increasing with the expanding greatness and power of his country.
