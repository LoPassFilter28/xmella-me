Trujillo Born on Farm, Worked in Sugar Mill

By the Associated Press

Rafael Leonidas Trujillo propelled his portly, well-groomed image up from the ranks to make the Dominican Republic a private domain for himself and his family.

The 69-year-old dictator began his life on his father's small farm, became a hand in a sugar mill, then a telegrapher. He quit school as a teen-ager but continued to study on his own at night.

National turmoil during his youth, with government following government, faded in 1916 as United States Marines came to the Dominican Republic under a plan to pay the country's debts and prevent any chance of foreign intervention.

Rafael Trujillo was 25 then. He became a cadet, then a lieutenant and finally a major in the national constabulary set up by the Marines. When the Marines left after eight years, he was the head of the constabulary.

Elected President in 1930

His long and powerful sway began in 1930. He was elected President that year after he had been promoted to brigadier general and organized the army.

Soon after inauguration Gen. Trujillo was given extraordinary powers to cope with a hurricane that took 2,000 lives and devastated Santo Domingo (the capital he renamed Ciudad Trujillo in 1936). He never surrendered that power and in every election thereafter either kept himself in office or put his younger brother, Gen. Hector B. Trujillo, in as a figurehead.

Rafael Trujillo kept at his littered desk seven days a week, working in shirt sleeves and piling up personal wealth in the multimillions for himself, his family and his associates. The Trujillo clique dominated—and profited from—virtually every phase of the country's economic life.

Gen. Trujillo insisted he had to be a strongman if the nation was to be lifted out of poverty. The Dominican Republic did make great economic strides. The national income rose from $7 million to $500 million, the peso stood at par with the dollar and the country stayed clear of foreign and internal debts.

Foe of Communism

Gen. Trujillo was a strong foe of communism for years. He also made enemies among his Latin American neighbors, with Fidel Castro of Cuba being among the latest. And his iron rule invited both internal and external plots and threats.

His repression of the slightest internal criticism and his endless arrests finally made the Roman Catholic Church in his largely Catholic country an enemy, too.

A plot last year resulted in the arrest of thousands and stories of harsh police brutality. Most were released, but at the same time Venezuelan President Romulo Betancourt was accusing Gen. Trujillo of masterminding a plot that almost killed him. The Organization of American States called on its members for political and economic sanctions against the dictator, and Washington broke off diplomatic relations, along with most Latin-American states.

"The man who takes a pot shot at me had better be sure of his aim," Trujillo once boasted. "Otherwise he'll be a dead man."

Gave Stern Warning

More recently, as active exile groups in Cuba, Puerto Rico, New York, Venezuela and Colombia talked of invasion, Gen. Trujillo said menacingly: "Brains and beards will fly like butterflies."

Yet he kept a food taster and had dinner guests eat first at his seashore mansion outside Ciudad Trujillo. He carried a rifle whenever he went without carloads of bodyguards and was a crack shot.

Gen. Trujillo's two marriages produced two sons and two daughters. The younger son, Rhadames, 19, attended Kemper Military School in Boonville, Mo. He left it for health reasons at 16.

One daughter, Flor de Oro, was among the many wives of Dominican playboy-diplomat Porfirio Rubirosa. Mr. Rubirosa is now his country's ambassador to France and his fifth wife is a French actress.

Gen. Rafael Trujillo, jr., 32, always his father's favorite, demonstrated a taste for high living while a student at the United States Army Command and General Staff College at Fort Leavenworth, Kans., in the late 1950s. He went back home without finishing his military course and his father raised a fuss over all the publicity.
