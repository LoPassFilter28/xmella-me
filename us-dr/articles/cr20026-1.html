With the Daily News Through The Years By John Hennessy

Continuing the movement for good housing, The Editorial of Feb. 6, 1940, added, “This lack of good housing facilities is a challenge for education, business and government. It is a dare which local leadership has never taken up and seems unable to comprehend. Although the Federal government has shown the way, private capital ignores the opening and remains frozen, inactive and hidden.

“A huge drive is being made in the United States through various agencies to make home-owning easy for those who really want to invest in real estate for personal use. Capital for building on a commercial scale is easily available, but it moves slowly. Here, we do not notice any considerable motion in building or remodeling, except in a few cases.

“Undoubtedly, the businessman who can visualize and replan French Village, Savan Polyberg, and a few other areas which need properly landscaped small family units of low rental, will reap a harvest of profit through the years. In the region occupied by the French colonists who have shown remarkable grasp of the housing situation, there is a special problem. Here is a field for education, landscaping, expansion, short and long-term loans, rental-selling plans and other modern financing. The wealthy men of that group might be able to handle the problem if they can be shown the solution.

“As soon as people in the Virgin Islands actually fall into step with the nation and invest in rebuilding the country, unemployment will decrease and capital will circulate. Business is bound to be much better and rest upon a sound foundation for a generation, at least.”

* * *

An interesting visitor to St. Thomas in February, 1940, was Generalissimo Rafael Leonidas Trujillo M., of the Dominican Republic, who arrived on his yacht “Ramfi,” and exchanged calls with the active governor.

In a comment on the modern student in secondary schools, the Observation Tower noted on Feb. 28, 1940, “Many parents and teachers are deeply concerned over the lack of interest shown by most students of today in cultural activities. They cannot understand, for example, why no literary club undertakes special work, such as studying modern poetry or reading Dickens. They are almost grieved that the young people have no enthusiasm for music groups that may plan and give recitals. Same cannot account for the lack of appreciation even of the essentials of schooling.

“Big girls who should be taking care of their clothing and learning to live decently in other ways, are slovenly, noisy, more interested in older boys than in grammar. The boys would rather play ball and loaf about garages or the car stand than study anything. On the whole, there is a feeling that this generation is going to the dogs.

“We are not pessimistic, but we do agree with some of the complaints. There is less interest in the things that used to inspire older folk. One reason for this is that there are many more diversions away from the home today than there were 25 years ago. Another reason is that oldsters are just as guilty of a slump in culture as are the children. After all, talking about lack of interest in anything is not half so effective as showing the way by doing.

“Students fail in school for the reason that parents do not concern themselves over the causes for the failures. Lack of interest or failures are caused by the present day indifference parents show for over-stimulating in play, late hours, bad companions, reading trash, attendance at grown-up parties, free spending of pocket-money, and poor checking of results.

“When these things are attended to, interest in cultural activities will follow. Children need guidance.”

* * *

On Feb. 29, 1940, an Editorial welcomed . . . “Bandmaster Alton A. Adams, U.S. Naval Fleet reserve, who has taken over and now publishes the Bulletin, which is the oldest daily newspaper in St. Thomas.

“During the days when the Navy administered the Virgin Islands Bandmaster Adams edited the St. Thomas Times, a weekly paper of fine appearance and good material. His experiences as a writer are too well known to need listing.

“The Bulletin, we feel sure, will not fall below its standard of conservative, yet general, treatment of all questions. We wish the venture success.”

* * *

Under a headline of Mar. 5, 1940, The Daily News wrote, “U.S. Threatened the Danes in 1915 . . . Used big stick to get the Virgin Islands.” In the AP article from Washington the “story of how America bought the 50 small islands which President Wilson considered of strategic value to the defence of the Panama Canal, was told by the publication of letters of Robert Lansing, wartime Secretary of State.” The Danes agreed to the transfer when Lansing said that he could conceive of circumstances under which the United States would seize the islands, the publication disclosed.

* * *

An Observation Tower of Mar. 5 commented sagely on foreign affairs . . . “The Government of the United States is older than the present form of any government in the great states of Europe. This is perhaps no great virtue, except that it represents the triumph of democracy.

“Today America, the United States in particular, is entering Europe’s competition for markets by seeking to arrange an honorable peace. This effort is commendable, but it reminds the historian of the same occurrences during the Wilson administration prior to the entry of the United States into the first world war. Every time a neutral makes an attempt to get the two belligerent camps, both of them suspect every motive and hamper every effort, while giving diplomatic sanction to the envoys.

“It must never be forgotten that this war is being fought because of greed and avarice. Jealousy and desire for markets underly all the high sounding principles. The mass murders of hundreds of thousands is the outcome of unbridled covetousness of the individual and nation. Even the United States has profited by the inability of the belligerents to maintain their gains in South America. This is an irritant which may cause such bad feeling that the United States may have to fight.

“The oldest government in the world did not grow in peace, even though it has progressed with less bloodshed (except the Civil War) than most others. War seems to be a national tonic, but peace is sustenance.”
