FROM SANTO DOMINGO.

Positive Information on the Progress of the Insurrection—The Destruction of Puerto Plata.

By the arrival on the 31st of October of the Bremen brigantine Santiago from Puerto Plata, we have received at last positive information of the political events in St. Domingo up to the 6th of the same month. The news reached us through the kind intermediation of various refugees, foreign merchants of said place.

After the destruction by fire of the inland and principal town of Santiago de les Caballeros, the Spanish garrison stationed there, together with reënforcements of about two thousand Spanish troops, began their retreat to the seaport of Puerto Plata on the 13th of September, joined by a great part of the foreign and native residents.

Shortly after their departure the insurgents began to harrass their march by continuous guerrilla fire, keeping the same up until their final arrival, on the 16th, causing them thereby a severe loss, estimated at from one thousand to one thousand two hundred killed and wounded, and some pieces of field artillery and a considerable amount of cash belonging to the regiment. Fresh arrivals of troops from Cuba and Puerto Rico reënforced the garrison in Puerto Plata up to four thousand men; nevertheless the insurgents succeeded in compelling the Spaniards to keep within the narrow limits of the town, and although they were not more in number than one thousand, not the slightest effort of repulsing the Dominicans was made by the Spanish garrison, and even small foraging bodies, dispatched to obtain food and grass, were driven back with considerable loss of life.

Intrenchments having been erected at the outskirts of the town, the Spaniards limited themselves to defend the same against the small assailing pickets who occasionally threatened to invade the town. This state of affairs continued up to the 2d of October, when preparations were made to evacuate the town, in order to retire to the fortifications dominating the port and town, which plan was executed on the dawn of the following day, by abandoning the intrenchments and retiring with the whole of the Spanish forces to the fort. Great dismay prevailed among the population in witnessing this unexpected and shameful event—the greater part of the same refuging on board the foreign vessels anchored in the port and in the fort, where, however, admittance was refused so much so that the distressed inhabitants were obliged to quarter themselves on the sea shore before the intrenchments erected there for the better protection of the fort.

The reason given for the evacuation of the town is that orders had been received to send the greater part of the troops to the City of San Domingo, where a new basis of operations is to be commenced, and not being able to defend all the barricades with the force remaining. It is, however, generally believed that the Spaniards evacuated the town either because of their inability to defend it, or because they wished to plunder it. The latter seems to have been their motive, as on the same day Spanish troops and Chinese laborers attached to the regiments, who were sent from the fort into the town without their officers engaged until sunset in pillaging the stores and houses. None of the Spanish officers attempted to stop their men from plundering. In the afternoon of the same day about four battallions of the Spanish troops embarked in the Spanish war steamers Herman Cortez, Velasco and Isabella la Catolico, their probable destination being the City of St. Domingo, reducing the garrison to about 1,500 men.

On the morning of the 4th of October the Spaniards began to pillage the town again. The Dominicans, finding that the Spaniards were bent on sacking the whole town, began to plunder also, and, while both parties were pillaging, there was some hot skirmishing going on, the Spaniards wishing to rival the Dominicans in securing the booty. In the afternoon every store and house, save one or two stores on the sea-beach, were emptied of their contents, and whatever objects the Spaniards could not remove they wantonly destroyed. The remaining garrison is under the command of Brigadier Rafael Prime de Rivera. About 4 o'clock p. m. a sally was made from the fort by 600 Spaniards against the insurgents, the number of whom was not known. A severe fight ensued, and the Dominicans were driven out of the town.

The Spaniards were protected by their guns on the fort, throwing shells to the center of the town, and about 5 o'clock p. m. a fire broke out in town, in the vicinity of the market, in the direction where the shells had fallen, it being the general belief that the fire was occasioned by the missiles thrown from the fort into the town. A weak attempt to check the fire was made by a company of sappers, which proved unsuccessful. The fire soon spread with great rapidity, and continued up to the moment the Bremen Bergantin Santiago left the harbor, being the 6th of October, in the evening, destroying among others the valuable stores of the considerable body of foreign merchants residing there.

In fact the whole town, with the exception of the Wesleyan Mission-House and a few small buildings in the eastern suburbs, has been destroyed completely by fire, and it is fully believed that the few remaining buildings will be destroyed equally. The losses on property, &c., from the firing of Santiago and Porto Plata exceed the sum of $5,000,000. The whole population of Porto Plata has fled to foreign countries, and thousands of foreigners, as well as natives, have sought refuge on the islands of St. Thomas, Curacoa, and Turks Island, and some of the principal merchants on the hospitable shores of the United States of North America. No notification whatever has been give to the representative of foreign Powers, and consequently the whole consular body consisting of the United States commercial agent, the French and British vice consuls, the consuls for Austria, Prussia, Hanoveria, Oldenburg, Denmark, and the free Hanseatic towns of Hamburg and Bremen, finding no protection either for their persons, residences or their nationality, have been forcibly compelled to leave the country and seek refuge on foreign shores, leaving behind their properties, wantonly destroyed, and saving nothing but their lives. It is to be hoped that ample and energetic measures will be taken by their respective governments to claim from Spain the just indemnification they are entitled to for the heavy losses sustained.

On the 21st the British war steamer Barracouta called at the port of Puerto Plata, but finding no necessity to afford protection to British and foreign property, viewing the strength of the Spanish soldiery sufficient for cases of emergency, left the harbor on the next day for Jamaica.
