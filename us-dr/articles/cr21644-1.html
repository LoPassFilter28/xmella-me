Our Navy in the West Indies

One of the objects for which the United States maintains a costly naval establishment in time of peace is presumably to protect American commercial interests in foreign countries. We have received intelligence from the West Indies which indicates that the Administration at Washington has lost sight of this object in one important instance at least. On the northern shore of the Island of Hayti or San Domingo is the town of Puerto Plata, one of the chief seaports of the Dominican Republic. It has a population of about 3,000 inhabitants, and carries on a considerable trade with the United States. Formerly our Government was represented there by a Consular agent, but more recently the trade with the United States has been regarded as important enough to justify the establishment of a full Consulate, and a son of Frederick Douglass was appointed the first American Consul.

This action certainly indicates that there are American interests of some consequence in Puerto Plata. The commercial and political importance of the town has made it a prominent point of attack and defence during the revolutionary contest, which has now prevailed in San Domingo for many months. For sixty days preceding the date of our latest despatches, the conflicts in the streets and the outlying districts had been almost incessant, and scarcely a day passed without more or less musketry firing among the warehouses and dwellings of the foreign merchants. The families of many of them had been compelled to take refuge on the shipping in the harbor. It would seem that at such a place as this, if anywhere, the presence, or occasional visits, of a man-of-war would have some effect to secure the lives and property of alien residents against needless peril and destruction; yet we are informed that no vessel of the American navy has made her appearance at Puerto Plata since the last outbreak began, now more than three months ago.

Under these circumstances, even Mr. Douglass, the Consul, has found the country too uncomfortable to remain in it any longer, and has come away, leaving behind him as Vice-Consul a native of San Domingo, who is a relative of the Minister of War under President Baez. It is anything but pleasing to the American residents of Puerto Plata to find themselves represented by a man who must needs be influenced in his acts by his adherence to one of the two parties now at war for the possession of San Domingo; and the fear is expressed that even if an American man-of-war should come into the harbor, this Vice-Consul would try to convince the commander that she was not needed.

It was the fault of the last Administration that it paid rather too much attention to the affairs of San Domingo; but at present the State Department and the Navy Department might properly and profitably devote some consideration to the matters we have mentioned.
