A RULER WHO MAINTAINS AN ABSOLUTE DESPOTISM BY VIRTUE OF THE DEADLY ACCURACY OF HIS "GUN-PLAYS"

Ramon Caceres, the cowboy president of a West Indian Republic, governs his country in the full possession of "that peace which the mere world cannot give, and which fades not before the clear eye of the accurate marksman." His word is law, but he rules with a laugh—the laugh which did not desert him when he shot down in his tracks the modern Nero who ruled before him, and which accompanies every order he gives, be it for a cup of after dinner coffee or the shooting of a man before breakfast. He rides like a centaur, shoots birds off the ends of orange sprigs for diversion, and men as a necessity.

By THOMAS R. DAWLEY, JR.

RAMON CACERES is a big man, fully six feet in height, and weighs considerably over 250 pounds. His word is law, but he rules with a laugh, whether it be to order a man shot before breakfast or to take his accustomed cup of coffee after a hearty meal. It is always a laugh. When besieged in the citadel above Santiago City, he looked around at his twenty generals and laughed. "Gentlemen," said he, "if you do not wish to remain here you will have to follow me," and, vaulting into his saddle, he ordered the gates thrown open, and with a wild yell charged down into the city, driving the enemy pell mell out of it. Then he looked around at his twenty generals who had followed, yelling too, and laughed. I do not believe this man ever appeared to take anything seriously, not even when he shot down in his tracks his predecessor, Ulysses Heureaux.

That is the impression I brought away with me of the present Dictator-President of that little oligarchy called the Dominican Republic. I first met him in the midst of a revolution. A division of his army had just been defeated and put to rout, his minister of war killed, and it was expected that the enemy would follow up their victory with a general assault on the city, yet Don Ramon Caceres laughed. He read the letter which I presented to him with a laugh, and bid me make myself at home with another laugh. I saw him attend the funeral of the dead minister of war, whom they buried under the floor of the church by the side of the President whom he had shot to death a few years before, and scarcely was the funeral over than he was laughing again.

I never could ascertain whether anyone feared Gen. Ramon Caceres [illegible] dent or general, or even by his last name, Senor Caceres. He is known throughout the land which he rules in his careless way as "Mon," the name which his mother and sisters and cousins knew him by when a child. No other name or title has ever taken its place, and I doubt if it would occur to the most abject prisoner pleading for his own life to call him anything but "Mon." As for the prisoner, he is not abject. The term is misleading, for I do not believe the most humbled Dominican ever becomes servile. Even when he begs he holds his head up and asks for what he wants with an air of you owing it to him. When President Morales ordered his two prisoners, General Mansuetta and Commander Gillheaux, shot the next morning at sunrise, Gillheaux called out to him as he was passing his prison door, "Say, Morales, you ought to let us off in celebration of your inauguration tomorrow." There was not the slightest tremor in his voice, and Morales replied with equal coolness, "I can't; I have already signed your death warrants." And I saw these two prisoners marched out at sunrise to the cemetery gate. They walked with a firm step, and reaching the gate they wheeled about, facing the firing squad, saluted, and the next moment their souls were sailing into eternity.

Ramon Caceres with his followers put Morales in the Presidential chair because he did not want the job himself. Morales was ambitious. He wanted to rule, but he found Caceres with his ubiquitous laugh ever at his elbow. He would have killed him if he dared, but he did not dare. Then the story went out that he intended to poison him, and when it reached Caceres he only laughed, but he watched Morales all the closer. Then Morales wanted to resign, but Caceres, with another laugh, reminded him that the Presidential job was of his own seeking and he must hold it down until his term of office expired. "But I have no power," mused Morales, "I am virtually a prisoner in my own capital," and so he negotiated with the revolutionists on the outside and precipitated a revolution against his own government. He had counted on the United States gunboats through Minister Dawson to aid him, but he had not reckoned on the quick eye, the intuitive brain of the man who always laughed, carried a gun, and could knock chippy birds with it off the branches of the orange [illegible]
