NEW DEAL ACCORDED HAITI HAILED BY LATIN AMERICA Resentment Over Past Military Policy of United States Fades on Advent of New Course.

BY GASTON NERVAL.

THROUGHOUT Latin America the "executive agreement" recently concluded between the Governments of the United States and Haiti has received enthusiastic approval. To understand this enthusiasm, although the agreement is only the first step to end American intervention in the island republic and does not correct all of the objectionable practices allied with intervention, one must bear in mind the high lights of Haitian-American relations in the past quarter of a century, and compare the methods then prevailing with the liberal and considerate treatment involved in the new agreement.

Despite the fact internal political disturbances had always been more or less chronic in Haiti, it was only after the establishment of United States financial interests in the country that they aroused concern in Washington. Soon after the appearance of the National City Bank in Haitian finances, in 1910, the State Department began to show anxiety for the instability of the Haitian authorities and to devise means to insure the safety of American investments.

Control Sought.

It was, precisely, at that time that the ultimate transformation of the Monroe Doctrine had taken place. The Roosevelt corrollary had definitely converted the old declaration, in the eyes of public opinion as well as in the understanding of many statesmen unfamiliar with its original meaning, into a policy which precluded any kind of European interference and which intrusted the United States with the protection of European lives and property in revolution-torn Latin American countries. The policy of the "big stick" had already been announced by Theodore Roosevelt and realistic obligations of "dollar diplomacy" had been avowedly praised by Taft and Knox.

Following the Dominican precedent, the State Department decided that the best way to bring about domestic tranquility, protect American interests and avoid possible European complications, was to secure control of the Haitian customs house, main source of the national revenue, and supervise the government finances. Suggestions to this effect became quite insistent after the outbreak of the World War.

After six of these overtures had failed, while one revolutionary regime succeeded another, the State Department advanced the peculiar doctrine that the United States, in view of "its tacitly conceded obligations as the nearest friend of Haiti in her relations with the rest of the world"—an obvious reference to the Monroe Doctrine—"would feel at liberty thereafter to insist that revolutionary movements cease and that all subsequent changes in the government of the republic be effected by the peaceful processes provided in the Haitian constitution."

Pressure Attempted.

When President Theodore was quietly elected in Haiti, the Secretary of State offered to extend him recognition in exchange for a convention conceding customs control, settlement of all outstanding questions between the government, the bank and the American railroad, and an agreement never to lease to any European power any portion of Haitian territory for naval or coaling purposes. This having proved of no avail, a method of pressure which had already been used in the neighboring Dominican republic, was attempted. The bank, controlled by the National City Bank, withheld all government funds, upon the advice of the American Minister that this course might persuade the Haitian authorities to turn over the customs houses.

Such expectation did not come true, and, several days later, fearing that the government might try forcibly to take its funds, the bank decided to transfer them to a safer haven, its home office in New York. For this purpose, a contingent of United States Marines landed in the Haitian capital, "proceeded to the vaults of the National Bank of Haiti and in broad daylight forcibly seized $500,000 and carried it aboard the gunboat Machias. The money was transported to New York and deposited in the vaults of the National City Bank."

Landing Force Ordered.

Soon afterward the disorder following the overthrow of President Guillaume Sam offered an opportunity for more drastic action. When the deposed Chief Executive was dragged out of the French legation, at Port au Prince, and murdered by a mob infuriated by his massacre of political prisoners, Admiral Capperton of the United States Navy received instructions from Washington to land forces "at the request of the State Department."

Admiral Capperton complied magnificently. He occupied the Haitian capital and reported that the situation was controlled by a committee of prominent citizens acting "practically" under his directions. He "persuaded" the Chamber of Deputies to postpone presidential elections, ordered the treasury service restored to the National Bank and suggested the sending of a regiment of Marines, in addition to that already landed, to suppress native rebel bands, "if the United States desires to negotiate treaty for financial control of Haiti." The admiral knew the plans of the State Department and, in his frank military language, did not attempt to conceal them. But he advocated the use of bayonets to obtain that which the diplomatic endeavors of the American charge d'affaires or the financial pressure of the National City Bank had been unable to accomplish.

U. S. Friend Elected.

Even for this, however, it was necessary to set up a local government favorable to the United States with which to negotiate. With the open and avowed support of the American forces of occupation, M. Dartiguenave, who had promised to support "any terms proposed by the United States," was elected President, while United States Marines stood at the doors of the legislative chamber and Admiral Capperton's chief of staff circulated among the Congressmen.

Immediately afterward the State Department notified the new President that it expected the signing of the proposed treaty "without modification." When the Haitian secretary of foreign affairs contended that the new government might have to resign rather than accept such imposition, the Secretary of State replied that if the treaty were not accepted "the United States would have either to set up a military government * * * or permit the control of the government to pass to some other political faction which would fall in with the American plans."

Customs Houses Taken.

At the same time Admiral Capperton was ordered to strengthen his hold on the country. In less than two weeks his forces took over the customs houses at the 10 leading ports of Haiti, the government being thus deprived of its chief source of income. Finally, after martial law had been formally declared by Admiral Capperton, he reported: "Treaty situation at present looks more favorable than usual. This has been effected by exercising military pressure at propitious moments."

Presently the treaty was accepted and signed by the government, but further pressure, military as well as financial, was required before the Haitian Senate consented to ratify it. It only did so when Admiral Capperton personally and dramatically declared before the cabinet that the United States had the intention to retain control in Haiti until ratification had been obtained.

Provisions of Treaty.

What were the provisions of this treaty for which the United States spared neither diplomatic nor military pressure? Here are the most important: (a) Appointment, upon the nomination by the President of the United States, of a general receiver to collect, receive and apply all customs duties, and of a financial adviser to be attached to the ministry of finance; (b) prohibition to increase the public debt without permission from the President of the United States; (c) prohibition to surrender any portion of the national territory by sale, lease or otherwise, or jurisdiction over such territory, to any foreign government; (d) creation of a native constabulary officered by Americans; (e) authorization for the United States to aid in the preservation of Haitian independence and the maintenance of a government adequate for the protection of life, property and individual liberty.

Under this semi-protectorate, the government of Haiti in the years to follow became a unique four-headed aggregation of powers: The military government, the treaty officials, the native government and the Minister of the United States

In 1918 the Haitian authorities agreed to sumbit all proposed legislation to the United States legation for approval. The year before the treaty had been extended to 1936. In 1920, upon disagreements on financial matters between the American adviser and the Haitian government, the former employed, again, the old recourse of withholding the salaries of the President and his cabinet and the principal executive officers. Several times popular opposition to the American occupation was quelled by the use of military force.

"New Deal" Arrives.

Until the "executive agreement," signed recently—justly considered the first tangible expression of the "new deal" in inter-American relations—the condition of Haiti had improved little from the restrictions upon national sovereignty involved in the treaty which the United States Navy secured for the State Department under President Dartiguenave.

As to the merits or disadvantages of American control of the island republic, this is not the place to discuss them. The material benefits must be paired against the humiliation inherent in lack of political self-determination, the complete subservience to American financial interests—which, until not long ago, had the active co-operation of the State Department in strengthening their ever-expanding net—the curtailment of individual liberty and the abuses unavoidable in military rule by foreigners.

The recent agreement tends to terminate these things. This, alone, even if it were not sufficient to heal completely the wound and remove resentment for past mistakes, is enough to win the praise of governments and public opinion on the other side of the Rio Grande. Latin Americans can better appreciate the change because they have not forgotten the methods of the past.
