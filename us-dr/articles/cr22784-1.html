Carib Indians Withstood Hurricanes Only To Be Wiped Out By Spaniards

By LESLIE HIGHLEY

WASHINGTON, Sept. 17.—(UP)—The Carib Indians, who inhabited the Caribbean area when Columbus discovered the new world, called the kind of tropical storm that hit Florida the "huracan" and it was their God of evil, satan.

The sun and the moon were their Gods of goodness.

The Caribbean hurricane mostly originates in the Lesser Antilles or that chain of islands which extends from the southern tip of the Florida Peninsula to the northern coast of South America—an area which was inhabited by the Caribs and the Araucas, two Indian tribes.

The Aborigines of these islands knew the huracan very well. They feared nothing worse than the fury of their God of evil. When the sun or the moon disappeared behind black, fast-sailing storm clouds, the Caribs knew the devil was upon them.

Indians Wiped Out

The Caribs and the Araucas were wiped out of the islands by the Spaniards, the British and the Dutch. But the present inhabitants fear a hurricane as much as the Indians did.

The huracan, with its destructive winds and flooding water, has had a great influence on the way of life of people who experienced its fury. The Indian, for instance, built his home and planted crops so that the hurricane would not destroy them, usually near natural windbreaks.

Today, the country man starts building a home with the storm house, which goes down into the earth before the home goes into the air.

The storm house is a hole in the ground, about 10 feet square, with little projecting above the ground level. During the hurricane season food and furnishings are placed inside. A storm might last several days.

The city man's home is usually made of concrete and reinforced with steel bars. This construction is in itself a storm house above the ground.

But even steel and concrete some times prove too weak before the winds of more than 100 miles per hour.

I have lived through three of these dangerous hurricanes in Puerto Rico—these of 1926, 1928, when the wind velocity reached 160 miles, and the one of 1932 which hit the capital of the Dominican Republic—then called Santo Domingo—and took 10,000 lives.

The storm of 1928 crossed Puerto Rico from the southeast to the northwest and leveled off everything in its path. Only the concrete structures were left.

I saw complete houses lifted into the air and exploded into a million pieces to be scattered over several square miles. Galvanized zinc, from roofs, floated in the air like pieces of paper.
