The New York Herald · March 4, 18584 de marzo de 1858

Original in English.Original en inglés.

Interesting from Santo Domingo.

THE BAEZ PARTY REDUCED TO THE LAST STRAITS—POSITION OF HIS FORCES AT SAN DOMINGO CITY AND SAMANA—WANT OF PROVISIONS—GENERAL PALMADTIER AT POINT OF DEATH—NAVAL NEWS—GERMAN IMMIGRANTS DYING OF HUNGER, ETC.

By the schooner Brontes, from Puerto Platto, we have received files of the Gaceta Oficial of the Dominican republic to the 31st of January, and a letter from our correspondent at St. Jago de los Caballeros, the provisional seat of government.

OUR DOMINICAN CORRESPONDENCE.

ST. JAGO, Jan 19, 1858.

Return of our Exiled Correspondent—Position of the Contending Parties—Formation of the New Constitution—Hopes of Recognition by Foreign Governments—War Vessels Fitting Out—Trade, &c., &c.

It is a long time since I had the pleasure to address you, but it has been owing to the political affairs of this country; in consequence of which I have been an exile, travelling in Curracoa and Turks' Islands. But now that I am settled again, you will receive my letters as regularly as before.

Enclosed you will find a file of the Official Gazette, by which you will perceive that Baez is confined to the old city of San Domingo, and that the gallant and good patriot, Santana, is at the head of a large army, besieging said city, where foreign provisions are very scarce, and none at all of the domestic. Besides, Baez is in a very hopeless position, having but very few soldiers, and these deserting almost daily, three by three and five by five, and sometimes in larger numbers.

The representatives appointed to revise the constitution by the different conventions of all the counties of the republic, met on the 7th of December last, at Moca, a small village twelve miles from this city, in order to make a new constitution, which is already drawn up, and now in the printing office. The debates will begin in a few days, and there is every reason to expect that the new political basis of this unfortunate country will be very liberal, the penalty of death for political faults being forever abolished, religious tolerance being established, harmonizing the democratic principles with the strength of the executive power, and granting several advantages to foreigners; not only to those belonging to friendly nations, but to the citizens and subjects of the countries at peace with the republic

We entertain the hope that as soon as the new authorities or powers of the State be regularly established, all the nations which have representatives in this country will recognise our new government, such a course being consonant with international practice, not forgetting that the will of the people, particularly in the republics, is the true sovereign; and that the whole population of this country having raised against Baez and his minions, has reason to expect from every civilized nation that their new rulers be recognized by them as the true and legitimate magistrates. We have now five good sailing vessels of our own fitting for war at Puerto Plata, and will shortly go to sea in order to capture the three small ones that Baez has on the waters of Samana.

Provisions of every description are very abundant here, and business goes straight and lively, the tobacco crop having been a very large one this year.

[Translated for the NEW YORK HERALD from La Gaceta Oficial of Santiago de los Caballeros, Jan. 17.]

The facts which the Generals commanding the besieging armies of St. Domingo and Samana, with all their details, have officially communicated to the government, as well as the letters written by private persons from the first of these two places, both to us and to other persons, fully confirm our prediction, that within a very short time, one or the other of these cities will be surrendered. Such is the inference naturally drawn from the relation given to General Bido on the 5th inst., by the Azuans, who at that date deserted from the old capital, and amongst whom, we see with satisfaction, was the valiant Captain Baldomero Victorino.

According to that account, Baez prohibited that the condition which Samana at present is in be spoken of at St. Domingo, the best proof that the brave Generals at the head of the army besieging that peninsula, cannot be taxed with exaggerating the reports they addressed to our government of the victories obtained by them over the troops of Palmantier. No other reason can be assigned for this extraordinary prohibition. In consequence of the official communications, in which the commander-in-chief of the rebels at Samana reports the repeated defeats our valiant troops caused to him, Baez came to the conviction that the surrender or capture by force of that place is inevitable. It is for this reason that he in advance imposed silence with regard to the war which Generals Puello and Mella are carrying on against Palmantier, in order that when victory crowns our troops nobody may learn of it or be astonished at the silence on that point, Baez having in advance put a curb into the mouth of the citizens and monopolized the bad news he is receiving from Samana.

But this is not the only reason which confirms us in our conviction relative to the speedy termination of the exceptional state of things wherein the country finds itself. As our readers will learn from the communication addressed by Gen. Bido, under date of the 7th inst., to the provisional government, the disaffection amongst the people of St. Domingo, and more particularly amongst the small force of troops Baez has under his command in that city, is going on increasing. When the Azuans left that city there were but eight head of cattle, a number not sufficient to provide the entire population of St. Domingo with meat, which is also wanted by the inhabitants of San Carlos and Pajarito, who, unfortunately for them, since the end of July, find themselves confined within the old capital, and the troops of the garrison, who, although small in number, must have their part of the provisions. The stock of hogs reported by the deserters of Baez will last for about two weeks; but will the poor be able to buy this meat at a cost of $65 the pound? This is impossible. Besides, the great majority of the population, and of the inhabitants of the two localities above mentioned, are composed of very poor people, who live partly by their labor (and to-day they did not find any) and others on their small farms which they at present are not allowed even to visit. As to the cocoa nuts, what kind of food can they get out of them? We do not see that they can be of any other use than to furnish them with sweetmeats or a certain quantity of oil.

To the above must be added those military persons who, as accomplices of Baez, are very much frightened at the idea that our flotilla might cut off their retreat. Poor cowards they are. Their conscience warns them that the day of expiation for their numberless villainies is close at hand, and that it must take place in a just proportion with the evils they inflicted on their country.

What is still worse, of the three war ships sent by Baez to Samana he has not had any news, although over two months have elapsed since the first two left St. Domingo and one month since the last of them sailed. Thus Baez has at present no ship left at his disposal, since those three were the only vessels he had. They are named El Libertador, La Australia and El Cirus, El 19 de Marzo having been delivered up to the provisional government by her commander, and La Merced having been sunk some two months and a half since by the well directed balls of the besieging army before Pajarito.

As regards Samana, the position in which the deluded soldiers who form the battalion under the command of the rebel Palmantier find themselves, is not any better. According to the report of the German immigrant alluded to by the commander of Puerto Plata in his despatch of the 12th inst. Palmantier was so seriously ill when the former left that place that he was not even able to speak; and there is no doubt but that the population of that peninsula find themselves in a very bad situation as far as provisions are concerned, a chicken being sold for five pesos, as is stated by the same individual.

This latter, with whose misfortunes we sincerely sympathize, adds that of his companions immigrants like himself, eight died in St. Domingo and six in Samana, one of sickness and the others by famine. The unfortunates! In September last, two or three of them had asked from General Santana permission to work in the fields agreeably to the contract on the stipulation of which they had left the shores of France; the libertador gave them the permission, and kept even for some days in the neighborhood of St. Domingo, a drove of beasts ready, with which he intended to send them to the provisional government, to be provided by the latter with land, and to be established thereon. Why have they not accepted the benevolent offer of General Santana? Nobody knows. It is much to be wondered at that the French Consul in St. Domingo, under whose protection those unfortunates had been in that city, did not take the necessary measures to prevent them from perishing by hunger.
