New York Journal and Advertiser · August 29, 189929 de agosto de 1899

Original in English.Original en inglés.

REVOLUTIONISTS SWEEPING THROUGH SANTO DOMINGO. Marching on the Capital, and There Are Reports of Its Fall. TROOPS ARE DESERTING. Jiminez, the Leader, on the Cuban Coast, but His Followers Fight Ahead. ARE TRYING TO PARLEY. Government, In Its Extremity, Sends Commissioner to the Rebels.

SANTO DOMINGO, Aug. 28.—San Francisco de Macoris, La Vega and Cotuy are in the power of the revolutionists. They are marching on this city.

The government has sent a commission, consisting of C. N. Moya, Leonte, Vasquez, Espaillat, Pena and Reynoso, to meet them and ask them what they want.

Nothing may be done before the commission returns. Not a single shot has been fired here. If the commission should not pacify the invaders with words its chief announces to-day that "It will take energetic steps, and serious trouble may follow."

Porta Plaza, Santiago, Mora and Monte Christi are said here to be quiet.

The Dominican war ships are all at this port. They may sail at any moment.

The United States cruiser New Orleans and the French cruiser Cecile are in port.

The revolutionists have not proclaimed any special candidate.

Telegraphic communications are generally interrupted.

Cape Haytien, Aug. 28.—General Pepin, returning from the defense of Santiago in the Dominican Republic, has been defeated by the revolutionists, and forced to retreat on Monte Christi.

Nao and a part of Guayacanes have declared in favor of the revolution. The revolutionists are massing in these towns. They have cut off communication with Monte Christi.

It is said that General Patino, Minister of the Dominican Government to the Cibao territory, failing in his mission, has joined the partisans of Jiminez, a leader of the revolution.

Puerto Plata, via Martinique, Aug. 28.—Santiago de los Caballeros has not surrendered to the revolutionists, but the townspeople are openly opposed to the government.

The government's forces, under General Juan Garrido, are entrenched on two hills about eight kilometres from the city. Many men are deserting. More than one hundred have gone to the enemy, carrying arms and supplies. Dr. Dionicio and a great number of persons, well equipped, have left the city to join the insurgents.

These are advancing rapidly to Monte Christi. They try to avoid battle. They promise immunity to all who will lay down their arms. They welcome recruits.

The crops are flourishing. The farmers are asking for peace at any price.

Santiago de Cuba, Aug. 28.—General Isidro Jiminez, revolutionary aspirant to the presidency of the Dominican Republic, arrived here with his two sons to-day by the South Coast boat. He was not permitted to land. This annoyed him exceedingly. He refused to grant a newspaper interview. He said that the press invariably misrepresented him.

Refugees from Santo Domingo tried to go on board the boat and salute him, but they were not permitted to do this. They are indignant.

Andriano Grullon, representative of the Santo Dominican revolutionists in New York, has received the following cable dispatch:

"Santiago de Cuba, Aug. 27.

"Revolution in capital. Government surrenders.

(Signed) LA MARCHE."

Mr. Grullon says this dispatch means that the government of President Figuero at Santo Domingo City has surrendered to the party of which General Jiminez is the head. La Marche represents the revolutionists in Santiago de Cuba. Mr. Grullon has received no other details.

Mr. Grullon was asked what the plan of the revolutionists was. He said: "So far as any plan has been considered it is safe to say that for the present some provisional government will be established until a general election is held. I know certainly that General Jiminez will not assume any office until he is chosen at an election by the people. He wants to be the popular choice. He does not want to ride into office by force or by any sudden coup. He will wait for the election and he will be triumphantly elected. The people want him. General Jiminez is not a professional revolutionist. He is a man of high ideals and wants to give to Santo Domingo a government free and civilized that may enable the people of his native land to prosper, as they certainly will do."

Charles W. Wells, vice-president of the Santo Domingo Improvement Company, denies that the city of Santo Domingo is in the power of the revolutionists. He said yesterday:

"We have 5,000 men employed in railroad construction in the northern part of the island of Santo Domingo. We have alert men at Moya, Monte Christi, Santiago and the capital itself.

"If the capital had fallen into the hands of the revolutionists we should know it. They would have telegraphed to us. In Santo Domingo is a New Yorker who has worked for us there for eight years. He telegraphed yesterday about new contracts, and said not a word of troubles at Santo Domingo.

"Jiminez's few followers are near Moya, where President Heureaux was assassinated, and at Santiago, fifteen miles from Moa. Others are at Monte Christi, in the northern part of the island.

"Santo Domingo is a seaport on the south coast. If trouble had occurred there the Government of the United States would know it. The report that comes from Santiago de Cuba cannot be true. Santo Domingo's army consists of about 4,000 well equipped men."

The break in the cable between Cape Haytien and Puerto Plata causes delay in the transmission of news. It was broken by the recent hurricane. Dispatches must come by two other routes, both very long. The first substitute is from New York to Halifax, from there to Bermuda, thence to Kingston, thence to St. Thomas, then to Martinique, then back to Puerto Plata, and from there overland to Santo Domingo City. The second way is from New York to Lisbon, thence to Para, Brazil, Martinique and Puerto Plata.

General Abelardo A. Moscoso said yesterday: "I open the Dominican Consul-General's mail in his absence. I see all his telegraphic dispatches. He has no news of the fall of Santo Domingo."
