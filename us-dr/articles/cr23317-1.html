IF THE O.A.S. IS TO RECOVER ITS LOST PRESTIGE

No. 7 of a series

El Caribe

EDITORA DEL CARIBE, C. por A.

J. A. OSORIO LIZARAZO, Director

CIUDAD TRUJILLO, DOMINICAN REPUBLIC

IF THE O.A.S. IS TO RECOVER ITS LOST PRESTIGE

Editorial from "El Caribe," July 19, 1960

WHEN only twelve years ago the Organization of American States came into being through the medium of the Bogota Charter, every country whose sole ambition it had been to unite on the basis of juridical equality, respect for each other and non-intervention, felt the satisfaction of having taken an enormous step forward in the bringing about of a state of solidarity based on national autonomy.

Everything seemed to be clear and explicit and the Charter contained provisions for possible emergencies, classifying forms of antagonism, conflicts and other difficulties that might arise and setting forth the respective solutions for these.

However, something fundamental was lacking: the perdurability of the beliefs of those who, at the moment, found themselves ruling these countries. Through the mandatory terms established under the system of non-continuity, the spirit of the Charter was weakened as soon as revolutionary regimes appeared which, inspired by typically Communist ideas, discarded all Covenants and destroyed agreements and all other forms of juridical structure.

No sooner had the O.A.S. been established than the luck of politics decided the rise to power in some countries of certain leaders whose object was to destroy peace, organizing an armed attack against one of the signatory States of the Charter, the Dominican Republic, a country which, being immensely loyal to her commitments, appealed to the clauses protecting her rights, though with practically negative results.

Once again, however, the O.A.S. seemed to recover its apparent power when these aggressor governments, having been overthrown and substituted by others having a higher concept of legality, there was again a promise of respect for the clauses agreed upon at Bogota, and for some time it seemed reasonable to suppose that the great ideal which gave the Charter its being had at long last been consolidated.

Once again, however, the coming to power of persons steeped in Communism, resentment, hatred and unwillingness to coexist, highlighted the weakness of Inter-American agreements and the general lack of moral cohesion. Once again too, the Dominican Republic became the target for external aggressions which threatened to destroy her peace and her right to free determination of her institutions, so explicitly consecrated in the Charter, and once again, the O.A.S. showed itself powerless to apply the established norms for ending such a state of affairs.

And what is worse, basing their actions on inane sophisms, shameful intrigues and hidden pressures, certain members of the organization sided with the aggressors and in an ostentation of cynicism, proclaimed the principle of non-intervention precisely for the purpose of intervening openly in the internal regime of this most persecuted of countries. And not because the juridical constitution of the organization permitted it, but because its statutes were deliberately interpreted mendaciously and in bad faith.

These circumstances, some of them provoked by the leadership of the Cuban revolution, have led these same men to doubt the efficacy of the O.A.S. in the conflict which has arisen between their own country and the United States, a conflict which could have been stopped a considerable time ago.

Cuba decided to sidestep the O.A.S. and appeal to the Security Council while she felt she had the backing of Khrushchev's guided missiles. Such a step has produced a great deal of alarm among the nations of the hemisphere as they feel the strength of their regional association threatened through the loss of its reputation brought about by its show of partiality and enmity towards one of its member states. This has carried the said organization to the brink of that situation of juridical and human distortion which is prejudgment.

This alarm, which could have been avoided if the members of the O.A.S. had always maintained the principles and doctrines of the institution, and not given way to the passions of the respective rulers who found themselves in power, has led to an attempt to restore to the O.A.S. its jurisdiction so that it may study the differences which have arisen between two of its members.

The formula proposed in the Security Council of the United Nations by two Latin American countries constitutes a noble effort towards recovering the O.A.S.'s lost moral authority. This formula suggests that the basis on which the Council may act must be a report from the O.A.S. and that the latter is bound to use whatever elements it has available to prepare such a report and must, therefore, enjoy the pre-eminence to which it is entitled. At the same time, the O.A.S. itself called for a new Conference of Foreign Ministers to prepare a plan which, by itself, if justly and impartially drawn up, might well constitute a solution to the problem.

There is no doubt that the authority of the O.A.S. has run a grave risk, a risk that could not be even partially mitigated while there still remained that condition of prejudgment and hostility which predominated in the question so slanderously and gratuitously presented by Venezuela.

Complete moral recovery can only stem from the absolute integrity with which the accusations made by Venezuela are judged and the gratuitous imputations adduced as proof, are analyzed. And when these are shown to be no more than a pack of lies, the whole charge must be thrown out and the absolute lack of guilt on the part of the country and government that have been so vilely slandered, must be proclaimed without reticence, sophism and evasion. Otherwise the O.A.S. will continue on its way to total disrepute.

THE DOMINICAN REPUBLIC PRESS SOCIETY

Ciudad Trujillo, Dominican Republic
