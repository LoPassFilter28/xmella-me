New Columbian Memorial

While the General Assembly of the United Nations was meeting in London, it gave its approval to the erection of a memorial to Christopher Columbus on the coast of the Dominican Republic at the capital city of Ciudad Trujillo. This vote of approval was not taken to provide any necessary official sanction, apparently, but rather as a respectful formality whereby a new international union of nations gave its blessing to an enterprise that had been initiated some years earlier by the Pan-American Union and for which the 21 American republics will supply the necessary funds, estimated at about $4,000,000.

Since the proposed monument was designed by a young British architect and his model was on display in London, it was fitting that the UN should have recognized the international significance of the projected tribute to the memory of Columbus while the Assembly was meeting in London. Now that the Security Council of the United Nations is in session at New York city near the site of what may become the capital of a federated world, news of the progress of plans for the Columbian memorial has taken on further related timeliness with the recent announcement that the clearing of the site is well under way and that work may soon be started on the monument itself, which is to include at the top of its 100-foot central pile of masonry a powerful beacon for ships and planes.

Plans for the mammoth monument on the shores of the Dominican Republic were initiated in 1929 when the Pan-American Union offered a prize for an appropriate design, which was finally adopted in 1931 when the prize award went to Joseph Lea Gleave, then a young university graduate only 23 years old. His design had been chosen as the best among all that had been submitted by 454 architects from 48 different countries. The memorial he designed will take the form of a huge recumbent cross 1600 feet long and 1200 feet wide. It will house a chapel and perhaps the bones of Columbus, a Columbian library and museum. It will serve at the same time as a majestic lighthouse and a shrine of historic interest.

A 40-foot model of the prize-winning plan has been exhibited for some time at the Royal Institute of British Architects in London where it attracted the attention of the United Nations Assembly and prompted its resolution of approval. Its designer is now head of the school of architecture at the University of Edinburgh, and the masterpiece of his youth, which, because of war conditions, remained for so many years a dream unfulfilled, seems destined at last to begin taking form in stone within a relatively short time.
