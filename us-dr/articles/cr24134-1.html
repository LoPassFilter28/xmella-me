GEN. MORALES HERE.

Political Exile Comes on Flying Visit from Hayti.

Exiled from his own country, of which he was once virtually dictator, and deported from another for political reasons, General Carlos F. Morales, ex-President of Santo Domingo, arrived here yesterday from Hayti on the steamer Prins Willem II. Fate has dealt unkindly with General Morales since he ran away from his countrv and power in 1906, before the revolution of General Ramon Caceres. His trip to New York was hurried and involuntary, and he will leave here this morning for Porto Rico on the steamer Coamo.

Last evening the ex-President went to the Hotel America, where he stayed for the night, although he made arrangements so that he would not be disturbed by callers. His only visitors were reporters.

Before going out for a walk, after dinner, last night, he inquired the price of a second class passage to Ponce, Porto Rico, but on learning that the difference between the first and second passage rates was only $15, he ordered a first class ticket.

General Morales was a priest before he went into politics. With its greater power than that enjoyed by the clergy in Santo Domingo, politics drew him on, and he discarded the cassock for the uniform of a general. He joined General Juan Isidro Jiménez in the revolution against President Woz y Gil. There was an understanding between General Morales and General Jiménez that if the movement was successful the latter was again to take the Presidency. The rebellion triumphed, but General Morales reached the capital before General Jiménez and declared himself President, notwithstanding the agreement. Two years ago General Ramon Caceres, Vice-President of the republic, began a revolution against President Morales, who in running away to escape imprisonment broke his leg and was captured. General Caceres, who succeeded in establishing himself in power, magnanimously permitted General Morales to leave the country.

Since that time the vanquished general has been in St. Thomas, writing proclamations, manifestoes and "cartas abiertos," criticising the present government, and it has been reported that he would try to reinstate himself as Chief Executive. Fearing some international complication, the Haytian officials deported him at once on his arrival at Port-au-Prince.

"You see, my being here," said General Morales, last night, "came about in this way. Since I was driven out of office and exiled from Santo Domingo, in January, 1906, I have been living with my family in St. Thomas. I thought I would take a little business trip to Port-au-Prince and return home again. When the ship landed at Port-au-Prince there were some generals at the dock, all in gorgeous uniforms, and I was arrested. They took me to the palace and placed me under guard in a salon. I much preferred a hotel, and told the Governor so, and that I would not try to escape. The Governor was very nice about it, but I had to remain in the palace."

The time for revolutions has passed, the former President said, referring to Santo Domingo and her past political turbulences. He said his love for his country would never cease and that should his countrymen call him he would be ready to serve her in any capacity.

The ex-President discussed the political situation in his country, praising President Caceres and also the United States for the collection of the customs revenues for the payment of the foreign debt. He declared that he would oppose, however, an effort to annex Santo Domingo. He expressed the kindest feelings for his enemies, and said he would return to Santo Domingo as soon as a general amnesty was granted to political exiles.

"We have had our troubles in Santo Domingo," he continued, "just as the people of all infant republics have had. When I was President the people were not ready for my policies, and my ministry put me out. Under our constitution I could not appoint a new ministry, and finally they stirred up a revolution and I had to fly. I went out into the country and had the misfortune to fall from my horse and break a leg. Friends carried me secretly to the United States Consulate, and there I was safe.

"I probably would not have become President, but my predecessor, President Woz y Gil, was what you Americans call a sport. He cared nothing for the administration of his office, and so a revolution started and I succeeded him. That was in May, 1903. I did not exile General Jiménez. He went away of his own accord. He is now in San Juan, but I do not think I shall see him. Former President Woz y Gil has a cigar factory in Santiago. I like General Jiménez very much. I hardly think he will ever go back to Santo Domingo.

"President Caceres is conducting his office in an able manner and placing the republic on a firm financial basis. I agreed with the Dominican Committee of Finance that the loan made in the United States should be $10,000,000, rather than $20,000,000, as first proposed. Of this sum the Santo Domingo Improvement Company claims $4,500,000. Think of it—that a company formed in this country to improve our roads and industries should make such a claim when it has not spent one cent in our republic! It is a shame that the United States should force a poor country to pay such a claim. The Santo Domingo Improvement Company has been asked repeatedly to make an accounting of the sums which it claims were spent, but the company has never done so. With a loan of $10,000,000 we could pay all just debts, including the Belgian claim."
