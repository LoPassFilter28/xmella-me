Branch Rickey's Big Mousetrap

VERO BEACH, Fla., Mar. 18.—As befits a genius, Branch Rickey's Brooklyn Dodgers' training camp is like none other in all baseball history. It has not yet been established that Rickey has built a better mousetrap, but by all odds it's the biggest, busiest, noisiest, most expensive and, paradoxically, most economical.

Brother Rickey has converted a decommissioned naval air station into a 1,300-acre training camp, not only for his Dodgers, but for his farm teams, too. Here also the St. Paul, Mobile, Houston, Montreal and sundry other clubs train for the coming season and the coming decade.

There were 258 players in camp the other night and before mid-April between 800 and 900 athletes branded by B. R. will have been run through the assembly line. It is the most complex innovation ever attempted in baseball.

Every ball player, big leaguer or newly signed schoolboy, is given a thorough physical examination, including ear, nose and throat and a Wassermann test. He is assigned a number and he becomes a card in a gigantic filing system. His name goes up on a huge blackboard, which constantly is changed as the player improves, stands still or slips back. (There were nearly 150 pitchers alone on the board the other day.)

Only members of the "varsity"—the Dodgers—may bring their wives, who live in motels near barracks for players who are batching it. Everybody must be in bed by 11 p.m. and arise by 7:30 in the morning.

At Boogie-Woogie, a Big Leaguer

There is a chow line, Navy style, and at the head of the line reads this sign: "Through these portals pass three times daily the hungriest and most talented ball players in the world."

There are billiard rooms, a swimming pool, recreation hall, free orange juice bars. There are talent shows, like the Happy Hours of the Navy. There are movies a couple of times a week. Playing nightly at the piano is a young man named Bill Ahlstedt, and if there's a better boogie-woogie artist in Florida the night-club samplers haven't heard him. By day Ahlstedt is a pitcher, a class C player who won 7 and lost 14 last year for Newport News.

Never one to waste talent, Rickey even has members of his Brooklyn football Dodgers on the scene to teach running, to help with the files and to make themselves generally useful during their own off-season.

Lest judgment be passed on a player who is nursing a charley horse or bone bruise, a gray cap is issued to every athlete not physically fit. Thus, if he seems slow or weak-armed, the assembly-line supervisors will overlook the shortcoming.

It's Preferred Over Ciudad Trujillo

It's estimated that Rickey will spend $225,000 to $300,000 on his camp, but this is a misleading figure. Because he is training his farm teams, with the notable exception of Hollywood of the Pacific Coast League, Rickey actually is saving on a prorata basis.

Fresco Thompson, chief of the farm system, insists the Dodgers like this camp. "They are far happier than last year at Ciudad Trujillo, Dominican Republic," says Fresco. "There, food was cooked in rancid grease and the water tasted funny, and a lot of the fellows didn't like flying to exhibition games."

Presiding over all is Rickey, who sits on a high perch at a vantage point and surveys his realm with an all-seeing eye. It is rank blasphemy to suggest that he lifted the idea of his king-size high chair from Coach Frank Leahy of Notre Dame. More likely he borrowed it from King Solomon.

Rickey is a firm believer in mechanical gadgets to save wear and tear on human tissue. He has a pom-pom type of mechanical pitcher and another machine that is reputed to throw knuckle balls. He has batting tees, which are flexible rods upon which are secured baseballs to be swung at. There is another gimmick called the "eight strings," fired at by pitchers aiming at the strike zone.

A Case of Too Much Muzzle Velocity

There's such a need for fungo hitters that Rickey has "bazookas," long tubes not unlike muzzle-loading mortars. They are adjusted for a proper trajectory and erupt long fly balls to be caught.

Once in a while a thing will backfire, as when Rickey felt that a bazooka aimed straight up would burp out ideal popups for the catchers. His Negro catcher, Roy Campanella, agreed, pulled on his mitt and the gun was fired.

The ball went up . . . up . . . up. It became a golf ball . . . a table tennis ball . . . a marble . . . an aspirin . . . and finally it simply went out of sight.

Such a scattering of folks you never saw, outside of a bombing raid. But by and large, little gets out of Branch Rickey's hand.
