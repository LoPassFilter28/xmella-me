Some Fragments of a Shattered Pledge

By WILLIAM DUDLEY FOULKE,

Former Member U. S. Civil Service Commission.

The last democratic national platform declared that "the law pertaining to the civil service should be honestly and rigidly enforced to the end that merit and ability should be the standard of appointment and promotion rather than service to a political party.". And further that "our pledges are made to be kept when in office as well as relied upon during the campaign, and we invite the co-operation of all citizens, regardless of party, who believe in maintaining unimpaired the institutions and traditions of our country."

Relying upon these assurances and upon the professed friendship of Mr. Wilson to the merit system, a large proportion of the earnest advocates of that system supported the democratic party. What has been done in fulfillment of this pledge?

The civil service act and the rules apply not only to existing positions in the service, but also to places created by future legislation. That was the condition of the civil service law when this promise assured us it would be maintained. But as soon as the democratic party began to enact important laws it also began to chop off piece-meal the application of the civil service law, both to those places which were created by new legislation as well as to places which already existed.

A large number of employes were provided for to administer the income tax, and congress made all the positions in the field force of this service the objects of political plunder, taking them out of the operation of the civil service law by providing for their appointment for two years under regulations fixed by the secretary of the treasury. The president signed the bill, although he had the power to direct the secretary to provide in the regulations for the appointment of these men by competitive tests. This was the first breach of the promise of the platform.

The democratic majority next fastened a rider on the urgent deficiency bill, removing from the operation of the law deputy revenue collectors and deputy marshals, positions which were already in the classified service. President Wilson signed this bill. Here was another distinct violation of the promise both by congress and by the chief executive himself.

Then the new banking law was enacted. All the employes of the federal reserve board created by that act were excluded from the classified service and the president again signed the bill.

Next, fourteen important positions of commercial attaches credited to American ministers abroad were permanently removed from the merit system to be made the spoils of party and changed with each change of administration.

Another important act of the present administration was the trade commission bill, and in this all the attorneys, special experts and examiners were also excluded from the classified service.

Of all the cabinet officers charged with the enforcement of the civil service law, Mr. Bryan, the Warwick, the king maker, the "premier," has been peculiarly the representative and mouthpiece of the administration which was so largely the work of his own creation. It is from his lips that we hear the most eloquent expressions of the lofty ideals with which that administration is permeated. Mr. Bryan's real views, however, can be best determined, not by those touching and edifying homilies which appear for public consumption in his addresses and in the pages of The Commoner, but by the more practical suggestions confidentially made to his own political retainers—communications which were not intended to see the light, but one of which was unexpectedly disclosed when the worthy gentleman to whom it was addressed was afterward removed from the service.

The letter in question was addressed on August 20 last to Walter W. Vick, then receiver general of customs at Santo Domingo. Places in the government service were evidently a little scanty at home. Pickings for the faithful must be found elsewhere, where the classified service did not extend, and in what better place than in Santo Domingo, where nobody was watching and where "our beloved and oppressed brethren" of the Dominican republic might be made to pay the piper?

The revelations of the inner working of Mr. Bryan's mind in respect to the promise of the democracy is shown by the following language, which cannot be too often recalled nor too highly praised for its illuminating power:

"Now that you have arrived and acquainted yourself with the situation, can you let me know what positions you have at your disposal with which to reward deserving democrats? Whenever you desire a suggestion from me in regard to a man for any place down there, call on me. You have had enough experience in politics to know how valuable workers are when the campaign is on and how difficult it is to find suitable rewards for the deserving."

And accordingly, the "strong, courageous, reliable" Sullivan, who was recommended in the letter, proceeded to "reform" things by transferring the public funds to a precious group of patriots in control of the "Banco Nacional," an institution described by one of the witnesses as "a joke," and from which these funds afterward had to be withdrawn and restored to the original depository. A newly created office at "$8,000 a year and expenses" was given to a man without experience, and other illustrations were given of Mr. Bryan's adherence to the principle that "merit, and not political services, should be the standard for appointment."

What excuse can be given for one onslaught after another upon the classified system in order to provide places for the hungry and thirsty retainers of the party in power?

Wm. D. Foulke.
