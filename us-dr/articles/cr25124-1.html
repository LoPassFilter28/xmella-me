The Washington daily news published an editorial criticizing American assertions of non-imperialism, pointing to U.S. control across the Caribbean and Pacific, including customs administration in the Dominican Republic and marine occupation in Haiti. The piece derided official claims of purely benevolent motives while exercising regional dominance. It argued that Washington's treatment of its dependencies, particularly the curtailment of self-rule in the Philippines under General Leonard Wood, contradicted its anti-imperialist rhetoric.El Washington daily news publicó un editorial en el que criticaba las afirmaciones estadounidenses de no ejercer imperialismo, señalando el control de Estados Unidos en el Caribe y el Pacífico, incluida la administración de aduanas en la República Dominicana y la ocupación de la infantería de marina en Haití. El artículo ridiculizó los argumentos oficiales de motivaciones puramente benévolas mientras se ejercía el dominio regional. Sostuvo que el trato que Washington daba a sus dependencias, en particular el menoscabo de la autonomía en Filipinas bajo el general Leonard Wood, contradecía su retórica antiimperialista.

Pickpocket or Parson?

SCENE: A street corner with a lot of doubtful customers hanging around.

A woman, passing by, drops her pocketbook.

Behind her a man of winning mien—perhaps a person, perhaps a pickpocket—the crowd can't tell—stoops and picks it up.

For a minute he stops and turns the object over in his hand.

"Say, cull," says one of the gang, "whatcher go'nter to with it?"

"Give it back to the lady," says the queer-acting man.

And he starts walking after the lady, but so haltingly that his actions belie his words.

"Like fun he is!" mutters the gang. "Watch 'im go south with it."

DURING the Spanish-American war Uncle Sam picked up the Philippines.

"What are you going to do with them?" asked a skeptical world.

"Give them their freedom," said Uncle Sam, looking them over.

"Like fun he will!" leers the knowing old world. "Watch him go south with them."

THE United States today is on trial for its honor. For decades we have been telling the world how honest we are. And now we have got to make good or admit we are no better than the rest—just a great, big, powerful empire whose morals are molded to conform to the dollar.

The whole world is watching to see what we will do next.

"You say you are not imperialistic," it jeers. And it sticks its tongue in its cheek and looks at the Philippines and Porto Rico and Hawaii and Guam and the Virgin Islands; at our virtual protectorate over Cuba and the Republic of Panama; at our control of the customs of the Dominican Republic and Haiti, and our occupation of the latter country by marines; at our hegemony, as they call it, over Central and much of South America.

And reviewing it all, it asks insinuatingly:

"What are you up to in all those places?"

"Just protecting the interests of the unfortunate peoples," we say.

"Oh!" says the world, winking prodigiously.

WELL, a showdown is approaching. The Philippines may well be the test case. And the case of the Philippines is this:

Congress officially promised the Filipinos their freedom as soon as they proved they could govern themselves. Gov. Gen. Harrison from 1916 to 1921 gave them a chance, and they did a pretty good job. And President Wilson in 1920 told Congress the time had about come for America to make good her promise.

Then bang! Something happened. It was Gen. Leonard Wood. He arrived in the islands and as governor general, instead of giving the Filipinos more freedom, took what they had away.

Now the islanders have put the whole thing squarely up to President Harding, meantime refusing to co-operate with Gen. Wood. They have gone out on strike.

CHEATING the Filipinos won't hurt the Filipinos much. But it will hurt us a lot. By cheating the Filipinos we will destroy our own great influence for good in the world. We will destroy the faith an otherwise well-nigh hopeless old earth still somehow manages to retain in us—faith which may yet save mankind from perdition.

We know what the people of this country would do about it. Wood would get his orders, and he would get them right.

But what will the government do? Let it not forget the world, not merely the Philippines, is waiting to hear the answer.
