Crisis and the OAS

At the opening session of their meeting in San Jose, Costa Rica, the 21 foreign ministers of the Organization of American States have been presented with an impressive special report that should be of more than a little help to them in dealing with the urgent problems of the Western Hemisphere.

The report is the work of the Inter-American Peace Committee, an OAS watchdog agency made up of representatives of Uruguay, Venezuela, El Salvador, Mexico and our own country. Having especially in mind the bitter Dominican-Venezuelan controversy and the Havana-Washington crisis, the committee's members have reached the unanimous conclusion that the general situation in the Caribbean has been steadily deteriorating in a dangerous manner.

Further, the committee has agreed that the tensions in that area have been "considerably heightened as a result of an increasing desire ... on the part of the Soviet Union and other extra-continental powers to intervene more actively in inter-American affairs. It is evident from statements made by the highest officials of these powers that they are seeking to exploit for their own purposes some of the aspects of the situation existing in the Caribbean." The report then goes on to warn as follows:

The American community is faced today with a serious crisis ...

Underlying this crisis is the striving of the peoples of America to improve their economic and social conditions ... within a system compatible with the respect for human dignity and the effective exercise of representative democracy.

Yet, in their efforts to achieve these objectives, the peoples of America frequently see themselves confronted by reactionary political forces which reject democracy and cling to the system of military dictatorship, as well as by groups which, influenced by extra-continental powers, maintain that economic progress can only be achieved by the implanting of another type of dictatorship.

... The conflict between the authoritarian tendencies noted above and the democratic aspirations of the vast majority of the peoples of the American states lies at the very heart of the recent international tensions in the Caribbean area.

This constitutes a good diagnosis of the basic ailment of our hemisphere. Although couched in diplomatic language, it makes clear that our whole inter-American system is threatened not only by military dictatorships like Generalissimo Trujillo's in the Dominican Republic but also—and more menacingly—by the Communist-backed dictatorship of Fidel Castro in Cuba. Certainly, since it has established very sinister ties with Moscow and Peiping, the Havana tyranny seems to be particularly dangerous in terms of its apparent readiness to help spread Red totalitarianism throughout the hemisphere.

Secretary Herter and the other foreign ministers now convened in San Jose face an exceedingly difficult and delicate task as they seek to work out mutually satisfactory solutions to these and kindred problems. The OAS, however, has functioned so effectively in the past that there is good reason to hope that it will be able to cope successfully with the great new challenge before it.
