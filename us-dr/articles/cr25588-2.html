The Revolution in San Domingo.

A correspondent of the New York Sun, writing from Puerto Platte under date of February 23, says:

As most of your readers are probably aware, the real cause which precipitated the fall of President Baez was that after the Fabens-Babcock treaty for the annexation of this republic to the United States had been killed in the United States Senate, Baez received money from Spain to enter into a treaty for the deportation to Cuba of refugees from the latter island.

When the revolution which drove Baez into exile had been consummated, Gonzales, who had hitherto been the champion of the Cubans and anti-annexationists, was raised to the Presidency. His promise to protect the integrity of Dominican territory, and carry out the constitution in every detail, were very ample and satisfactory.

The last Spanish occupation of this republic has unfortunately left here an element most pernicious to all republican governments. Under the sway of this element President Gonzales fell. The political action of this element originated in the animosity evinced by Spanish residents of the capital against the publication in this city of two papers which warmly and logically advocated the cause of the independence of Cuba, and it may be said to have culminated in the consent of Gonzales to issue a decree for their suspension. Subsequently money was sent to Gonzales from Havana, and he was induced to sign a treaty with Spain which bound San Domingo to return to Cuba all refugees against whom Spain might trump up a charge of incendiarism. The treaty was cleverly worded, because it carefully avoided comprising in its stipulations the rendition of political prisoners. But of course it virtually included every Cuban exile, for Spain could easily accuse any of them of complicity, at least, with incendiaries.

As soon as it was known here that the treaty had really been signed, the Cuban population not unreasonably felt alarmed. The Governor of this city increased this alarm by issuing a decree that if more than three Cuban exiles embarked for foreign ports on board any one vessel, they should be seized and delivered over to Spanish war vessels as intending to form part of a filibustering expedition against the peace of Spain.

The Cubans held sundry meetings on the subject, and a final one was to have been held on Sunday, the twenty-third ultimo, when a radical change occurred, the details of which I will condense from my note book.

At 4:30 P. M., January 23, shots were heard in the little plaza in front of the Protestant church, in the neighborhood of the government house, and at the residence of General Luperon. The latter, who was in his house with two friends, returned the fire. The whole city was excited. The common council assembled, sent for the foreign consuls, and forwarded propositions to Governor Ortea and General Luperon, which were declined. Ortea insists on capturing Luperon dead or live, Luperon resists, and all hope of a peaceful solution seem at an end. Everybody expected a fearful night of fire and blood, but luckily it passed in alarms only. The laborantes avail of the opportunity to spread thousands of rumors against Ortea and President Gonzales.

Monday, twenty-fourth.—The Governor accedes to the protest of the common council and consuls, and offers guarantees to General Luperon until the receipt of fresh orders from the government. Outside the city several cantons assemble opposed to the government; committees are sent out by the common council to confer with the cantons, but the latter refused to yield; reports are circulated that everything will be settled without having recourse to arms. But the laborantes work hard; they manage to induce the troops to desert, and fraternization between the soldiers and the Cubans in the shape of drinks and handshaking is the order of the day. A lieutenant whom I met in the bodeja of Concha Rey said to me, "Look out to-night, at twelve we meet at the end of the Plaza del Mercado to attack the commandancia where Ortea is shut up." They are more than on time; firing commenced at eleven o'clock. One man was killed, and they disperse. Many families leave the city; men go out into the country, and the Cuban families seek and find refuge in the English, American and German consulates. None go the French consulate, for the consul is supposed to be in league with the Spaniards.

Tuesday, twenty-fifth—Three guns are discharged from the fort as a signal of alarm, but nobody comes in from the country. A proclamation is read at the street corners calling on the citizens to arms, and declaring every one an enemy who does not repair to the government house. All stores and private houses are closed, and the city wears a mournful aspect. One meets nobody but groups of Cubans in the streets.

Wednesday, twenty-sixth—The city is declared in a state of siege, and citizens are prohibited from carrying arms. More excitement.

Thursday, twenty-seventh—Groups of more than three people are prohibited from assembling in the streets.

Friday, twenty-eight—A committee arrives from Santiago de los Caballeros with a protest against the abuses of Ortea. An impeaching board had been appointed in Santiago, and the people of this place give in their adhesion to the accusation there made before the Legislature against President Gonzalez.

Saturday, twenty-nine—A committee goes to Santiago, composed of the President of the Common Council and other prominent citizens. To-day two barricades are completed at the fort.

Thirtieth—Nothing but alarms.

Monday, thirty-first—A bando (proclamation by town crier) is issued, declaring that Governor Ortea did not attempt to escape last night by flight. It states that he relies on 800 armed men and on public opinion. This announcement is made as usual, accompanied by music, and is read out aloud at the corner of every two or three blocks. The reason whereof is that the majority of the people here can not read.

Tuesday, February 1.—At 5 A. M. crowds began to assemble in the streets shouting, "Hurrah for the constitution!" Down with the treaty!" and not a few added "Death to Spain!" Luperon rides through the streets on horseback with a considerable following mounted and on foot. He takes the Comandancia and the house of the municipality. At 7 A. M. the common council assembles a committee from this body, to which are added Messrs. Francisco Colis and Pedro Recio, both Cubans, and Ponce de Leon, of Porto Rico, is appointed and sent to Ortea, demanding his surrender and the capitulation of the fort, with guarantee of life to everybody. The people indorsed the impeachment of Gonzales, as demanded by the citizens of Santiago.

At 8:30 the whole population was gathered in front of the government house, the protest from Santiago was read, and it was unanimously indorsed. General Luperon addresses the crowd, and sketches the abuses of the administration of Gonzales and of subalterns, Ortea in particular. He adds that he is in possession of letters from several generals, who assure him that the entire province of Cibao has "pronounced."

Ortea requests that in his surrender the consular body should accompany the committee which waited on him. This request is granted, he surrenders, and at 12 M., in company with General Luperon, takes refuge in the British consulate. At 10 A. M. General Arceno enters the city at the head of fifty men. At eleven, General Lithgow and others, who some days previously had been imprisoned by Ortea, are set at liberty.

At 4 P. M. a proclamation is issued announcing that Senor Segundo Ymben had been appointed Governor and General Lithgow commander of the city. Until Senor Ymben's return he is represented by General Telesforo Pelegrin. The families who had taken refuge in the consulates return to their homes. A loose sheet detailing the occurrences is published. It is stated that President Gonzales is in the vicinity of Santiago, and refuses to submit to trial. Santiago is the centre of the movement. End of my note book.

Since then there has been nothing important in this city except the sending of soldiers into the country. President Gonzales collected some cantons, who helped him to resist, and a few people were killed. On the seventeenth he surrendered to a committee from the capital, and he has repaired thither. The Baezistas meanwhile have not been idle, and have managed to raise a few cantons in one or two places. It seems now as if everything might pass off without the horrors of a civil war, although these people belong to the last person who may harangue them.

The English steamer arrived to-day from the capital, and brought the news that Gonzales, impeached as he was, has resigned. I am inclined to think that this was the best way to settle matters.
