PRESIDENT'S MESSAGE GIVEN TO THE PUBLIC Clearly Outlines Government's Policy Towards San Domingo Republic.

Washington, Feb. 17.—President Roosevelt's message, transmitting to the senate the new Dominican protocol of an agreement providing for the collection and disbursement by the United States of the customs revenues of San Domingo for the adjustment of all obligations of that government, has been made public by order of the senate in executive session.

Th document includes a new agreement as well as the original protocol and award of the settlement of claims of the Domingo Improvement Co., under which the agents of the United States are already collecting revenues at certain ports of the Dominican government.

Few documents that have came to the senate in relation to any of the South or Central American or West Indian republics, have attracted so much attention. Because of the controversy that has arisen as to the right of the executive to enter into treaty arrangements with any foreign government without the advice and consent of the senate extraordinary interest has been aroused.

The document came to the senate Wednesday at an hour past the usual time for most senators to leave the chamber and therefore it was brought up soon after the senate convened yesterday. The message of the president outlined the policy of the administration in relation to the Monroe doctrine, holding that it demanded that the government take charge of the customs of American states when it is manifest that they are unable to maintain their own integrity.

In addition to the president's discussion of the Monroe doctrine in the case of San Domingo, the protocol itself makes a specific declaration of its application and in that respect is unusual in treaty-making. The declaration occurs in the second paragraph, which recites that the government of the United States views "Any attempt on the part of a foreign government outside of the western hemisphere to oppress the Dominican republic as a manifestation of unfriendly disposition towards the United States."

The especial point is made by the president, that the government does not propose to take any part of San Domingo or to exercise any other control over the island save what is necessary to its financial rehabilitation in connection with the collection of revenue, a part of which will be turned over to the government to meet necessary expenses of running it and a part of which will be distributed pro rata among the creditors of the republics upon a basis of absolute equity.

Discussing the course aggrieved nations may take against American republics without interference with the Monroe doctrine, the president says any action may be taken which "does not take the shape of interference with their form of government or any disportionment of their territory under any disguise, but short of this, when the question is one of a money claim, the only way which remains, finally, to collect, is to blockade or a bombardment, or a seizure of the custom house; and this means what it is in effect, possession, even though only temporary possession, of the territory. The United States then becomes a party in interest, because under the Monroe doctrine it cannot see any European powers seize and permanently occupy territory of one of these republics. And yet such seizure of territory, disguised or undisguised, may eventually offer the only way in which a power in question can collect any debts, unless there is interference on the part of the United States. There are governments which do sometimes take energetic action for the protection of their subjects in the enforcement of merely contractual claims, and thereupon American concessionaries supported by powerful influence, make loud appeal to the United States government in similar cases for similar action.

Incorporated in the message is a financial statement showing that the Dominican government owes to foreign governments $22,600,000 and $18,000,000 of this amount is recognized. The president discusses at length the negotiation for the collection of these debts, and says that as foreign governments were put off from time to time they became importunate in pressing their unsatisfied demands.

Relative to Cuba, the president says:

"We can point with just pride to what we have done in Cuba as a guaranty of our good faith. We stayed in Cuba only as long as was necessary to start her aright on the road to self-government, which she has since trod with such marked and distinguished success. And upon leaving the island we exacted no conditions save such as would prevent her from ever becoming the prey of the stranger. Our purpose in San Domingo is as beneficent. The chief material advantage that will come from the action proposed to be taken will be to San Domingo itself and to San Domingo's creditors. The advantages that will come to us will be indirect, but nevertheless great, for it is supremely to our interest that the communities south of us should be or become prosperous and stable and therefore not merely in name, but in act, independent and self-governing.

"I shall call attention to the urgent need of prompt action on this matter. We now have a great opportunity to secure peace and stability in the island, without friction or bloodshed, by acting in accordance with the cordial invitation of the government authorities themselves. It will be unfortunate from every standpoint if we fail to grasp this opportunity, for such failure will probably mean increasing revolutionary violence in San Domingo, and very possibly foreign complications in addition. This protocol affords a practical test of the efficiency of the United States in maintaining the Monroe doctrine."
