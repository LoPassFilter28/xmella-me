The Negro in Latin America

JIM CROW VS. THE GOOD NEIGHBOR

By Harold Preece

Old Man Jim Crow is the great-granddaddy of all fascists. He was born from a cross between a horsewhip and a tar bucket; he was yanked out into the world with a lynch rope and sleeps with death in the creek bottoms.

He hates colored people like the devil hates truth. He hates for folks of different skin to be good neighbors.

So he's trying to put his brand on Latin America with its great tradition of equality, and on our Good Neighbor Policy toward the 21 Latin-American republics.

Last week I pointed out how the new Good Neighbor policy of President Franklin D. Roosevelt was bringing the colored Negro-Indian majority of Latin America jobs, hospitals, schools, and luxuries like sewing machines that they had never had during that long nightmare of "big-stick" imperialism. I pointed out that these new, good things for our colored brothers of Latin-America would help us hammer out a good neighbor policy for Mississippi and Georgia as well as Mexico and Guatemala.

That's the way most Americans feel about it since most Americans are neither lynchers nor landlords. That's not the way Jim Crow feels about it and the old scoundrel has a lot of his friends scattered through Congress and our State Department.

Recently, a leading Negro newspaper revealed in a blistering expose that Confederates in the State Department had forced Negro Haiti to shame herself before all democracies by drawing color distinctions between light-skinned and dark-skinned Haitians.

Haiti, it was said, was bringing all of her dark-skinned diplomats home from the United States and seeing to it that almost white people were her sole representatives in this country which fought a bloody Civil War for the freedom of all colored peoples—whether they were pure black, mulatto, or light octoroon.

I'm not excusing President Elie Lescot and the rest of Haiti's official family for appeasing Dixie when I point out that more than $10,000,000 of American sugar trust cash is invested in Haiti. The American dollars that control the country are, in turn, controlled by sugar trust outfits like the Haitian Agricultural Corporation and the Haitian American Development Company.

Both of these corporations are among the old Big Stick imperialist outfits that gained a foothold in Latin America, with the assistance of United States marines who shot and murdered rebellious colored peoples under the gentle guidance of that old firebag, President Theodore Roosevelt.

Both of them, along with United Fruit, Standard Oil, and other giant American trusts, hate the Good Neighbor policy because it means paying wages of more than a quarter a day to colored workers. It means that they'll have to stop their policy of giving some cream to native aristocrats—like many of the Haitian mulattoes— who acted as their straw bosses over the rest of the people.

But the peoples of Latin America will maintain their tradition of equality and fraternity whatever the funny business that may be pulled by corporations and governments.

Equality comes easy to them; was it not the Negroes and Indians who, time after time, forced the wishy-washy white aristocracy to keep on fighting for independence against imperial Spain when the aristocrats sought compromises that would have benefited them but would have harmed the colored peoples.

The spirit of equality and fraternity has already beaten Old Man Jim Crow across boundary lines and into countries where colored people form very small percentages of the population. Costa Rica, where the population is overwhelmingly white, recently repealed her Chinese Exclusion Law, following the lead of Cuba, whose people, by a generous estimate, are no more than fifty per cent white.

White Costa Rica went to the polls the other day and elected a progressive president over a racist candidate who, it said, was being helped along with guns smuggled into the country by somebody close to the United Fruit Company. White Costa Ricans would clamp Senator Bilbo or any other Dixie fascist into jail if he came to their country suggesting that the small Negro minority, living along the coast and back in the mountains, should be deported to Africa.

If a Negro Costa Rican should ever be elected president—and that is not inconceivable in most any Latin American country—very few white citizens of the republic would feel alarmed or disgraced. Along with the strong tradition of equality and fraternity in these countries, there is the tradition that any talented person has the right to serve his country with all the gifts of his talent.

Many Presidents

Nobody knows just how many people, with Negro blood, have been presidents of Latin American republics. Records about race aren't kept very carefully in a part of the world where race is not a chronic mass hysteria.

Nilo Pecanha, former president of Brazil, would have done credit to the White House.

Cuban President Fulgencio Batista is a Negro ex-sergeant who worked his way up to the highest office in the gift of his people. President Elie Lescot of Haiti and Rafael Trujillo of the Dominican Republic are Negroes who might as well recognize that Dixie "statesmen" will always regard them as Negroes, even if they spend their days trying to change their complexions with prayers and bleaching creams.

It is hard to find any legislative body in Latin America which does not have a large quota of Negroes, sponsoring and debating bills and resolutions, serving as chairmen of important committees, giving far better accounts of themselves than the poll tax shysters who make laws for Texas and Tennessee.

Our country has had only two Negro senators—Blanche K. Bruce and Hiram R. Revels—elected during the never-to-be-forgotten days of Reconstruction. Dozens of Latin-American Negroes have served in their countries as senators.

Cuba's Negro senators have followed in the footsteps of those early American Negro senators and congressmen who saw that ignorance was a worse danger to a country than invasion. They have been particularly active in getting better schools for poor children of both races.

Cuba's outstanding Negro patriot, Juan Gualberto Gomez, died as a senator of the republic which his restless, energetic brain had helped to create. He fought for education in Cuba—through a decent public school system and for an enlightened free press living up to the standard of his paper, Fraternity.

Latin-Americans are very much puzzled over the poll tax requirements that Dixie enforces for voting, and over the fact that ten per cent of America's citizens have only one congressman—William L. Dawson—to speak for them in our national legislative body.

They are much more interested, however, in what Congressman Dawson has to say than in the foul bromides which Mississippi Congressman John Rankin spews out when he takes the floor. They point out that they have a tradition of sending Negroes to their congresses because Negroes produce able men who ought to be in Congress.

If an American Negro went to Little Honduras, for example, he would see many of his race sitting in the chambers of the national legislative body. The same thing would have been true in Brazil before the present Vargas dictatorship.

Vargas doesn't think much of legislatures and, privately, doesn't think much of Negroes. But even this iron-handed despot has not dared to flout the equalitarian tradition of his country where about forty per cent of the people have Negro blood.

What goes for politics also goes for business in Latin America. Negroes serve capably and well as owners and managers of commercial firms, as salesmen, jobbers, bookkeepers, and clerks.

Women are just beginning to enter business and professions in the 21 republics.

But it will not be long before trim Negro girls will be pounding typewriters, side by side, with trim white girls in neat, comfortable offices without Jim Crow signs. When woman suffrage becomes a reality in that part of the world, those same girls will take time off to go to the polls and vote without the shabby restriction of the poll tax.

Nor will the Latin American countries ever need a Fair Employment Practices Committee as our good neighbor develops her industry and commerce. The growing Latin-American labor movement has never been bothered with the problem of color because the larger proportion of its members were Negroes and Indians to begin with.

The peoples of Latin-America fought the battle of color when they fought the white Spaniards and sent them sailing back across the Atlantic. For them that battle was finished, long, long ago.

And Old Man Jim Crow will be finished if he keeps on trying to stir up an old battle in a new world.
