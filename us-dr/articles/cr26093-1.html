Our Side of Ocean Enjoyed Relatively Calm 50 Years; Relations Within Inter-American Family Were Marred by Few Serious Wars; Mexico Invasion a Black Mark

By Edward Tomlinson

In the field of hemisphere relations the 300,000,000 people who live in the American nations may well look back over the past half a century with a considerable degree of pride and satisfaction. Here in the New World are located more than half the sovereign and independent countries of the universe. Most of them are nations as different one from another, in race, language, culture and tradition, as are the nations of the Old World. Yet for 50 years their relations with one another have seldom been marred by serious war and bloodshed. At least during that period they have made more progress in the voluntary peaceful solution of their international disputes than the balance of the world has made since the birth of Christ.

Since 1900 two catastrophic wars have ravaged and devastated Europe and Asia and taken the lives of millions of men, women and children. A dozen more bloody conflicts were fought in the Balkans, the Near East and Asia. During those same years the entire North and South American continents have experienced only a half dozen international clashes.

One of the most serious of these was our invasion of Mexico just prior to the first World War. Another was the long-drawn-out border dispute between Paraguay and Bolivia in the Gran Chaco of South America which reached its climax in the early '30s. Even the fighting in the Chaco was eventually halted and a satisfactory solution of the border question worked out through the co-operative methods and procedures which the American nations have applied to their regional disputes over the years.

Haitians Massacred

One of the bloodiest, if not the most inexcusable of these conflicts also took place in the '30s, when 10,000 Haitians, who had been accused of occupying or squatting on Dominican territory, were brutally massacred. Fortunately, the tragic incident was not allowed to lead to open warfare between the two countries. There were brief encounters between Peru and Colombia over a remote piece of Amazon jungle territory called Leticia, and the more recent clash between Peru and Ecuador. Both of these difficulties were settled by the peaceful and friendly efforts of their neighbors.

Within the past 12 months two minor threats to peace, one involving Nicaragua and Costa Rica, and the other between the Dominican Republic and Haiti, have so far been kept under control by the peace machinery of the Organization of American States. Even the current sword rattling of Dictator-President Trujillo of the Dominican Republic over what he charges are plots by Cuba and Guatemala to overthrow him is already being dealt with by the Peace Committee of the OAS.

This American machinery of peace, which characterizes the Organization of American States is something unique. It is the oldest, as well as the most successful international peace body in existence.

Long before any one even dreamed of the League of Nations or the United Nations, leaders of the American republics had been gathering periodically in public meetings and thrashing out in open debate their neighborhood problems and disagreements. The first of these meetings took place in Washington, D. C., on April 14, 1890, when delegates of the various countries met and passed a resolution setting up the "Commercial Bureau of the American Republics." Its duties consisted primarily of gathering and passing on trade and other economic information. But during a long series of meetings held in Mexico City from 1900 to 1902, the work of the bureau was expanded and transformed into the Pan American Union with a staff of eminent scholars and diplomats to direct and operate its many agencies.

Model for Atlantic Pact

Finally at another and more recent meeting in Mexico City, in 1945, a complete reorganization of the Pan American Union was agreed upon, the details of which were subsequently adopted at the Bogota Conference in April of 1948. The new and revitalized Organization of American States with an Assembly, a Council and other agencies similar to those of the United Nations, is the result. To co-operative agencies formerly in existence and covering almost every inter-American activity, from public health and sanitation to military collaboration, have been retained and new ones added.

The organization even has teeth of power mutually agreed upon to defend any or all the countries against a possible attack or encroachment by a non-American power, as well as aggression from within the American family of nations. Its military treaty of reciprocal assistance, adopted at Rio de Janeiro in July of 1947 and now in effect; a treaty which became the model for the newly created North Atlantic pact, is backed up by the Inter-American Defense Board, a sort of permanent inter-American general staff representing all the military establishments of the different nations.

There are many other unique and significant aspects of this hemisphere machinery of collaboration. Perhaps one of the most important and that which differentiates it from the United Nations, is the fact that its growth and development have been the result of gradual and entirely voluntary effort. It did not begin with a high-sounding constitution and a definite set of iron-clad rules to be put into effect and applied overnight. It has become the effective organization it now is because its development has been step by step; its every move based on experience and mutual, usually unanimous, agreement.

More important still, the OAS operates on a basis of absolute equality between nations, large or small. Both in its general conferences or assemblies and in its council meetings every nation is represented. Every nation has a representative on the council with the rank of ambassador, who has power to speak for his government. No one nation or group, however powerful, can force a veto. Tiny Haiti's vote is equal to that of the United States or Brazil.

Weakness Noted

If the spirit and performance of this all-American family organization seems to be largely on the credit side, insofar as international relations are concerned, we may as well acknowledge certain weaknesses and shortcomings as they apply to the international affairs of the various countries.

Every treaty, resolution and document in the OAS framework is dedicated to the maintenance and furtherance of democratic institutions. One of the most eloquent international pronouncements ever penned is the Declaration on the Rights and Duties of Man adopted at the 1948 conference in Bogota, a document which antedated the much discussed Declaration of Human Rights later adopted by the United Nations. In the Bogota declaration, consisting of 38 separate articles, every country in this hemisphere pledges itself to guarantee to its people all the civil, religious and political rights, including the right of free speech, the right to vote and to participate in government, the right to protection under law, the right to own property and all the others.

Yet, today, in Venezuela and Peru, in the Dominican Republic and Argentina, in more than a third of all the 21 republics, dictatorships hold complete or partial sway over the lives and property of their peoples. Freedom of speech, press and radio are partially or wholly denied in Argentina, the Dominican Republic, Peru, Nicaragua, Venezuela and several of the other nations. Literally thousands of persons are in exile from the Dominican Republic, Nicaragua and the various countries of their birth because their political opinions differ from those who hold power or control the individual governments.

Governments Upset

In Venezuela and Peru and several others constitutionally elected presidents and congresses have recently been overthrown by military cliques. Colombia's long record of peaceful, impartial and constitutional government has lapsed. In the recent elections only one party participated, and the campaign that preceded it was marked by coercion, rioting and murder.

In the OAS, in spite of its injunctions and rules for the preservation of democratic institutions, the self-chosen representatives of all the dictator and arbitrary governments not only participate on an equal footing with the representatives of the most democratic governments, but they are often key members of important committees and agencies. They share equal responsibility in the investigation and settling of disputes and difficulties between other countries.

At any rate, as the members of the inter-American family contemplate the first half of the 20th century, they are entitled to the greatest satisfaction over the progress that has been made in developing regional machinery of co-operation, and the peaceful solution of most of their major international difficulties. But they may well be disturbed by the unfortunate trend of events that have marked the integral political situation in many of the countries in the last two or three years.
