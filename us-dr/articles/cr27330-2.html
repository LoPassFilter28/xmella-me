DRASTIC STEPS TO STOP REVOLT Dominican Rebels Told They Will Not Be Recognized, If Successful. STATE DEPARTMENT ACTS United States Also Will Refuse to Turn Over Customs Proceeds, of Which It Is Receiver.

The "big stick," "dollar," "drum-stick," and other forms of diplomacy exercised by the United States in its efforts to maintain peace in the turbulent republics of Latin America have all been outdone by the present administration under the Democratic party, which has vigorously attacked the milder policies of previous administrations as too radical and high-handed. This was revealed yesterday when the action of the State Department with reference to the revolution in the Dominican Republic was learned.

The administration determined to discourage the Dominican revolution to the utmost of its power, and in so doing laid down two new and radical extensions of previous policy in Latin-America. The rebels in Santo Domingo were notified first, that if they succeed in overthrowing the Bordas government in that country they will not receive the recognition of the United States. This important declaration was followed by one even more significant and drastic, to the effect that if the rebels succeed in gaining control of the government the United States will not only refuse to recognize them as a government, but will decline to turn over to them the proceeds of the Dominican customs houses, of which the United States is the receiver, under the convention of 1907.

Has Had Good Effect.

This stern and repressive policy upon the part of the United States, backed up by two such vigorous threats as the declarations enumerated above, has already had its effect. Fighting in Santo Domingo has practically ceased, and the rebels are making no attempt to extend their control of territory. It is confidently believed that as soon as the meaning of the announcements of the United States have sunk into the minds of the rebel leaders, and they are thoroughly convinced that the State Department will make good on its declarations, if the issue is forced, the Dominican revolution will disappear.

So great is the confidence felt in the success of this plan, by virtue of its intense practicability, that officials here familiar with Latin-American troubles go so far as to venture the prediction that the days of revolutions in Central America and the West Indies are nearly over. While the United States has not the same control over the finances of other Latin-American countries as it has over those of the Dominican Republic, it is believed that the Washington administration which can so readily bring into play the means available for keeping peace in Santo Domingo will find a method of maintaining order in other revolution-ridden countries.

Greed at Root of Revolt.

Information laid before President Wilson by State Department officials convinces him that the revolution in Santo Domingo was purely one of greed upon the part of a small group of malcontents, and that the rebellion against the Bordas government was not established on any principle other than that of a desire for office and the proceeds thereof. Consequently, it was determined almost at the outset that everything should be done to discourage the revolutionists. They were at once informed of the disfavor with which the Washington government viewed their attempt to bring on another political upheaval. This declaration of disapproval was soon followed by the double threat of nonrecognition and withholding funds in case the rebels should succeed by force in gaining control of the government authority.

Minister Sullivan, recently appointed Minister to Santo Domingo, has arrived at Puerto Plata, the stronghold of the revolutionists, and is reiterating to the rebel leaders the determined position of President Wilson. He is backed up by the gunboat Des Moines. It is expected that as soon as the rebel leaders are convinced that the United States government means what it says in the message conveyed through Mr. Sullivan the revolutionary forces will dwindle away. It is quite possible that some sort of a compromise will be made peacefully between the government and the revolutionists, who include in their number officials of the province of Puerto Plata.

Collects Customs Dues.

The convention of 1907 provides that the United States shall collect the customs dues of the Dominican Republic, and apply them to the payment of the bonds by which the republic was brought out of bankruptcy. The receiver general of Dominican customs, Walter E. Vick, was appointed by President Wilson under authority granted him in the convention, and he has absolute control over all funds so received. It is the practice for him to remit to the New York banking house acting as Dominican fiscal agent the entire sum received each month, minus the expenses of the receivership. Out of this sum there is remitted to the Dominican government all that is left after providing for the monthly interest and additions to the sinking fund. From this procedure there is absolutely no escape by the rebels, so that they face absolute bankruptcy from the start in case they overthrow the present government.
