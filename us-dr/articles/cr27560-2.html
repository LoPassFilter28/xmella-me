Caribbean Islands Preparing For Big Vacation Influx

Special Dispatch to The Star

KINGSTON, Jamaica, Dec. 10.—Next to Florida and Mexico the islands and countries of the Caribbean are experiencing one of the most intensive winter resort building booms in history. Upward of $100,000,000 worth of new luxury hotels, beach clubs and other vacation spots recently have opened their doors or are being rushed to completion.

Winter tourist travel to this area was already becoming popular before the war, but it was largely a cruise-ship travel. People spent most of their time on board and went ashore in the various ports only to sightsee and shop. The principal reason for this was lack of accommodations on shore. Except in Nassau, Havana and Jamaica, there were few good hotels. Most of them did not know how—or did not think it necessary—to cater to the tastes and desires of American travelers.

With but very few exceptions, there has been a vast improvement all along the line. As everywhere else in the world, a tremendous effort is being made to lure the Yankee dollar. In Puerto Rico, the new 300-room, air-conditioned Caribe-Hilton Hotel has just opened its doors. The old Condado Beach Hotel, one of the early Biltmore chain houses built in the fabulous '20s, has been refurbished and a new wing added. Leading, not to say showing the way in all this modern hotel building in San Juan, is the meticulously operated Pan American Guest House, one of a number of up-to-date small hotels operated by Pan American World Airways in several countries and islands.

The ultra-modern Jaragua Hotel in Trujillo City, capital of the Dominican Republic, has brought Generalissimo Trujillo more favorable publicity than any other activity in the little country over which he rules with an iron hand. The Jaragua, overlooking the sea and cooled by the trade winds that sweep out of the South Atlantic, is a Latin American innovation in fine cuisine and service.

It has become so popular that the general has had constructed a smaller but equally modern and luxurious inn at the picturesque mountain town of Jarabacoa, a 2½-hour drive over a new, paved highway from Trujillo City.

Havana's American-operated Hotel Nacional, with its acres of parks and gardens and its several swimming pools, one of them almost lake size, is continually crowded, winter and summer, in spite of its high prices. But it will soon have stiff competition when the new Hotel Varadero Internacional at Varadero Beach is completed, probably next March. Cubans insist that this new edifice will be the island's most modern and luxurious hostelry. A private beach, as well as pool, a ritzy cabana club, several bars, restaurants and a night club will be among the chief features.

In Nassau, the famed British Colonial, the Fort Montagu Beach and the Royal Colonial Hotels are still well run and popular. But a number of new clubs and beach resorts will be ready for business early in the year. The finishing touches are now being made to Balmoral Club, 5 miles from Nassau on Cable road. Balmoral Club will have 14 Georgian-style houses, each consisting of three bedrooms, with private baths, a living room, sun porch, dining room and kitchen. Vacationers may rent an entire dwelling or simply a room and bath, and dine at the central clubhouse. In addition to restaurants, the clubhouse will have the usual bars and shops.

A similar but smaller club will be inaugurated on Eleuthera Island in January. Known as the Three Bays, it will have an 18-hole golf course, bathing and sporting facilities, including a special fishing cruiser which will take guests to the schooner cays on fishing parties.

But perhaps the most unusual tourist project now taking shape in the Bahamas is William E. Butlin's Vacation Village at West End, Grand Bahama. On this tiny island, 70 miles due east from Palm Beach, Fla., and reached by plane from the Florida coast, Mr. Butlin is converting a sleepy old fishing town into an $8,000,000 island playground. Vacation Village will cater principally to the $50-to-$100-a-week vacationers among the white-collar workers, or those who cannot afford the higher priced resorts. It will have accommodations for 2,000 guests. Mr. Butlin, who also owns and operates the Fort Montagu Beach Hotel in Nassau, is famous for his holiday camps in the British Isles, where hundreds of thousands of London clerks and other professional and office workers enjoy inexpensive outings and vacations each summer.

Next to Nassau, the British colony of Jamaica offers the best hotels and vacation facilities of any of the British possessions in the Caribbean. The spacious old Myrtle Bank in Kingston and the Casa Blanca and Sunset Lodge at Montego Bay and others are well known for the comfort and atmosphere of British elegance. But one of the newest is the Tower Isle Hotel, a complete resort within itself. It is located 55 miles from Kingston, just off the main highway to Montego Bay. It has accommodations for 160 guests and will eventually have its own golf course and other recreational facilities.

The Island Republic of Haiti, in anticipation of the influx of visitors to its $15,000,000 International Exposition in February, is readying a number of small modern hotels. The Roosevelt, 3 miles from the heart of Port Au Prince, on the bayshore drive, is expected to open in January. It will accommodate 100 persons.

The El Rancho, built by United States and Haitian interests, will open January 1 under American management. Only part of the rooms will be ready at that time, but it is planned for 100 guests. The El Rancho is 1,000 feet above the sea and overlooks the great sugar-cane plantations of the Cul de Sac Plain, as well as Port Au Prince and the Bay of Gonave.

The Virgin Islands are at last enjoying a hotel building boom. The 125-room Virgin Isle Hotel on St. Thomas, up in the hills from the island capital of Charlotte Amalie, is scheduled to open in March. Its spacious dining and dancing terrace, swimming pool and other features will make it the first luxury hotel in these American possessions. The well-known Bluebeard Castle Hotel and others, built in the early days of the Roosevelt "New Deal," are being reconditioned.

Trinidad and Barbados, most southerly of the British West Indies, have long attracted winter visitors from the British Islands and some from the United States and Canada. More and more Americans are visiting them each winter, although they have made less progress in providing new and modern hotel facilities than either Jamaica or Nassau. The air-conditioned Piarco Guest House in Trinidad is another in the chain of Pan American Airways hotels. Located near the Piarco international airport, 20 miles from the city of Port of Spain, it has become a crossroads for air travelers.

The comparatively new Hotel Avela in Caracas, Venezuela, built by Nelson Rockefeller, is one of the outstanding hotels on the South American continent.
