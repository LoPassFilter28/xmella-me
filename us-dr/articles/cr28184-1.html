New Honors for Columbus

THE OBSERVANCE of Columbus Day this Friday will mark the 500th anniversary of the birth of the great admiral whose voyages opened a path to the New World. Columbus was born to a middle-class Genoese family in 1451, studied at a nearby university and then went to sea. Ever a student of nautical knowledge, he pored over sea charts, studied existing instruments and avidly listened to tales of seafarers who had observed strange portents of a distant land. Research convinced him that the world was shaped like a sphere, but he actually set off on his voyages under two misapprehensions. He underestimated the size of the world and he overestimated the size of Asia. Thus he hoped to find a short route to Asia, the lands made famous by Marco Polo, by simply sailing west. Instead, he discovered a new world far from the Orient.

His first expedition numbered 88 persons and three ships. They weighed anchor at Palos, Spain, August 3, 1492, and dropped anchor, October 12, 1492, off the island of San Salvador. Arriving back in Spain in March, 1493, Columbus immediately went to Barcelona, where Ferdinand and Isabella were holding court and his stories of the land he had found kindled the fire of adventure that led to the settling of the Americas.

Not only in America is Columbus honored today. His birthplace, Genoa, has many statues and plaques in his honor, plus a museum where many things pertaining to his career have been gathered. This year, in honor of the anniversary, other items have been brought from distant places and put on display in the Columbus Museum in Genoa, where most of these pictures were taken. The others were made in Santo Domingo, where Columbus is buried, in accordance with his wishes that his body might lie in "the land I loved the best."
