HEUREAUX'S BLOODY RULE.

HIS END A BEFITTING ONE FOR HIS CAREER.

Held the Nominal Republic in Iron Grasp, and Conquered His Foes With Knife, Bullet and Rope, Instead of by Finesse or Intrigue.

THERE was dramatic justice in the tragic end of the Dominican president, Ulysses Heureaux, who died by an assassin's hand last Wednesday. Out of a modern republic he had wrought a medieval empire, and from the intrigues and cruelties he had fostered grew his own undoing. He was shot to death by the son of a man whom he had sent fifteen years before to an untimely end. When Ramon Caceres, at Moca, on Wednesday afternoon, fired the shots which ended Heureaux's life, he avenged not only the murder of his father, but that of scores of others who had been killed at the dictator's word—some with a feint show of legal trial, others by cold-blooded and undisguised assassination. Throughout his career Heureaux showed a contempt for human life which was scarcely matched in the days of the Huguenot struggle in France. He conquered his foes with knife and bullet, ignoring modern diplomacy and political intrigue for the surer methods of hired assassin or firing squad. Ostensibly the president of a republic, he was, in fact, as absolute a monarch as ever wore a crown, and he held the life of every citizen of his beautiful island country in his hand.

Heureaux was unique in his generation. Of unbending will, endowed with much intellectual force, and possessed of great courage, he strove honestly for the people of his fatherland, but with methods which found their only counterpart in the middle ages. In the less than fifty years of his life he rose from a half-breed peasant to the dictatorship of Santo Domingo, and in the thirteen years of his administration he materially advanced the country in civilization, welding out of a mass of half savage brigands an army of considerable strength, and building up a financial system which brought prosperity to his people. He established schools, compelled a degree of education in the cities, and fostered a commerce which increased a hundred fold under his care. His people looked upon him either with a devotion akin to idolatry, or with horror and fear, for his vigorous personality admitted of no lukewarm sentiment. Whether they loved or hated him, they obeyed him without question.

Thirty years ago, an ill-clad, half-starved mulatto boy from the hills of the interior, he joined as a private the so-called army. It was a lawless, impotent mob when Private Heureaux found it, controlled by corrupt politicians and incompetent generals. Despite a withered arm, which was almost useless, Heureaux developed into a good soldier, and soon rose from the ranks to an under officership. His genius for organization developed, and by rapid promotions the young officer advanced until he became commander-in-chief. Then came the turbulent days of 1883-84, and in the disorder of the time Heureaux found his opportunity. With his power as head of the army, he forced his election as president, and thenceforth, to the hour of his death, despite dozens of desperate attempts to displace him, he firmly maintained his position.

His political career abounded in stirring episodes. He established an elaborate secret service and through it his life was saved many times. Many of his spies were women, and through them he was able, time and again, to crush incipient rebellions and stamp out plots against his life. He administered himself "the high justice, the middle and the low," and the luckless plotter found in him judge and jury. When he deemed it unwise to order a military execution, the victim of his displeasure met his fate at home or in a dark street at the hands of one of the President's secret agents.

The visitor to Santo Domingo, the capital of the republic, found evidence of the terror-inspiring character of Heureaux' rule on every hand. From the moment of landing the footsteps of the tourist were dogged night and day by detectives. The residents were shy in their intercourse with strangers, and it was almost impossible to secure from them expressions of opinion on any subject touching the president or his policy. When it was possible, however, to find a Dominican who was willing to talk of the life of Heureaux, the stories told had the flavor of old-time romance. One of the striking instances of the president's personal courage is to be found in the story of his supression of the "Santiago" rebellion a few years ago. Santiago is inland from the famous Samana bay, and at a distance from the capital which made it possible for the residents to foment trouble with the possibility of escaping immediate punishment. Here a band of malcontents from all parts of the island gathered and soon succeeded in enlisting the sympathies of almost the entire population of the town. Encouraged by the fact that the terrible president was several days' journey distant, the ringleaders finally became bold enough to issue a manifesto declaring Heureaux a monstrous tyrant, and urging an uprising. Great posters were printed bearing the picture of a coffin and calling on all patriots to aid in ridding the fatherland of its oppressor.

The government secret service soon apprised the president of the affair, and in his usual picturesque manner he proceeded with preparations for revenge. He ordered made a rough, black coffin, and putting it aboard his little armored yacht, he took a detail of soldiers and sailed around the coast for Santiago. Landing with thirty soldiers, he started on the twenty-five-mile horseback journey inland, and, arriving within a few miles of the town, halted his company and spent the night. Just before dawn, with two soldiers bearing the coffin, he entered the city, leaving the troops behind. The town bugler was aroused, and, at the point of the bayonet, was compelled to sound the alarm, which, in Dominican cities, calls the residents together. Half-dressed and heavy with sleep, the townspeople rushed to the little palm-shaded plaza, and there, all alone under a great tree, upon which was nailed one of the revolutionary posters, the president stood, his arms folded across his uniformed breast. Terrified, the people huddled together. Heureaux called to the bearers to bring the coffin and place it at his side. As the rapidly coming day revealed the faces of the people before him, the president turned to the manifesto and read it aloud.

"Well," he said, as he paused at conclusion; "well; why do you not act? Here is the tyrant. Here is the coffin. Why do you not make good your glorious purpose? Is there no man here? No; half a man?"

Then he burst into a tirade of abuse which reduced his hearers to the last stage of terror. Finally, he strode towards the crowd and pointed out man after man of those whom his spies had declared to be ringleaders.

"Stand out there," he cried. "You—and you—and you," and when he had finished eighteen wretches stood in line awaiting their doom.

"Now march," he ordered, and out through the gates of the city the eighteen men went, the two soldiers with the coffin at their head, and of all the friends and neighbors who saw them go, not one dared raise a finger to oppose the black warrior who stalked behind. When the camp was reached the conspirators were placed in line opposite a file of soldiers. Ten minutes later Heureaux and his soldiers had gone, and eighteen dead men were all that remained of the Santiago rebellion.

Heureaux's heartlessness in the discharge of what he believed to be his duty was never better shown than in the execution, for conspiracy, of his brother-in-law. With the president lived a half sister, much younger than he, and the one person in the world for whom he seemed to have a real affection. The girl met and married a young Dominican merchant. Heureaux soon took the young man into the government service and advanced him rapidly. But the young official was soon deep in the councils of a group of conspirators who had for their object the assassination of the president and the overthrow of the government. The all-wise secret service soon knew all about it, and Heureaux, for his sister's sake, contented himself with warning his brother-in-law that a repetition of his offense would cost him his life. The man's treachery was kept from the public and he was allowed to retain his government position on promise of future good behavior. In less than six months, however, he was again at work plotting against his chief's life, and again his guilt was reported to the president. Heureaux carefully concealed his knowledge of the treachery and issued orders for a state breakfast at his palace. With the noon hour officials of army and state gathered, and with them came the young conspirator. He arrived without his uniform, and Heureaux ordered him to go and don it.

"This breakfast is in your honor," he said, "and I wish you to appear as you should." When the breakfast party was seated at the table the young man was given the place of honor at the president's right, and the officials and their wives wondered at the great show of favor. As the repast was finished and the people rose, the president turned to his brother-in-law and asked if he had enjoyed himself.

"I have, indeed, your excellency," the young man replied.

"I hope you have," returned Heureaux, still smiling, "for you are going to be shot in five minutes." He touched a bell and two black soldiers entered and seized the doomed official. The momentary silence was broken by the screams and frantic pleadings of the president's sister, but he ignored her and calmly led the way down the great flagged stairway and out into the shade of the tropical foliage of the court. The young man met his end bravely, refusing to open his lips as he was led beneath the window of the banquet room and faced about before a file of soldiers. Heureaux, with his sister's screams ringing from the windows above him, coolly gave the order to fire, and stood by, with his cigarette, while the uncoffined body was quickly buried in a flower garden.

Several months after this incident the president received word one stormy evening that a number of conspirators were meeting in a deserted house a mile outside the city to devise a plan for the ruler's assassination. Heureaux ordered a horse and started alone for the house. He dismounted a short distance away, crept up to the door, and suddenly forced an entrance. As he strode among them the score or so of men, who had even then been swearing to take his life, quailed as though before a fully equipped battery.

"I wished to show you," Heureaux said, "that you are not nearly so anxious for my life as you believed. I know you all but I shall take no action. You are free." And he turned and went away. He kept his word and none of the conspirators was punished, but the majority of them left the country.

Heureaux had the features of a full-blooded negro. His manner was polished and his conversation entertaining. The aristocratic Dominicans, who are of pure Spanish blood, refused to admit him to their social circles because of his negro blood, and he felt their slight keenly. Little was known of his family history or his parentage, and he was always reticent about the incidents of his life before he became famous. His palace in Santo Domingo is a three-story building covering a block, and built in the typical style of the tropics. Its plaster walls and green window slats are all that can be seen by the tourist.—Chicago Tribune.
