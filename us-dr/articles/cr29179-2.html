WEST INDIAN ANNEXATION.

It is evident from the indications of the Northern votes in Congress, on the question of acquiring West Indian territories, that the Radical reconstructors regard the job which they now have on hand as quite big enough. And, indeed, it is, judging from the pattern on which they are working it out. These philosophers, statesmen, reformers, regenerators, having undertaken to annex the American Union to a confederation of carpet-bag colonies and negro communities in territory once occupied by the Southern portion of the United States, have no room in their spacious intellects for the entertainment of any other schemes of annexation while this one is pending. And this one is in its nature endless; or, at least, we shall see water flowing up hill to find its level, and a pyramid immovably balanced on its apex, just about as soon as shall see the Radicals complete to their own satisfaction the work of identifying republican government with despotism, and freedom with compulsion.

But it is also indicated in various ways that the people of this country begin to tire of paying for the prosecution of this little job, which consumes all their resources in the impossible problem of undoing the dispensations of the Almighty and establishing the identity of contradictions. They are at length becoming aware that the Radical idea of annexation is, first to break the constitutional bonds of the Union, then to desolate the fairest portions of its territory, then to erect on the ruins spurious States composed of a mixture of prowling white adventurers and the most vicious elements of the native blacks, and finally to bind the rest of the country to this infectious mass of political and social rottenness. They perceive that, while Radical policy is detrimental to all the producing interests within the present limits of the United States, it has not acquired one foot of new productive territory or extended in any direction the area of American commerce.

The shrewder men of the Republican party are warned by this complexion of the popular mind that the party cannot be saved if they allow it to be dragged henceforth in the rut of Radical policy. And it would seem that one of the expedients by which they hope to get out of this rut is the inauguration of some process of West Indian annexation. The subjoined resolution, introduced into Congress by Mr. Orth, of Indiana, from the committee of foreign affairs, is regarded at Washington as an experimental move in this direction:

Resolved by the Senate and House of Representatives, That the territory belonging to the Dominican Republic shall, on the application of the government and the people of said republic, be admitted into the Union as a territory of the United States, to be called the Territory of St. Domingo, on the conditions and in the manner following:

First. The people of said republic shall adopt a republican form of territorial government, by delegates in convention assembled for that purpose.

Second. The action by the people shall be with the consent and operation of the existing government of said republic.

Third. Such form of government shall thereafter be submitted to Congress for its approval.

Fourth. The admission of said territory shall be with a view to the ultimate establishment of a State government, republican in form, in and over said territory, in conformity to the Constitution of the United States, with the approval of Congress.

The motion to lay this resolution on the table was carried by 110 ayes to 62 noes. Mr. Orth was not dissatisfied with the result of the vote. He said that the proposition received a larger support than he expected, and announced that it would be brought up again in a different form, accompanied by important information which, aided by more mature reflection on the part of members, would, he hoped, determine a majority of the House in its favor. The information to which he refers is presumed to consist of official proofs of overtures from the Dominican government for the annexation of its territory to the United States, and of statistical exhibits of the richness of this territory in mineral and agricultural resources. And still more is hinted at. It is intimated that evidence may be produced to show that the populations of the most desirable of the West India islands, including Cuba and Porto Rico, are eager to solve the troublous and, at present gloomy, problem of their destiny, by annexation to the United States.

It is not to be supposed that, if the Dominican republic were annexed, Hayti would be content to remain, or would, in any case, long remain outside of American jurisdiction. Dominica occupies the larger part of the territory, and contains the greater portion of the population of the island of St. Domingo, of which the whole area is 28,000 square miles, and the whole population 1,250,000. Next to Cuba, in point of population, this island is also, next to Cuba, the most beautiful and productive of the West Indies. And the value to American commerce of the trade which would be developed by the opening of this island to American enterprise by annexation, is only less than that which would result from the annexation of Cuba. We have no idea that any definite programme for these ends will be carried in the present Congress, but we do expect it will be a subject of increasing agitation during the incoming administration. The commercial interests of the great West are waking up to the large benefits which they would realize from West Indian annexation, and public opinion in general is shaping itself in favor of a comprehensive policy looking to the ultimate, and not distant, acquisition of all the groups in the West Indian archipelago. Those Radical politicians whose ambition is concentrated in a system of despotism and plunder, which depends upon their maintaining, with continuous aggravations, the present abnormal condition of the country, will, of course, oppose the introduction of new elements which may interfere with their present calculations. Burrowing in their peculiar schemes, they are as blind as moles. Their minds are contracted to what they work in. The sun blinds them. They shrink from the light of the blue sky, arching from horizon to horizon, is a fearful burden to them. They abhor anything like real movement, actual expansion, solid and palpable accessions to the country's greatness and prosperity, for all such things are utterly destructive to their political education, to their mental habits and moral bent.
