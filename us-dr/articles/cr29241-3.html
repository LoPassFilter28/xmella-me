An account in the Daily Kennebec Journal reports observations from the San Domingo commission, noting that the commissioners found the island remarkably rich, with soil more fertile than neighboring islands and vast inland tracts of valuable mahogany and timber. The report explains that the commissioners consider it their duty to gather facts rather than argue for or against annexation, allowing the public to draw its own conclusions. In response to a serenade by Dominican citizens, Commissioner Dr. Samuel Gridley Howe delivered an address describing how the island's enchanting scenery, balmy climate, luscious fruits, and music captivated his senses, offering a bright contrast to local grievances regarding civil wars and political oppression.Una reseña en el Daily Kennebec Journal recoge impresiones de la comisión de Santo Domingo, indicando que los comisionados hallaron la isla sumamente rica, con un suelo más fértil que el de las islas vecinas y extensos bosques interiores de caoba y maderas de gran valor. La nota explica que los comisionados consideran que su deber es recabar hechos y no abogar en favor o en contra de la anexión, dejando que el público extraiga sus propias conclusiones. En respuesta a una serenata ofrecida por ciudadanos dominicanos, el comisionado Dr. Samuel Gridley Howe pronunció un discurso en el que relató cómo los fascinantes paisajes, el clima benigno, las exquisitas frutas y la música cautivaron sus sentidos, en grato contraste con las quejas locales por las guerras civiles y la opresión política.

Daily Kennebec Journal · March 13, 187113 de marzo de 1871

Original in English.Original en inglés.

In a conversation with a correspondent at St. Domingo of one of our papers Mr. Wade is reported as making the following remarks:

The President, by authority of Congress, sent us down here to investigate the condition of the country. We have pursued that investigation as fully and earnestly as possible. First of all, we find Baez President of this republic de facto—it is not our duty to say or to inquire whether he is the de jure President or not. He is in peaceful possession of it, directing its army such as it is; controlling the finances, such as they are; performing all functions of government. Then we find that the people are all in favor of annexation; that a vote was taken on the question twelve months ago; that the people were then unanimously in favor of it, and are more vehement and enthusiastic in their desire to be annexed to-day than they were then; that its debt will be fully covered by one million and a half of dollars; that the country is a healthy country; that concessions of land were made to a railroad company, which by the non-performance of the contract, are utterly worthless; that a contract was entered into by the government with a company in New York to survey the whole territory, in consideration of which it was stipulated that one-fifth of the public lands should be given to them, and,—the contract is not very clear on this point—one-fifth of the mineral wealth—but we did not come to adjudicate upon that. We find that San Domingo is rich beyond all comparison—rich and more fertile in soil than any of the surrounding islands; that in the interior are large mahogany tracts, of immense value, and immense quantities of valuable kinds of timber.

From the other two commissioners, Mr. White and Dr. Howe, not much has been heard. The commissioners understand that their business is to report facts and allow others to draw their own conclusions, and hence they have nothing to say for or against annexation. To a party of Dominicans who serenaded the commissioners Dr. Howe made a short speech, in which he dwelt upon the delights of the climate as follows:

People too often overlook or forget the good things they have and think only of their sufferings and wants. Since I have been here, people have been talking continually to me about the disadvantages under which they labor—of the convulsions, and civil wars, and oppressions, and all that. There is another side of the picture. I find myself in danger of having my reason carried away by senses. All my senses are, as it were subjugated by the surroundings. I find the most beautiful island I have ever seen. Tke balmy atmosphere, the mind and even temperature—everything addresses itself to my sense de bien etre. My eyes are dazzled by the beauty of enchanting scenery. My sense of taste is gratified by the luscious and abundant fruits that are everywhere around us; and now comes your music, and so charms away my sense of hearing that I find all my senses enlisted and carried away in your favor; and although I may not say what I shall advise my countrymen to do on the subject of annexing the Domican republic, I feel inclined to annex myself and my family to this beautiful island.

The full report will be looked for with a good deal of interest. Should it speak favorably of the island and the people there will still remain a question whether annexation is desirable and best under all the circumstances, but by all means let us hear from the commissioners, and obtain all the light possible before we pass judgement. The annexation of Louisana and subsequently of Texas were bitterly opposed, but after they were annexed we would not part with them and kept them in the Union with the bayonet. Although at the far southern extemity of the Union there were more Unionists in those States during the rebellion than in Virginia. Let us wait patiently and learn what the commissioners have to say.
