Rival Dominican Groups Converge on Capitol Hill Anti-Trujillo Speech in House Draws Demonstrators; Police Tighten Guard

Extra police precautions were taken today to keep apart two rival groups of Dominicans who converged on the Capitol as Representative Porter, Democrat of Oregon asked for a congressional investigation into the disappearance of an American aviator in the Dominican Republic.

Both factions hoped to get into the House of Representatives during the speech, but Capitol Police said nobody would be permitted in the galleries without careful screening.

The guided tours through the Capitol building were ordered to bypass the House visitor's galleries.

Bus loads of tourists still were admitted to the rest of the building, however, and guides showed them through other areas. Individuals bearing passes issued by their Representatives were being admitted to the House galleries where an undisclosed number of plain clothesmen were stationed.

Puerto Ricans Recalled

"We don't want a repetition of the Puerto Rico thing," said Capitol Police Chief Robert Pierce. He referred to the shooting of five Representatives on the floor by a band of four fanatic Puerto Ricans three years ago tomorrow.

Mr. Porter took the floor shortly after noon to ask for an inquiry into the death of one of his constituents, Gerald Lester Murphey, the young flyer who disappeared in the Dominican Republic.

The bizarre case came to light with a story in Life magazine which said the pilot had been hired to take Jesus Maria de Galindez, Columbia University faculty member and outspoken foe of Generalissimo Rafael Trujillo, to the Dominican Republic.

In his speech today Mr. Porter traced the thread of the story in which Murphy supposedly took the kidnaped Galindez, drugged and disguised as an ambulance patient, to the Central American republic, where he was later killed.

When Murphy later disappeared, Dominican authorities said he had been pushed off a cliff by a Dominican aviator, Octavio De La Maza, during a quarrel. The latter then killed himself from remorse in a jail cell, the Dominican authorities insisted.

Mr. Porter told the House today he had conducted his own investigation. He analyzed a "suicide note" supposedly left by De La Maza to throw doubt on its authenticity.

Mr. Porter called for economic measures against Trujillo, such as imposition of a ban on tourist trade and suspension of imports from the country.

"Clearly the time is long overdue to demonstrate that the United States will not tolerate Trujillo's abuses," he said.

The Representative also asked whether the United States should not "revise our present official policy of toleration, conciliation and condonation of the Dominican tyranny so that no more American boys will disappear in the Dominican Republic under circumstances which not only suggest murder, but indeed shriek of murder."

Extra Guards Called

As the two groups of Dominicans, one supporting Trujillo and the other opposing him, began demonstrations, extra guards were stationed inside and outside the Capitol.

Demonstrations on the Capitol grounds are not permitted, but a group of 130 pro-Trujillo people, arriving in three buses from New York, were driven past the Capitol.

Four policemen were assigned to accompany them. They remained orderly, and at noon were taken to the White House where they paraded up and down with signs.

Earlier in the day, they were received by the Dominican Ambassador. The Ambassador said some of the visitors were friends he had met in New York while he was consul general here. He said they were here to express their solidarity with the Dominican government.

The anti-Trujillo group was made up of about 40 persons, calling themselves the Dominican Revolutionary Party.

Their leader, Nicolas Silfa, said the party expected to demonstrate in orderly fashion with a black coffin representing the "murders" of two Americans in less than a year by Trujillo.
