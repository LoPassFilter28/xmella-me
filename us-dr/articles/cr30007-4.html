Original in English.Original en inglés.

It is hinted that frauds against the government are still possible in the matter of the expenditure of the money turned over to it, and the construction of the new boulevard that stretches west from the city seventeen miles along the coast, at a cost of $20,000 per mile, is cited as an instance where the government did not drive a very hard bargain. However, the boulevard is a splendid improvement, affording a delightful drive and has encouraged the use of automobiles and some thirty-five cars are now owned in Santo Domingo. The government palace has been rebuilt into an excellent structure, and this, with the boulevard and other improvements, has been made possible through American financial supervision.

The weak spot in the convention, according to some who took the occasion of Knox's visit to express their views, it that the American control is limited to the custom houses. There remain the internal dues, stamp taxes, liquor taxes and consumption taxes, which if honestly administered, should return about $2,000,000 annually. These, which amount to about 25 per cent of the total income of the government, continue under the control of the Dominican officials and furnish a loophole for the revolutionists, of whom there are always a few at least.

The theory of the American state department is that revolutions cannot thrive unless the custom houses fall into the hands of the disturbers. Remove the sinews of war or make their seizure impossible because of American control and the disturber is helpless. Peace and prosperity follow logically.

The Americans, apparently, were so anxious to conclude the financial arrangement with the Dominican republic that they took what they could get at the moment. Now it is a question whether the internal revenues are not sufficient to stake a revolutionary movement. It was pointed out that there is nothing to prevent a rebellious leader pledging these incomes for a loan with which to finance a revolution. If the pledge is made to interests inimical to the United States or to bankers whose governments are not in sympathy with the Monroe Doctrine, so much the worse. That something of the sort is now brewing is one of the stories that the visitor to the Dominican republic is told.

President Victoria is not popular and the government is admitted to be weak. When President Caceres was shot to death, Alfred Victoria, who, at the age of 27 was the commander of the army, assumed control of affairs. He summoned the members of the council of ministers to him one at a time and forced the election of his uncle as president. He made himself minister of war, and having the support of the army, has since ruled according to common report, his uncle being little more than a figurehead. The relatives of the two Victorias have been well taken care of in minor offices, and this has given rise to the prevailing complaint of Nepotism.

The assassination of Caceres is attributed by many to hatred of Frederico Velasquez, who, as minister of finance, dominated the Caceres government. Often appealed to to remove Velasquez, Caceres would reply: "I will go down with him, first." Since the assassination, Velasquez has kept out of the country and until a few days ago was in St. Thomas.

Among the guests at American Ministers Russell's reception for Mr. Knox at the legation was the Archbishop of Santo Domingo, who has the distinction of being the first archbishop in the western hemisphere, as his bishopric was the first to be established in the new world. He wore a tiny American flag on his breast and a cap ribbon, reading "U. S. S. Washington." on his arm.

Former President C. F. Morales is one of the political prisoners held at the capital. Recently there is said to have been a mutiny at the barracks and 16 soldiers were shot. The most hopeful view of the present situation is found in the history of former revolutions, for it is said that these movements had been successful usually within three weeks, if at all. The latest disturbance has continued for two months without threatening the capital itself.
