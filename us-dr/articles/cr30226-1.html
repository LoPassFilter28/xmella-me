Trujillo, Man of Steel Dominican President Calm During Strife.

By EDMUND CHESTER.

SAN CRISTOBAL, Dominican Republic (Correspondence of the Associated Press).—In the midst of a war scare that has sent the people of Haiti and the Dominican Republic into a state of near hysteria, Rafael L. Trujillo, doctor, generalissimo, president of the Dominican Republic and strong man of the West Indies, stands calm and serene.

He appears to have the normal interest of a leader whose people have been accused of killing 8,000 aliens in two months. He is a man of steel. He is a man of discipline. He applies that discipline to his own life as rigidly as he does to his 1,500,000 people.

For the last seven years the name of Trujillo has been creeping into the news of the world. He has become a topic of conversation throughout the West Indies and in the United States.

It is easy, from long range, to visualize President Trujillo as a tyrant, but it is difficult to retain that opinion once you meet and talk with the man. His method of ruling may be subject to debate, but there is no argument about the personality and power of this farm boy who lifted himself to the head of his country by his unusual driving force.

Nor can his firmness be doubted.

"Good Offices" Accepted.

He took a stand of "no outside help wanted" in the controversy with Haiti, but later agreed to accept "good offices" of United States, Mexican and Cuban arbitrators.

(The Dominican strong man came to power in the 1930 revolution and in an ensuing election was named President, a post he has held uninterruptedly since. Many of Trujillo's political enemies went into exile after his election and a strict press censorship was imposed.)

Seated on the porch of his farm house here, 20 miles west of Ciudad Trujillo, the nation's capital, President Trujillo preferred to talk about his 2,500 head of Holstein cattle or about the public works program of his government than about the charges that his people were killing Haitians in the Dominican Republic in "an explosion of bloody and needless hate."

"This thing is a purely local incident and our judiciary power is investigating it," he said.

"Must Show No Fear."

Switching the conversation from war to political philosophy, the handsome ruler said: "A man in my position must have no fear, personal or otherwise. He must have courage for the sake of his country. If he shows fear, he is lost, and his people are lost with him."

President Trujillo receives strangers rather coolly until he knows something of their mission. Then he warms up and talks to his callers as though they were old friends.

Of medium height, he has a strong face, whose white skin has been tanned by years of work in the tropical sun, first as a farmer, then as a soldier, and again as a soldier. He has dark brown eyes, steel gray hair and is immaculate in white linen riding breeches and boots. His culture cannot be doubted, and his manners are letter perfect. He thinks quickly and speaks with a firmness that marks him as a leader of men.

Born in this little village 46 years ago, the son of a wealthy farmer, he quit school in his high school years and went into the farming business. He loved books and spent his evenings reading and studying.

Joined National Guard.

In 1917 he became interested in the military and joined the National Guard of the Dominican Republic, then under jurisdiction of the United States Marine Corps. He was commissioned a second lieutenant and became a favorite of the United States Marine officers in the country.

Promotion was rapid, and he became commander in chief of the armed forces. Seven years ago he reached the presidency and has held it ever since. He says the American marines taught him much, and he is proud of the fact that he still carries on personal correspondence with many marine officers.

Next year probably he will be re-elected for a third term to serve at least four more years. The campaign for his re-election is under way now, and, with unusual frankness, the 1938 election is called "the re-election" in all posters and demonstrations.
