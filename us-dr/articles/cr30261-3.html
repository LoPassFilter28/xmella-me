Shifting San Domingo. The Santo Domingo Improvement Co's Affairs. Anent the Revolution. This Company Runs All of the Finances of the Island. Collects Its Debts and Disburses Its Revenues. Is the Sponsor for the Debt and Pays Its Interest and Provides a Sinking Fund—Owns the Railways in Large Part and Is It—It Was a Heureaux Arrangement and May Not Be Accepted by the Revolutionists

New York, September 1.—The Santo Domingo Improvement Co., whose offices are in this city, express no concern over the revolution in Santo Domingo. Vice President Charles Wells said he had perfect confidence that the contract between the company and existing government would be respected by the new authorities. He intimated that the power of the United States government would be exerted if necessary to that end. Since 1893 the Santo Domingo Improvement Co., by virtue of a contract with the Heureux administration has exercised a large measure of control over Dominican affairs. By virtue of this contract it became the agent for payment of the Dominican debt, amounting to £4,345,000 the principal and interest being payable in gold in London. The rate of interest on the Dominican bonds varies from 2¾ to 4 per cent and the company has established a sinking fund for the payment of the principal of the obligations, some of it with nearly a 100 years to run. According to a prospectus issued by the company, the revenues of the government in 1892, the year before the contract became operative, amounted to $1,306,000 the Mexican silver dollar being the standard. Since then there has been a steady increase and in 1898, the income amounted to $3,400,000. All of the revenues are collected by the company which turns over $15,000 a week to the native authorities for the support of the government, retaining the remainder for expenses, interests and the sinking fund. Of course the company hopes to reap a large profit from the arrangement.

"Besides this direct relation with the government," said Vice President Wells, "our company has other important interests in Santo Domingo. We own the principal railway in the country which now runs from Puerto Plata on the seacoast, to the interior city of Santiago, a distance of about 50 miles. We now have a force of 500 men engaged in building an extension of the road from Santiago to Mosa, about 20 miles in length, and when this is accepted, we expect to extend the lines 20 miles further to Marcori, where it will connect with the only other railway. The latter is about 50 miles long, extending from Samana bay to La Vega. It is owned by the Bairds, of Glasgow, who are anxious to dispose of it to us. Our road runs through a mountainous country which presented difficulties too great for the Belgian engineers, who built it for the German company from which we purchased it. We are now rebuilding several miles of the original line, in order to do away with the steep grades which have interfered largely with its operations.

"The commercial affairs of the country are largely in the hands of German firms, but there are important American interests besides those controlled by our company. Many of the largest sugar and banana plantations are owned by Americans, the planters living in the island in the winter months and coming to the United States in the summer. We have no fear that any American interests will be hurt by the change of government. The United States has just loaned the services of an excellent custom officer to take charge of the customhouse at one of the principal Dominican seaports. We expect to employ Americans at the other ports as soon as we can obtain the right men. We have a dozen representatives, mostly Americans, residing in the principal towns of the country. They have informed us that the insurrection has not thus far interfered with our interests or with the general prosperity of the island. This revolution has not been a bloody affair. In fact, in the only battle of serious magnitude which has been reported the insurgents were defeated by the government forces. But there appears to be a general desire for a change, mainly due to the currency question which has been the cause of popular upheavals in the United States and other countries. It is singular, but true, that the prosperity of the country was the real cause of the currency trouble. The banking facilities are inadequate and the people are in the habit of hoarding any money which comes into their possession. When times began to improve, there was a shortage of silver coin with which to do business and President Heureux, who was not the corrupt tyrant he has been painted, decided on an issue of paper currency to meet the demand. The foreign firms started a campaign to debase this currency and succeeded better than they expected, so well that they ruined themselves and involved the country in the present trouble.
