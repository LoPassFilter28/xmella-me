Long Way Around, From East to West Coast Can Become Caribbean Island 'Dream Trip'

NEW YORK, Dec. 11.—A straight line may be the shortest route between two points—but it's not necessarily the most interesting.

By forgetting the old axiom, that Coast-to-Coast vacation or business trip you've been planning to California can be combined with a delightful tour of a half dozen Latin American playgrounds in a minimum of time—and at a minimum of expense.

Such a "dream trip" need not adhere to any fixed itinerary, for stop-overs can be made at no extra transportation cost. Multi - daily Clipper service by Pan American World Airways makes it possible to tailor the travel program as you go along and to linger longer than you have originally planned at spots which particularly strike your fancy.

Six Countries En Route.

Following what the airlines call an "open jaw" circuit, you can visit Puerto Rico, the Dominican Republic, Haiti, Cuba, Mexico and Guatemala en route from New York to Los Angeles—and your total expenditure, if your on-the-ground tastes are not too elaborate, will be in the neighborhood of only $500.

You begin the luxury air cruise with a Clipper flight to San Juan, Puerto Rico, linked to New York by daily nonstop service.

In spite of many American trappings, Uncle Sam's tropical island is as Spanish as garlic and olive oil. The Spanish language predominates, although English is widely spoken.

Colonized almost a half century before St. Augustine, Fla., San Juan is filled with Spanish colonial buildings, churches and forts. Among the most interesting are La Fortaleza, originally built as a fort in 1533 and now the official residence of the governor; El Morro, grim guardian of the bay, whose massive walls, moats and dungeons are as solid as when it was built by the Spaniards in 1539, and the cathedral where Ponce de Leon, colonizer of Puerto Rico, is buried.

Your air cruise continues with a visit to Ciudad Trujillo, capital of the Dominican Republic and oldest city in the Western Hemisphere. The PAA flight from San Juan takes only an hour and 45 minutes.

Like Americans.

Dominicans are among the friendliest people in Latin America, and they sincerely like Americans. In more ways than one you feel their welcome—both official and unofficial.

When you reach the chalk-white Hotel Jaragua, you realize the Dominican government was thinking in terms of American tastes when it built this lavish structure. It looks like something straight out of Hollywood.

It has innumerable tiled terraces. On one, encircled by palm trees, there is dancing nightly. On another you may take a sun bath. On still another you may dine facing the garden and swimming pool.

When you sally forth to see Ciudad Trujillo, it isn't long before you feel the unofficial welcome of the people. American travelers are not treated by merchants as easy targets. If you want to buy, "muy bien," they say. If you don't, that's all right.

Next logical stop on your tour is mysteriously enchanting Haiti, which shares the island of Hispaniola with the Dominican Republic and is only 85 minutes away, as your Clipper flies.

Both Africa and France.

Haiti is the only Negro republic in the Western Hemisphere. French in heritage and in language, it is an extraordinary blend of Africa and France. In Port-au-Prince, the capital, you encounter both the cultured elite, educated in France or the United States, many of whom have French blood in their veins, and the pure African peasants—simple folk whose hospitality you sense even though you cannot understand their patois.

In one of the planes of the Haitian Air Force you can fly from Port-au-Prince across the mountains to the north coast and explore the Citadel, a gargantuan fortress atop a mountain peak, which was built by an ex-slave who became a revolutionary leader, president and finally king of Haiti in the 19th century. This fabulous fortress was constructed at the cost of thousands of human lives.

Cuba, only two and a half flight hours away from Haiti, is a perennial favorite of United States vacationers.

The magic of the Cuban capital, which irresistibly drew Spanish adventurers 400 years ago, remains undiluted and largely unchanged. War-born prosperity, however, has created a hum of activity where before life was slow-paced.

Still a European City.

In spite of the quickened tempo and progressive air, none of the city's special flavor has been lost. Havana is still a European city.

The Cuban capital's night life is famous the world over. No one—natives or tourists—would think of going to bed before the wee hours of the morning. There is far too much to see at de luxe night clubs and at tawdry, riotous water-front cabarets.

Fast, nonstop Clipper service from Havana puts you in Merida, on the Yucatan peninsula, within two hours.

The Yucatan is a land of contrasts. The airport terminal is as up-to-date as any in the United States, with an excellent restaurant and bar and striking murals of native scenes by the well-known Mexican artist, J. L. Vergara.

Only 15 minutes from the airport is the heart of the city.

Guatemala, one of the most colorful of the Central American picturebook republics, can be reached from Merida in only two hours.

Capital Clean, Modern.

Guatemala City, the capital, is a clean, modern city with an aspect of newness. This stems from the fact that earthquakes in 1917 and 1918 destroyed most of the older structures. Much of the city has been rebuilt since that time.

Although it is not a cosmopolitan capital like Havana, Guatemala City has a number of excellent restaurants and gay night clubs where marimba orchestras play Latin and North American music for dancing. The two best known clubs, Ciro's and Casablanca, provide elaborate floor shows and U. S.-style dance bands for their tourists and upper-class Guatemalteco clientele.

Guatemala offers many bargains to shoppers. You can buy long silver necklaces called wedding chains; earrings in the form of wire baskets; hammered silver cases in which a standard United States lighter fits; tooled leather purses, suit cases and hand-woven cotton and woolen textiles famous for their durability and colorful designs.

Lavish Night Clubs.

Next on the "open jaw" circuit is Mexico City, oldest metropolis on the American continent. A modern city of almost 2,000,000 inhabitants, it has lavish night clubs, excellent restaurants, smart shops and good theaters. Bullfights are staged the year-around.

Relics of Mexico's former rulers—the Aztecs and the Spaniards—constitute the principal sightseeing objectives. The National Museum contains some of the most prized archaeological treasures in the world. Among them is the Aztec Calendar, which scholars believe was sculptured in 1419 and which summarizes the astronomical knowledge of pre-conquest Mexico.

There are many all-day excursions from Mexico City. Among them are those to the floating gardens of Xochimilco, to the pyramids of San Juan Teotihuacan and to Cuernavaca, once the summer residence of Maximilian and Carlota, who governed the land briefly in the 19th century.

The trip to Los Angeles can be completed from Mexico City by daily nonstop flights of Compania Mexicana de Aviacion, an affiliate of Pan American.
