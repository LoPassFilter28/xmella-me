DREW PEARSON

WASHINGTON—When Dwight D. Eisenhower of America and Jawaharlal Nehru of India sat down together Sunday they were the embodiment of Rudyard Kipling's famous lines: "East is East and West is West and never the twain shall meet, till . . . two strong men stand face to face, though they come from the ends of the earth!"

The two strong men face the problem of bringing two opposite parts of the world together. And no two men could be more different in taste, experience, outlook. In real fact they do come from the ends of the earth.

Nehru spent his life in passive resistance. He has never held a gun in his hand. He bucked the power of the world's then mightiest empire, Britain, to win independence for India. It was the most notable achievement of passive resistance in the history of the world.

The man he sat opposite spent 40 years as a military man. His earliest training was to drill with a rifle over his shoulder. All his life he has relied on force. His mother was a devoted pacifist, a member of Jehovah's Witnesses. Her son went on to lead the world's greatest invasion armada, became commander of the world's most powerful, victorious army.

The thin little man who dreamed dreams of Indian independence has spent 13 years in jail. That was where he had so much time to dream dreams. Today he is not bitter toward his British jailers. He has kept his country a part of the British Empire.

* * *

NO GOLFER HE

"I am not prepared to say that the many years I have spent in jail have been the sweetest of my life," says Nehru with an ironic touch of humor. "I have been a dabbler at many things. I began with science in college, then took to law, and after developing various other interests in life, finally adopted the popular and widely practiced profession of jail-going."

The man from the west who is trying to bring east and west together has never dabbled. He has never wavered from the military career begun in 1911 at West Point. He is an outdoor man. He chafes at confinement, spends long hours on the golf course, long vacations fishing.

Nehru has never had a golf stick in his hand. When in jail his only exercise was 30 minutes in the early morning. "In order to compress as much open-air exercise as I could do in the short time at my disposal," says Nehru, "I took to running, and gradually increased this to over two miles daily."

The rest of the time in jail he spent reading and thinking. He studied Marx, discarded Marx, became a strong anti-Communist. His British jailers recommended that he read religion. They didn't want him to read books on politics.

"It is wonderful how dear to the heart of the British Government is the subject of religion," says Nehru, "and how impartially it encourages all brands of it." The man who will talk to Nehru over the week end has read few books outside military textbooks and, more recently, westerns. He has not dabbled in political theory or religion. He says frankly: "I prop myself up on the pillows at night to read government reports and then in about 20 minutes I doze off. Mamie comes in and turns out the light, and I sleep like a baby."

* * *

HOPE FOR RUSSIA

Nehru is an internationalist of the type hated by the old guard of the President's party. He has been sharply critical of the dictators to whom the other man has given blessing and support—Franco of Spain, Chiang Kai-shek of Formosa, Syngman Rhee of Korea, Trujillo of the Dominican Republic.

He has not been so critical of Russia, yet he has consistently fought Communism. He sees Russia as emerging from the despotism of czarism, which he considered something so bad that it must never go back. He thinks the despotism of Communism just as bad, but predicts that time will cure the Soviet's evils.

"Nothing in the world that is alive remains unchanging," says the man who waited 13 years in jail for the freedom of India. "All nature changes from day to day and from minute to minute. Fresh water runs on, and if you stop it, it becomes stagnant. So also it is with the life of a man and the life of a nation."

Here Jawaharlal Nehru and Dwight D. Eisenhower begin to approach each other. Eisenhower, himself an internationalist, himself a hater of Communism has never hated Russia as a group of people.

Both are brilliant, sometimes exhausting conversationalists. Both, according to some of their friends, lack terminal facilities. Both are genuinely concerned about the peace of the world. Eisenhower has come part way round to the Nehru view that neutralist Asia holds part of the key to peace.

NEW "POINT 5" PROGRAM

A new approach to the free world's recurrent dollar crisis has been tossed up for discussion by Ralph T. Reed, president of the American Express Company—namely, more travel abroad by Americans.

Actually it's erroneous to figure that western Europe's dollar problem will be solved by reopening of the Suez Canal in a few months. In England a new dollar crisis was approaching long before the Egyptian adventure diverted public attention. Sterling reserves had dropped by a whopping $288,000,000 in 1955.

Meanwhile, the Soviet Union has been winning new friends with a mammoth program of foreign aid while the U.S. has stood by helplessly.

What the free world needs, says Reed, is a "Point Five" program to promote foreign travel by Americans in ever-increasing numbers. Dollars spent abroad in this way constitutes economic assistance without strings. At the same time, the American citizen gets something for his dollar, in contrast to what he pays in taxes to the government for doling out overseas grants.

Travel by Americans is already one of the free world's biggest dollar earners. Last year Americans spent $1,500,000,000 to go abroad. But Reed says the expenditure could double in the next 10 years if given the proper encouragement. The big jet transport planes due in 1959 will help by bringing Europe within six hours of New York. So will the forthcoming non-luxury ocean liners equipped with simple accommodations and cafeteria meals—at low cost.

"Point Five" is most needed in Asia where the free nations are now receiving the lion's share of Point Four aid, but with relatively few dollars coming in from American tourists.

If Reed is right, new hotels may soon prove as vital as steel mills in many foreign countries which face dollar difficulties.
