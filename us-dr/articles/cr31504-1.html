Latin America: Complications

Last April a boatload of 90 armed Cubans scrambled ashore in Panama in an abortive invasion attempt. It was the first in a series of incidents which have brought about one of the most critical periods in Caribbean relations since the days of Dollar Diplomacy.

Other Cuban invaders attempted to strike at the Dominican Republic, which is headed by Dictator Rafael L. Trujillo. Nicaragua, too, had to repel raiders from outside its borders. A vicious propaganda duel, between the Dominicans, on the one hand, and Fidel Castro's revolutionary Government of Cuba and Venezuela, on the other, began to foul the air.

Faced with an obviously worsening crisis, the foreign ministers of the 21 American Republics, members of the Organization of American States, convened in an extraordinary session in Santiago, Chile, in an attempt to find a solution which would restore tranquility to the area.

The key issue is an extremely thorny one: How can democracy and freedom be promoted without violating the cornerstone principle of inter-American relations, that of non-intervention by one or more states in the affairs of another?

Cuba and Venezuela argue that there must be some modification of the non-intervention policy if dictatorships of the Trujillo ilk are to be eliminated and freedom brought to all people in the hemisphere. But most of the other members of the OAS, including the United States, are voicing strong support for non-intervention.

When it was originally adopted, the non-intervention policy was directed primarily against the United States, which had had a record of sending Marines to square away any troubles Americans may have had in the "Banana Republics." American officials find it ironic now that some Latin countries are willing to water-down the guarantees against interference by any country.

Ends vs. Means

In an address to the ministers, Secretary of State Herter referred to the recent invasion activities and declared:

"Yet, whether or not they were commendable, we cannot conduct our inter-American relations on the theory that the end justifies the means. . . . To weaken the principle of non-intervention and the principle of collective security in an effort to promote democracy is . . . a self-defeating activity. . . .

"The tensions which have developed in the Caribbean area in the last six months have, in the opinion of the United States, had an effect opposite from that of encouraging democracy."

The Secretary proposed a special Caribbean peace committee to study the crisis and report to the 11th Inter-American Conference in Quito next February. The committee would have no real authority over OAS members, but it could keep a spotlight on any new invasion moves and investigate hostile propaganda.

Reports from Santiago indicate, however, that the Herter proposal, as stated, is not likely to be accepted. It was believed more probable that the ministers would restore the wide powers of investigation and conciliation the Inter-American Peace Commission had up until three years ago.

Mr. Herter did not mention Cuba or Mr. Castro by name in his remarks about invasions which were organized with the knowledge and support of government officials. But the shoe seemed to fit. Friday night, in a radio-television speech billed as a report to the people on how an anti-Castro revolt had been put down, the Cuban leader denounced the Santiago meeting as a "farce."

Shiploads of Arms

He said the ministers were interested in discussing his government, not the Trujillo dictatorship. He criticized Mr. Herter for talking about "invasions" and not about food for hungry babies. He asserted that four shiploads of arms had been sent into Cuba to anti-Castro forces—one from the United States and three from the Dominican Republic. And he told, with great relish, how his aides, led by William A. Morgan, an American soldier-of-fortune type, tricked Trujillo into sending a transport plane loaded with arms to an "anti-Castro" airfield in Cuba which turned out to be a well-laid ambush.

United States officials are inclined to let Mr. Castro have his head of steam and roll with any punches he may aim in our direction. One key reason is a fear that by wrong moves or words, the United States might push him into the waiting arms of the Communists. This is particularly worrisome in view of the growing economic difficulties, including 500,000 unemployed workers, now being experienced by Cuba.

Mr. Castro is regarded by Washington as a sincere, dedicated leader who truly seeks the betterment of his people. But he also is looked upon as a man who is ignorant in the ways of democracy. Here it is noted that he has not called for free elections, he has supported aggressions against other countries and he has underestimated the threat of the Cuban Reds.
