Colonel Harry Betancourt, who served under the anti-government banner, recounts the machete cavalry charge at the battle of La Vega, where about a hundred men on each side, half-clad rebels with spurs strapped to naked ankles, fought hand to hand with cane knives in place of sabres. He says seventy-five per cent of the combatants on either side were killed or disabled, that he was the only United States citizen holding a command, and that three battles were fought that day around La Vega, with an interval of two hours between each.El coronel Harry Betancourt, que sirvió bajo la bandera antigubernamental, relata la carga de caballería con machetes en la batalla de La Vega, donde unos cien hombres de cada bando, rebeldes semidesnudos con espuelas atadas a los tobillos descalzos, lucharon cuerpo a cuerpo con cuchillos de caña en lugar de sables. Dice que el setenta y cinco por ciento de los combatientes de cada bando resultaron muertos o incapacitados, que él fue el único ciudadano de los Estados Unidos con un mando, y que ese día se libraron tres batallas alrededor de La Vega, con un intervalo de dos horas entre cada una.

The Sun · August 17, 191317 de agosto de 1913

Original in English.Original en inglés.

"General Nicio Pichardo was in command. He was the right soldier in the right soldier's place. He was a natural battle tempter. He was proud of his military rank and proud of the chance to display his authority. But you would condone his haughty mannerisms and sword flourishes. He was a fuss and feathers hero. Ever in the van and cheering on his men, no officer of any of the world's armies was ever more courageous. We fought three battles that day about La Vega—actually three battles, with an interval of two hours between each. In each of these encounters Gen. Pichardo received a wound. He fought in the most dangerous spots. He died of his injuries a few days later.

"Some of our troopers were boys only 15 years old. Yet they felt the battle power and tingle of the militant demigod. They held themselves stiffly erect, although scores of them had only their horse's backbone for a saddle. Heads high, and with the lofty, confident air of a field marshal, they eagerly and anxiously listened for the command to charge.

"Every machete held in the black or brown hand had been sharpened to a razor edge. For two days the sharpening had gone on in our bivouac outside La Vega. When there wasn't anything else to demand the attention of the troopers they fell back to putting a new edge on the machete in their belts. In some cases there was a scabbard, but in most cases there wasn't. The sharp edges of the great knives caused many injuries to the men themselves. A large number of the troopers were cut about the calf of the leg where the machetes rubbed against the naked flesh. The shedding of their blood by themselves was exhibited as a proud earnest of how they could endure hurt without flinching and what they would do to the enemy. In a number of cases these machete wounds were not treated, but the blood was allowed to flow until it stopped of its own accord.

"It was shortly after dawn when we rode out of the jungle. La Vega was asleep, its little houses and plaza still misty in the early light. We took a road that skirted a cane field, a side path from the main thoroughfare, leading to a piece of elevated ground overlooking the place we were to attack. It was neither a siege nor a storming. There were no fortifications to carry. It was just a case of an open charge and we were to drive the enemy right out of the town. We were to force them to retreat through it, just as the Confederates forced the Federals to retreat through Gettysburg on the first day's battle, and then we were to chase them the same as the Confederates chased the Federals through the little Pennsylvania hamlet.

"We halted on a little plateau while our officers levelled their glasses on La Vega. As they looked they saw that early as we had started the enemy had not been caught sleeping. About five hundred men were observed forming near the plaza. Careful examination through the glass made it plain that they had a force of horse about equal to our own and about 50 per cent. more infantry. Our riflemen numbered about 200. The force of the enemy divided. The cavalry turned toward the south and the infantry due east.

"In about ten minutes we saw the head of the cavalry column come out of a corner of the jungle fringed path we were on, about 150 yards distant. They saw us at the same instant, for we were drawn up on the plateau well in the open. Their leaders quickly passed warning word and wheeling galloped around the corner of the path again. Our troopers lost all discipline and brandishing their machetes demanded to be led against the enemy. Gen. Pichardo himself was of that frame of mind. He was about to give the command to charge down the path by fours when one of his staff restrained him. It took some courage to do this, for the General was a martinet. But the subordinate in a few quick spoken words pointed out the extreme likelihood of an ambush. Pichardo nodded his comprehension sullenly.

"Five minutes, ten minutes, fifteen minutes we waited. Not a movement from the enemy. They evidently expected us to charge. At last Pichardo ordered a feint. A dozen troopers better mounted than their comrades were ordered to charge. They galloped pell mell down the path toward the enemy sounding a defiant battle cry. Within twenty-five yards of the enemy's hiding place they wheeled their horses and halted, facing us. The feint worked. Out of the wood come troop after troop of the Government forces. They galloped toward our men, who turned, laughed and jeered at them, and soon regained our lines.

"The enemy, with more than half of his force exposed, gave up further hiding plans and headed for an angle of the plateau on which we stood. They were better mounted than we and soon all their force was on a level with us about a quarter of a mile distant. Seeing they intended to fight Pichardo lost no time. He ordered a charge and we dashed at them. They were prompt to decide, and as we drew near they were in full gallop toward us. In two minutes we had clashed. It was a sight for the great war captains in Valhalla.

"Within five minutes we were tangled up as completely as if we had been one body of soldiery. Hand to hand it was. The very horses seemed to bite at each other in animosity. Machetes circled through the air. At the end of swish after swish of the blades would come the flesh and bone stoppage of the blow. You could hear it plainly. Very few of the big knives seemed to strike each other in the swing and parry. It was all offence, careless of defence.

"I'll never forget the sight and the panting calls of defiance of the combatants. I did not hear a single cry for quarter. I saw heads nearly severed with one blow. Hands and fingers were lopped off cleanly. Cheeks were laid open by scores. The horses shared in the horrible wounds. The razor edged machetes missing the rider often struck the neck and flank of the mounts.

"The fight lasted from forty-five minutes to an hour. Brief as it was it will very likely hold the record for being the worst machete cavalry fight in history. And the odd part of it was that hardly a hundred shots were fired. Those of the officers who were armed with revolvers fired until their pistols were emptied and they were so closely pressed hand to hand and horse to horse they had no time to reload. The deaths and wounds by powder were not more than half a dozen all told. It was the cane cutting knife that did the awful work.

"Ten minutes after the battle was under way one of our troopers, a mere boy not yet 16 years old, dropped one of his spurs. The strap had slipped over his bare foot. The spurs to him represented the proof of his growth to quick manhood. He did his fighting with his machete, but he had used that for a year or so in the canefield. The spurs—they meant something of rank and standing as a fighting man. So the boy trooper dismounted and seized his dropped spur. While he was picking it up a big framed, coal black cavalryman of the Government forces galloped down on him. He swung his sabre as his impetus carried him to and past the boy. According to all calculations the boy should have been beheaded or cleft from crown to chin. But he was neither. He dodged just like a playful gamin eluding a pursuer. Before the big trooper could wheel to see the result of his blow the dodging boy had plunged his machete into the horse from beneath. The animal dropped dead, bringing down his giant rider. The boy was on him in a minute. The man never rose again.

"After the battle the youngster offered me his foe's boots as a mark of consideration and remembrance of one or two small favors. He hardly understood why I declined the gift. The dead man's spurs were strapped on his feet. He had given his old ones to another fighting boy who had not been so fortunate in the killing.

"We whipped the Government forces. A remnant of them escaped to the town again. During this first fight of the day our own infantry as well as the infantry of the Government forces did nothing to help. They stood gazing wild eyed at what was going on. It was a new sight for them and they refused to take their eyes from it. Our infantry officers said after the battle that it was better that way, because had they moved the enemy's infantry would have moved and the result might have been different. Odd sort of reasoning. As the infantry in the two other encounters of the day took an honorable part, however, they could not be too severely criticised. We captured La Vega and our victory kept us from grumbling about details.

"It was a grewsome sight where the enemy's flag had been captured by our troopers. A dozen members of the color guard lay slashed to death. The staff of the flag had been severed completely in two places by single blows of machetes. It was a gloriously fought battle and not to be sneered at by any means because the combatants did not number thousands instead of hundreds."

"With all their theatrical ways they are some fighters," says Col. Betancourt. "Gen. Pichardo fought like a demon until he received his death wound. Gen. Zarzuela had his leg amputated on the battlefield, smoking a cigarette during the operation. I think I am something of a judge. I served through the Spanish-American war. I was with the Johnny O'Brien expedition to Cuba in 1896 and fought against Spain there with Garcia, Joaquim Plancas, Pedro A. Perez and Vincente Miniet. This last Santo Domingan campaign measures up to all of their fights."
