War Embarrasses Social Amenities

Hostesses See Difficulty in Entertaining Diplomats—State Troubles of Messenger "Eddie."

Many-sided war in Europe is hardly more vexatious to the State Department and other government officials than it is perplexing to society hostesses in Washington, and even to the humble doorman for the Secretary of State.

Washington hostesses view with great perturbation the coming social season. With Lord Kitchener's suggestion that the war may run for at least eighteen months they clearly see the winter will require hours of thoughtful care in so planning their functions that the amenities will be observed and no warworn diplomat offended.

Pate de foie gras and champagne must not be served when the Austrian ambassador is a guest. Frankfurters and kartoffel salad cannot appear on the dinner menu when M. Jusserand or members of his staff are present. Caviar and roast beef must not be served to the Germans.

Safety Dinner Plan.

The suggestion has been advanced that at fashionable dinners this winter there will arise an extraordinary popularity for scattered tables—little tables seating five or six, with no head and not foot, a subterfuge to avoid complications of precedence.

But while the hostesses are looking into the future—as one might say, "borrowing trouble"—Eddie Savoy, veteran negro messenger to the Secretary of State, has his worries all in the present tense, walking in upon him daily very much in the flesh. He has to see that these various diplomatic representatives of warring nations are kept diplomatically apart while in the neutral zone of the State Department.

So nicely has Eddie guided his charges through the maze of diplomatic avoidance that any time he wants to quit the State Department service he ought to be able to get employment as adviser to fashionable Washington on how to side-step diplomatic embarrassments.

Eddie Savoy's Diplomacy.

Eddie is a nautral diplomat. For nearly two-score years he has guarded the portal of the Secretary of State and announced the coming of foreign representatives of high or low degree.

It is Eddie who "keeps tab" on the rules of precedence among the Secretary's visitors. He escorts them to the anteroom. He awaits the pleasure of the Secretary. When all is ready for the call of state, he ushers the plenipotentiary or the charge, or whomsoever the visitor may be, into the presence of the head of the foreign office.

Eddie is confronted at this time with the toughest problem he ever has tackled. The State Department has only one anteroom worthy the name. With war raging through seven nations of Europe and none of their representatives in Washington permitted to recognize even the physical presence of the representative of an enemy state, the difficult but highly important duty of keeping them apart has devolved on Eddie.

A Situation Avoided.

A notable example of Eddie's worriments was brought to the attention of an interested and amused group of newspaper men when the German ambassador, Count von Bernstorff, called on Mr. Bryan. The tall, slender Teuton, his blue eyes a-twinkle and his "kaiserlich" mustache partly concealing a smile, emerged from the elevator.

"Yes, your excellency?" Eddie greeted him.

"Is the anteroom occupied?" questioned the ambassador, cautiously.

"Yes, sir, the minister from Santo Domingo is in there, sir," replied Eddie.

"Oh, that is all right," replied the count. "We are not at war with the Dominican republic."

And so the ambassador entered and greeted his fellow-diplomat cordially.

It would have been different if the incumbent of the anteroom had been a representative from Great Britain, France, Russia or Belgium. Diplomatic etiquette forbids even the most formal recognition of the presence of an enemy's official.

Had Count von Bernstorff collided with M. Jules J. Jusserand, the ambassador from France, he could not properly have so much as nodded to him. Such a greeting as "wie gehts" or "bon jour"? Oh, my, no! The precedents are all the other way.

On the other hand, such a greeting as "I hope you choke!" is likewise barred under the rules of diplomatic usage.
