Trujillo Grip Is Unshaken Fourth-Term Sweep Is Proof of Power in Tiny Republic

By Reginald L. Wood

CIUDAD TRUJILLO, Dominican Republic.—Rafael L. Trujillo, 56, who named this capital city after himself, who erected his own monument and who is addressed as "His Excellency, Mr. President, Benefactor of the Nation," will begin his fourth term as ruler of this tiny Caribbean republic August 16.

Trujillo was re-elected May 16 by a landslide plurality over two token presidential opponents, nominated by parties he permitted to exist after he said in 1945 the country was ready for a two or three party system.

Trujillo's control is as absolute as that of any ward boss. The newspapers La Opinion and La Nacion, which support the government, said of his recent tour of the country that he was received with "delirious acclaim."

He Is Personable Man.

Contrary to the impression that dictators have horns, Trujillo is a personable man. Wearing a close-cropped steel gray mustache, he looks and acts like a corporation executive who likes to work in his shirt sleeves but his collars appear to be choking him.

The 17 years he has ruled this country have given him a regal bearing. He walks precisely and talks in clipped sentences, but his eyes twinkle and he is quick at repartee.

Trujillo's legal opposition, the National Democratic Party headed by Rafael Espaillat, cabinet member in former administrations, and the National Laborite Party which supported Francisco Prats-Ramirez, produced only a token vote and did not constitute a genuine movement against the Dominican Party created by Trujillo when he gained the presidency in 1930.

The real opposition to Trujillo's government apparently centers around the Partido Socialista Popular, patterned after the Cuban party of the same name. The Dominican DSP is headed by Felix Servio Docoudray and Ercilio B. Garcia. Servio Ducoudray claims only 200 party members, but Dominican Republic government officials place the total membership between 2,000 and 3,000, a tiny percentage of the nation's 2,000,000 population.

Servio Ducoudray directs publication of a clandestine mimeographed newspaper, El Popular, which has a restricted circulation. He said police interfered with distribution.

Peaceful Resistance.

The PSP head said his party stood for peaceful resistance to the Trujillo government. "Our party repudiates all acts of violence and disorder, which only serve as a pretext for the enemies of democracy to deprive the people of their rights and liberties," he said.

Two members of the legal opposition parties won seats in the new Congress, one from the Province of Santiago and the other from the Duarte district. The vote polled by the Democratic Party in Santiago, some 22,000, was significant because it was there that the last uprising against the Trujillo administration occurred.

It will be the first time since Trujillo's rise to power that minority parties have been represented in the National House of Representatives. Separate parties did not legally exist prior to 1945.

There was no reason to believe that the ballot count after the election was not accurate. There was no reason for election officials to pad the count because the Dominican Party controls upward of 90 per cent of the vote.

Only those who hold cedulas (a form of registration carrying fingerprints and pictures) are entitled to vote. PSP charged that the cedulas were issued under government party control and that those who honestly opposed the government were unable to register, the cedulas were distributed free to the poor and at prices ranging from $1 to $500 to workers and professional men.

The constitution provides for listing of all parties on one ballot with the right of each voter to mark the ballot in secret. Ballots could not be split, however.

Typical Election Scene.

At one polling precinct about 15 miles from Ciudad Trujillo where helpful government officials were showing correspondents how the vote was cast, I saw this scene:

Election officials at Villa Mella had placed a bar across the door of a barn used as the voting precinct. Voters stepped up to the bar, passed in their cedulas which were stamped to prevent double voting. Then, without any request from the voter, a precinct official marked the ballots for Trujillo and dropped them in the box.

The chairman of the precinct board explained that the system "saves a lot of confusion," men and women of 18 years or over can vote whether they can read or not. An estimated 30 per cent of the voters are illiterate, possibly more.

Trujillo has been charged with many political crimes by the real opposition, outstanding among them the death of between 10,000 and 12,000 Haitians in 1937 after they had encroached Dominican Republic lands along the border.

Government officials, asked about the "massacre," shrugged it away and said, "Many Dominicans died, too."

Asked if it were true that many opponents of his government had been persecuted and jailed, Trujillo replied: "You are welcome to visit any of the jails in the country, and you will be free to ask any prisoner if he is a 'political prisoner.'"

It appears now that the Dominican Party is maintaining itself in power through careful and thorough organization rather than political despotism.

Haitians Are Put to Work.

Trujillo is wooing the Haitians. He has an ambitious industrialization program along the frontier.

The frontier zone is sparsely settled by Dominicans, but the other side of the border is bulging with Haitians. He proposes to bring Haitians to work in factories under contract. One spokesman for the government described the idea as "magnificent" because it would relieve pressure on the Haitian side of the border, and at the same time provide labor for Dominican industries without danger of union organization.

Each worker would be contracted individually. So long as he works he could remain in the Dominican Republic, but if he becomes involved in union organization other than that permitted by the government he would be subject to deportation.

Haiti's population is about 3,000,000, or some 260 persons to the square mile, while the Dominican Republic has about 88 persons per square mile.

The two republics have exchanged foreign ministers as ambassadors to seek a solution for the critical population problem.
