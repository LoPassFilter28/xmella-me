ED ALVAREZ Ancestors Changed Name And Countries Before Reaching New World

By JANE JASTRAM

His first attempt at public speaking is still vividly remembered by John Jenaro Edward Alvarez, known to Nogalians as Ed Alvarez. Immediately after the American occupation of San German, Puerto Rico, in 1898, the American commanding officer asked the school superintendent to choose a school boy to receive the first American flag to fly over the city. Ed, then 9 years old, was the boy chosen.

He can still recite the speech he memorized and eagerly spoke in Spanish on that all-important occasion, and he recalls it in English: "We accept this flag as a symbol of our new country and we shall protect it to death."

The next six years passed quickly in the Alvarez home where a family of three boys and three girls made life interesting for Captain Vicente Alvarez and his wife, the former Carmen O'Neil Pabon. Ed's soldier father, also a lawyer and judge for the Southern District of Puerto Rico, retained his judgeship after the American occupation.

Ed, who joined the family Sept. 19, 1888, remembers that his sisters always had to be chaperoned by their mother when they saw their boy friends, and the piano duets he and his oldest sister played in the local little theater where special programs were presented. He deserted music in later years, however. He got his first business experience in copying leases and deeds and writing out receipts for his father who had considerable real estate holdings.

The Alvarez family, originating in Sweden, were military and legal adventurers who left the peninsula and wandered down through France and Spain, eventually going to the New World with the Spanish. They were proud that one member of the family had been a member of the Rigstad, now considered the oldest Parliament in the world. Their name had changed in the journeyings from Alfersen to Alva to Alvarez.

Soon after the discovery of the Caribbean Islands Gen. Vicente Alvarez was sent by the King of Spain to the early 1600's to govern Puerto Rico. He returned to Spain later, but so many of his stories of the beautiful Puerto Rico were passed down to the succeeding generations that Ed's father, Captain Alvarez, and other members of the family decided to see for themselves.

Ed in turn ventured to the United States after he graduated from school in San German in 1904 to attend Ohio Northern University at Ada, the alma mater of the fiance of his sister.

He recalls the United States did not seem strange. He was familiar with United States history and government from his school work and he made the trip from New York to Ada via American history sightseeing journey. He was duly impressed with the Statue of Liberty and historic sites in New York. He stopped in New Jersey to see the Delaware River at Trenton and tried to visualize General Washington and his men crossing the river in a storm of winter, a season he had never experienced. Near Pittsburgh he looked out the train window and saw what appeared to be great flakes of cotton falling to the ground and rapidly covering the mountainsides. It was several minutes before he realized he was seeing snow for the first time.

The hardest adjustment he had to make, Ed recalls, was becoming acclimated to the freezing weather and snow after living all his life in a tropical country. Once when the thermometer registered 25 degrees below zero, Ed huddled over the floor register in one of the college buildings so long the buttons on his overcoat melted and twisted and the soles of his shoes cracked and stiffened making them unwearable.

He had not been enrolled at Ohio Northern University long before he became well acquainted with Miss Rose Stage of Cambridge, Ohio, who was in his English literature and math classes. They became engaged on Thanksgiving Day, 1904. Because he had begun his study of English as soon as Puerto Rico was taken over by the United States, Ed got along very well in the university. He continued his public speaking career in the Franklin Debating Society and was a lieutenant in Company D.

After graduating from the university as the youngest member of his class, Ed went to New York City where he took a job with a firm of German importers of foreign books. In the evenings he studied law and chemistry. He and his university sweetheart, Rose Stage, were married Nov. 30, 1907, in New York where their eldest child, Carmen Rose, now Mrs. J. D. Poindexter of Burlingame, Calif., was born.

He became the legal clerk in charge of deeds, leases and contracts for the South Puerto Rico Sugar Company in 1909 and returned with his family to Guanica, Puerto Rico, where his other children were born: Edward, now in New York with the United States Engineers; Robert Theodore, Nogales; Mrs. Alex Webb (Ida), Akron, Ohio; Mrs. Ben Sasnett (Judy), and Albert, Nogales; and Mrs. Lloyd O. Tritch (Ruth), Hayward, Calif.

Alvarez was still working for the South Puerto Rico Sugar Company when the United States entered World War I. His position was considered essential to the war effort and he was not permitted to enter active service. He spent the war years in Red Cross work and other patriotic endeavors.

About 1923 Ed met his first Nogalian in Havana, Cuba, where he had gone for the General Sugar Company of New York to do the work of reorganizing and refinancing 23 sugar mills which the company had acquired. It was there he met Thomas D. Boyd, also interested in the work. In 1926 Boyd returned to Nogales where he was president of the Mexico-Arizona Trading Company, first winter vegetable organization on the West Coast of Mexico.

In Mexico 1926 was a bad year for tomato growers. They owed large amounts of money to the trading company and contracts needed renegotiating. Boyd remembered Ed's work in Havana. He wired Ed to come to Nogales to manage this job and other legal phases.

Leaving his family in Puerto Rico, Ed arrived in Nogales in August, 1926. He completed his job by Jan. 1, 1927, and returned to Puerto Rico, never expecting to see Nogales again. He was again employed by the South Puerto Rican Sugar Company and was sent to Santo Domingo, Santo Dominican Republic, where he organized a large sugar development. Here some of the land titles he investigated went back 300 years.

The assassination of Boyd's brother-in-law, Robert C. Howell, by a Chinese in Los Mochis, left the Mexico-Arizona Trading Company without a manager. Boyd persuaded Alvarez to return to Nogales to assist in operation of the company.

Ed accepted and arrived on the border in 1928, bringing his family the following year. All of his children with the exception of Carmen, Edward and Ida were graduated from Nogales in the ensuing years.

When the depression in 1931 put the skids under the tomato business, along with almost everything else, Alvarez started Easons Auto Supply Company early in the year. His sons Robert and Albert in later years became his associates.

Ed, with characteristic enthusiasm and energy, entered actively into the life of the border town. He was president of the Chamber of Commerce in 1933 and 1944 and served on the Board of Directors from 1936 to 1947. He became deeply interested in fraternal work and is a past chancellor commander of the Knights of Pythias, a past master of Nogales Masonic Lodge, No. 11, a past president of the Scottish Rite Club, and past patron of Unity Chapter, No. 13, O.E.S.

He has served the Nogales Rotary Club as president and is permanent chairman of the organization's International Relationship Committee. He is chairman of the Board of Trustees of the Methodist Church and a past chairman of the Santa Cruz County Republican Central Committee which he also served as secretary for several years.

During World War II Ed was district supervisor of the United States Office of Censorship on the border, served on the Advisory Board for draftees and was a member of a committee working with the Office of Price Administration, receiving a certificate of commendation for the latter service from President Harry S. Truman.

Ed is rather expecting a new title in 1951, that of great grandfather. He has 15 grandchildren, one grandson in the Army Air Corps at Hamilton Air Base, California.

Alvarez says he stayed in Nogales because he saw a great future for the border town and because he enjoyed the pleasant relations with the Mexican people. "I've always tried," Ed said, "to be a good connecting link between America and Mexico."
