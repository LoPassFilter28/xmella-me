Our Dominican Correspondence.

City of St. Domingo, July 15, 1848.

Decree for the Organization of the Civic Guard—Requisition on Foreigners to Enroll themselves therein—Subsequent Modification of it in Regard to this Class of Residents—The Question of the Lawfulness of such a Requisition, under the Law of Nations, Briefly Considered—Steps taken to Obtain the Recognition of the Republic—A Ground of Difficulty in this Matter Stated.

A measure of some interest to foreigners resident here—United States citizens, as well as others—and on which action has just now begun to be taken, seems to demand some notice at the hand of your correspondent, if from no other American quarter.

Acting under the general authority conferred upon him by the article of the constitution noticed in my last letter, the President of this republic promulgated on the 22d of February last, a decree for the more effectual organization and enrollment of the civic guard, or militia, of the State. This document commences by declaring that all those persons who at the sound of the tocsin of alarm, by the proper authorities, shall not present themselves to take arms, shall be considered as suspected persons, and treated and punished as such, as the law or circumstances shall require. It further declares that foreigners who have entered the country, shall also form part of the civic guard after three months' residence therein. They, as well as the citizens, are allowed to choose in what arm to serve; their uniform and arms to be furnished at their own expense.

It is decreed that the civic guard shall appear under arms to pass review once every three months—namely, the first Sundays (the regular parade day) in January, April, July, and October respectively, and in all cases provided for by the laws, which includes the obligatory liability to march to the frontier, or into cantonment, in case of apprehended invasion; and, in short, to serve in any emergency, as militia may be required to do.

After entering into some details, which are unimportant to my present purpose, the decree proceeds to prescribe specific penalties for non-compliance with its requirements. Any member of the guard who shall not appear at the prescribed reviews, is to suffer, for the first delinquency, arrest and imprisonment—an arrest of twenty-four hours; for a second, double that length of time, and a very small pecuniary fine; and, for the third, in addition, he shall suffer the penalty of being incorporated into the troops of the line. No distinction is here made between citizens and mere foreign residents, who do not enjoy the rights pertaining to that character.

The promulgation of this decree caused great excitement among the foreign residents, who are almost exclusively of European origin; and the consular representative of France—the only European one here, and under whose protection most of the foreign residents from the old world range themselves—protested against its provisions as applicable to them, and, moreover, directed them not to comply with the mandate. The United States commercial agent, also, who resides at Puerto Plata, on the north side of the island, took ground against it; and besides sending for his deputy here, at the capital, for a list of whatever American citizens were resident here, he also sent directions to him to protest to the government against the requisitions of the decree, in case an intention on the part of the authorities to enforce it against citizens of the United States, should be manifested.

The opposition made to the right of enforcing this decree against foreign residents, made by the French functionary, had the effect to induce the President to modify its terms in reference to this class of the population, which he did in a supplemental decree, dated the 29th of March. In this latter, he states that the same penalties cannot be applied to foreigners who are required to enroll themselves in the civic guard; as to the natives,—nacionales—inasmuch, as by not being in the enjoyment of political rights, (in other words, not being citizens,) the former cannot be made to constitute a part of the permanent army. On this account, the penalty against them will be, for the first and second delinquencies, arrest and imprisonment in the Consistorial House—los arrestos en la casa Consistorial—and the small prescribed fines; and for the third, deprivation, or the withholding of patents, that is, licence to trade, &c., and of the right to exercise their profession or industry for the space of two years. The foreigners are further allowed by its provisions, to form a company by themselves, which shall constitute part of a battalion. And lastly, they are not to be required to march to the frontier, or other cantonment, even though occasion should arise for the mobilization, or calling forth and marching, of the civic guard. But they are required on such occasions only, to take arms in the place of their residence, and place themselves at the service of the government for the purpose of protecting their own (or its, as the peculiar construction of the Spanish language allows it with equal propriety to be translated, though the understanding of its meaning by the public here, is in accordance with the former construction,) interests and the public security. But whenever any of this company become citizens, they are to be transferred to other companies of the civic guard.

I have thus given a summary of the requisitions of the original decree, in connection with that of the subsequent and materially modified explanatory one, for the sake of preserving the historical connection of events and of illustrating the course of things here, and not for the purpose of casting odium or reproach on this government for an act which, though a flagrant contravention of personal rights of foreign residents, under the law of nations, was yet essentially modified on protest being made against it. But even as the law now stands, the decree, having all the binding force of one, there are, I conceive, serious objections to it on the same ground.

It is, I take it, a well settled, undisputed principle of what is called the private law of nations, that the foreign residents of a country, who have not acquired the rights and privileges of citizenship, according to its laws, while they owe the same obedience that natives do to the general municipal local laws, are, however, entirely exempt, of right, from all military service whatever; and that, in all cases of public disturbance or commotion—intestine or from without—they have the indefeasible right to stand neutral, and, in this character, to place themselves under the protecting folds of their country's flag at the consulate or embassy, as the case may be. The decree in question violates this principle. Undoubtedly, this government has the right to say, that foreigners resident in the country shall not be allowed to carry on trade or follow any other particular calling, unless they will serve in the militia. This is an act of sovereignty which, as such in the abstract, cannot, I suppose, be called in question, though its exercise is certainly contrary to the liberal spirit and enlightened practice of modern times. But to subject foreigners to arrest and fine—which latter, no matter how small, necessarily carries with it as a sanction the power to enforce its payment, either by imprisonment or the seizure and confiscation of property under process of law, to satisfy a claim to the State, the legality of which is altogether denied—is as manifest a violation of individual right, under the law of nations, if I am right in my view of the subject, as was the original decree, though by no means to the same objectionable extent. The representative of France is understood to have acquiesced in the terms of the supplementary decree, and this morning there was a general meeting of the foreign residents for the purpose of organizing their company by enrollment and the choosing of officers. Your correspondent, Mr. Herald, of course looked in on them. Uncle Sam was unrepresented, at least by any of his legitimate boys, whose number, at present here, lacks one, to tell the truth, of being sufficient to form a trio. If, instead of the form of a penalty—sanctioned decree or law—the call upon the foreigners had been in the form of an invitation and appeal to their chivalry or public spirit, our venerable and beloved uncle would certainly have been represented at the meeting by those "of his own flesh and blood." But it is, I believe, a characteristic trait of that worthy gentleman's male representatives, abroad as well as at home, that they won't be driven into a thing which cannot be rightfully required of them, though they may be led into it by reasonable request or solicitation. Like old Sir John—though, to say the truth, more ready generally to back their words by acts than was that worthy—"if reasons were as plenty as blackberries, they wouldn't give one on compulsion."

Notwithstanding the directions acknowledged to have been given, as before stated, no official opposition to the operation of this decree has, as yet, been made here in behalf of the United States' citizens. The writer contents himself, for the present, with this simple statement of the fact, reserving to himself the right to comment on it and its attendant circumstances in full, should occasion hereafter arise to call forth such comment.

The truth is, the United States ought to be represented here at the capital and principal port by an agent appointed directly by the department at Washington, and in immediate communication with it, and not by a deputy of the one so appointed and resident at Puerto Plata, which port, for all practical purposes, is farther removed from the capital than is Castine, in the State of Maine, from Corpus Christi, Texas. Nor are the facilities for secure and independent communication between this city and Puerto Plata as great as those, for the same purpose, between this and the port of New York.

Further, the interests of American citizens who may from time to time be temporarily resident here, ought to be more frequently and carefully looked to by the United States government than they have heretofore been. No American vessel of war has touched here for several years. Let the head of the navy department order one to do so; let him instruct her commander to enquire after resident American citizens and their interests, and whether any among them are denied any rights or exemptions which the law and usage of nations guaranty to the foreign residents of a country. Such a step, with such instructions faithfully carried out—as they certainly would be, if committed to any officer of the American navy—would have a most salutary and abiding effect. Such a visit, so far from creating ill feeling, the writer knows would be considered complimentary, while it would show that the American government is jealous of any infringement of the rights and privileges secured to its citizens by the code of nations, the nice prescriptions and distinctions of which, it is to be feared, the Spanish States of the new world do not always regard with the most scrupulous care.

This republic, now—since the 27th of February last—in the fifth year of its existence, has not yet been recognised by any nation. Soon after the establishment of the present government, a commissioner was sent to Washington, for the purpose of obtaining its recognition by the United States. He did not, however, succeed in effecting the object of his mission.

Since that time, this government has sent commissioners to Europe, to endeavor to obtain the recognition of the new republic by the courts of Spain, France, and Great Britain. The aim has ever been to first obtain its recognition by the first of these three governments. This is regarded as a very desirable, if not, indeed, a necessary preliminary to obtaining that of the other two, on account of the old unextinguished and unrenounced title to the crown of Spain to the territory of this, her ancient colony.

To make this clearly intelligible, it may be well to remark that, by the treaty of Basle, some half century since, the territory of the old Spanish portion of the island, now embraced within, and co-extensive with, the limits of this republic, was ceded to France, as I have stated in a former letter, who held possession until the year 1809 or 1810, when Spain again resumed her ancient title and possession, in virtue of the treaty of Paris, and continued thenceforward to hold it under the rule of royal governors appointed by the Crown, until the 30th of November, 1821, on or about which particular date the Spanish Governor was deposed in a popular insurrection, put on board a vessel, and sent home to Spain. The home government seems to have been too much occupied with other affairs to resent or notice this; at all events, this was the last governor sent out to rule the colony, and the inhabitants who had, on his expulsion, formed a provisional government, after much intestine and party strife, at last hoisted the Colombian flag. But that South American republic declining to receive within its territorial limits the proffered island colony, the inhabitants were again thrown back on their own governmental resources and abilities, and after many scenes of anarchy, they—or a faction of them, at least, for the thing is a matter of dispute here—invited Boyer to come and take possession of the country, which that dignitary responded to by marching his army to this city, as has been stated in a former letter, in the year 1822. The occupation by Hayti, thus commenced, continues the chain of overriding titles, by occupancy, down to the year 1844, the era of its separation from that republic, and of its erection into the present independent Dominican republic.

This brief historical recapitulation—in some respects more complete than the account I have previously given—shows the foundation and present state of the old Spanish title to the territory of this republic; a title which has been so long and so frequently over-ridden without protestation, by supervening ones, as to be now buried, one would suppose, almost beyond the power or reach of resurrectionary resuscitation. I have said, without protestation. That is not altogether correct for the whole of the above period, as will appear by the following historical anecdote, which I have from good authority, and repeat, partly to preserve a historical fact of which there is, I believe, no record existing, on this side of the ocean at least, save among the musty public archives at Port au Prince, and partly to illustrate the political astuteness of the shrewd Haytien chief, Boyer. In or about the year 1827, the King of Spain sent an envoy, one Senor De Castro, to Port au Prince, to formally demand of President Boyer the surrender to the Spanish crown of this part of the island, at that time constituting, as has been seen, a part of the Haytien republic. To his demand, made at an interview held for that purpose, the cunning black chief replied that he had taken possession of it, at the solicitation of its inhabitants; but that he was willing and disposed to refer the question back to them, whether they would remain as they were, an integral portion of the Haytien republic, or return under the dominion of the crown of Spain. If they should decide by their popular voice, in favor of the latter course, he had no objection to their adopting it. No decided offence could be taken at a so seemingly fair proposition; while, at the same time, the envoy knew as well as President Boyer did, what would be the result if the matter were to be submitted to this method of decision; for a large majority of the present citizens were recently emancipated slaves, and those who sympathized with them from being of the same color and of a similar class; and these would, of course, have an equal voice with other classes of citizens, in deciding the question, which, to the emancipated portion of them, would be no less than a question of continued freedom, or return to servitude under Spanish masters. The envoy hereupon took his leave, and the demand was prosecuted no further.

The last despatches, within the writer's knowledge, received by this government from their commissioners in Europe, were of the date of November last, from Madrid, in which they express themselves as entertaining strong hopes of obtaining the recognition of the republic. It had, in fact, some time previous, been expressly promised by the Spanish minister; but his displacement very soon after, and the successive changes that rapidly followed each other, of a similar kind, had thus far delayed and prevented the termination of the negotiation.

As to the recognition of this republic by the government of the United States, that is a question for the authorities at Washington, in their wisdom, to determine for themselves. I do not feel called upon, nor indeed am I disposed at present, to state what the opinion of so humble an individual as myself may be on this subject. I have thus far contented myself mainly with stating facts and circumstances of various kinds, leaving those who may read them—be they in authority or otherwise—to draw their own conclusions on this as on other points. There, at least, so far as regards this particular question, I shall stop for the present. Hereafter, I may perhaps present my views, whatever they may be, on the point in consideration, supported by as good reasons as a somewhat favorable and tolerably well improved opportunity for arriving at correct conclusions will have enabled me to bring together.
