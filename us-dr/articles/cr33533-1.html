Jewish Refugees Build New Lives As Settlers On Estate Given By Trujillo; 4,000 Reborn In Dominican Haven

BY S. S. GITTLEMEN

Honorary Consul in Arizona for the Dominican Republic

Gleeming in the golden Caribbean sun, twin columns of native pink marble stand side by side as a simple but meaningful monument on the shores of the Dominican Republic.

Unveiled about four years ago, these twin columns were inscribed as a lasting monument to "The Open Door." Both bear bronze tablets with identical inscriptions, one in Spanish and the other in English.

They say just this:

"The republic, in conformity with its constitution and laws, hereby guarantees to the settlers and their descendants full opportunity to continue their lives and occupations free from molestation, discrimination or persecution, with full freedom of religion and religious ceremonies, with equality of opportunity and of civil, legal and economic rights as well as all other rights inherent to human beings.

"Dedicated in homage to Generalissimo Dr. Rafael Leonidas Trujillo, the great friend of mankind, by Dr. James N. Rosenberg, Dr. Maurice B. Hexter and the eternally grateful Dorsa settlers of Sosua."

SIMPLE THOUGH these words may be, their importance lies in the fact that they symbolized the saving of 4,000 Jewish lives.

They are the words of Article I of a contract between the government of the Dominican Republic and the Dominican Republic Settlement Association (DORSA), signed on Jan. 30, 1940, and ratified by the Dominican legislature within three weeks.

This was the answer of President Trujillo to the bestial depredations of the Nazi regime against all of European Jewry.

A year or two before the outbreak of World War II the late President Roosevelt called together 38 nations, whose representatives met in France in 1938, to alleviate the plight of those fleeing from Nazi barbarities. It was at this convocation that Trujillo opened wide the door of asylum to European Jews.

NO OTHER NATION followed this example.

This was followed by visits from U. S. government experts and also by the Intergovernmental Committee for Refuge and Spokesmen for the Jewish world community.

These groups unanimously selected the extensive property at Sosua as the most advantageous place for a first settlement. Located on its north coast, it had an ocean frontage of some 8 miles. As it turned out, these lands were the private property of the generalissimo.

Without waiting for completion of any contract or ratification of same, he offered the entire settlement as his free gift, by addressing to Dr. Rosenberg (head of the refugee group) the following letter:

"I am deeply interested to cooperate in a practical way with the humanitarian plans of President Roosevelt. I hope the immigration of European refugees to the Dominican Republic will stimulate the progress of our country, and will intensify the development of our natural resources as well as industries.

"My country property located in Sosua, Province of Puerto Plata, has, as you have informed me, been selected by Dr. Joseph A. Rosen as an appropriate place for settlement.

"This property, of about 26,685 acres contains twenty-four dwellings, reservoirs and other installations for the accommodation of settlers. The property has 4,950 acres of cultivated pasture and large extent of virgin forest with an abundance of valuable timber, which represents for me an actual investment of not less than $100,000.

"To repeat, I am deeply interested in the realization of this settlement enterprise, because I am convinced that we must all cooperate within our possibilities for the success of this humanitarian and noble effort. It is, therefore, my dear Dr. Rosenberg, my pleasure to offer to the Refugee Association, of which you are the esteemed Chairman, my Sosua property, as my personal contribution for the establishment there, of the first refugee settlemnt in the Dominican Republic."

Said Dr. Rosenberg: "The Dominican Republic authorized all its consulates throughout Europe to issue immigration visas to all Jews who had succeeded in escaping from the Nazi terror. These visas were veritable life insurance policies for more than 4,000 souls."

Each homesteader was given (not sold) 75 acres of fertile land, upon which stood ready a frame two-room dwelling, a cow barn, and other farm buildings. Each settler received 10 cows, an extra cow if he was married, and two additional cows for each child. He was also given a horse, a mule, and a loan for miscellaneous needs.

Since that time the colony of DORSA at Sosua has flourished. They have a resort hotel, delicatessens, restaurants, poultry farms, furniture and clothing manufacture, haberdashery stores and a tree nursery. Some have become salesmen, entrepeneurs, exporters. They produce sausages and cured meats. They have a loan cooperative that is, in effect, the community bank.

The conductor of the Dominican National Symphony Orchestra was formerly a professor of music in Vienna. Several of the colonists are at present teaching at the University of Santo Domingo.

They have a hospital at Sosua, with 16 beds and a maternity ward. These are available to Dominicans and colonists alike. At their Cristobal Colon elementary school, the three R's are taught in Spanish, English and Hebrew. And all youngsters have free access to secondary schools and the university.

The religious center of Sosua is the synagogue, where not only are services held at all times, but also are children given regular instruction in Hebrew and Jewish History. Religious and racial prejudice have no place whatsoever in the Dominican Republic.

It is significant to read a declaration made by Trujillo when, about six or seven years ago, the Sosua settlers presented him with a memorial scroll as a token of their esteem and appreciation.

"I am deeply grateful to my dear friends, Dr. Rosenberg and Dr. Hexter, for the Scroll which they have given me, and I wish to say that my admiration of the Jews comes from the fact that they are champions of the principles of humanity, justice, civilization, brotherhood and progress.

"Lately, the Dominican Republic at the instance of its humblest servant, that is, myself, has offered to receive and is desirous of receiving 20,000 to 25,000 people who have been brutally persecuted by communism and who have fled from the countries behind the Iron Curtain. Any time there is a commission appointed by the United Nations or other responsible body to select appropriate places for receiving such refugees, my government will be delighted to welcome them, to work with them, and to give them all the help that is necessary so that they may make a full study of the possibilities of settlement in the Dominican Republic, just as Dr. Rosenberg did 15 years ago. Such settlers will be welcomed and will, in due course, be entitled to citizenship. I can state that there is ample room for such settlers.

"Ours is a small country. So was ancient Israel. So was Greece. Perhaps from the small countries can surge a spirit of human brotherhood. It is in this spirit that the Dominican Republic is ready to open its doors to righteous and honest people, who are ready to work and become a constructive part of the Dominican Republic and of its internal life. Such men and women will be welcomed with settlers at Sosua.

"The settlement at Sosua is a small one. It is only an experiment, a pilot plan, but it serves to show, but its splendid record, what conscientious, intelligent and hard working people can do when a government extends the hand of friendship, welcomes them and gives them help. Good citizens are the most valuable assets a country can have."

The displaced people who arrived in Sosua 15 years ago are living prosperous, happy lives in the new world they created for themselves.

Just as the Statue of Liberty was presented to the United States by a grateful France to commemorate the democratic freedoms sought by immigrant peoples, so too, the twin pillars of marble looking out to sea at Cuidad Trujillo becken to the oppressed.

The door is open.
