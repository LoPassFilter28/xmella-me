An Absolute Dictatorship European Autocrats Mere Upstarts Compared With Ruler of Dominican Republic, According to Exile Here

By WILLIAM PHILIP SIMMS

WHAT is now perhaps the world's most absolute dictatorship is not in Europe, Asia or Africa, as Americans might casually suppose, but right at Uncle Sam's front door—in the Dominican Republic.

Its lord and master is Gen. Rafael Leonidas Trujillo-Molina, commonly called Trujillo (pronounced Trooh-ee-yo). There is a cabinet, but its members are his men. And a congress, but it votes unanimously what he tells it to vote.

"My life may pay for it if I tell you these things," said Angel Morales, Dominican attorney now in exile in this country. "A friend of mine was recently shot in New York when mistaken for me. But I will not hide. Nor will I keep silent. I am not afraid.

'PRESS MUZZLED'

"A complete censorship has been clamped down on the island. There is no such thing there as a free press or free speech. No Dominican newspaper would dare print anything hostile to the regime. The editor would pay for his indiscretion with his life.

"From Cuba sometimes the radio broadcasts criticisms of the dictator. People in Santo Domingo, like people in this country, love to listen in. But the moment the name Trujillo is heard coming thru, they don't wait to hear more. They spring up and shut it off, or switch to another station.

"It isn't that they are fond of the dictator. They are afraid neighbors or passersby might hear an anti-Trujillo speech coming out of the house and report its inmates as enemies. And that might bring fatal consequences.

CHILD GETS COLONEL'S PAY

"The dictator is the law of the land. He has a 5-year-old son named after him. Trujillo made him a full-fledged colonel of the army or constabulary—not just an honorary colonel such as the Czar made the little Czarevitch, but a regular one.

"The 'Little Colonel's' appointment was published in the official gazette. He receives $400 a month salary, wears a full colonel's uniform, and wherever he goes soldiers and junior officers click their heels and salute him."

President Trujillo rose from obscurity. He was a sergeant of constabulary when the American Marines were in occupation. Later he rose to captain, colonel, and second in command. In 1930, by a coup d'etat, he put a temporary president in office, then held an election and made himself chief of state.

"There are no longer any political parties in Santo Domingo," said Morales. "There is only one party and that is Trujillo's. So, when Trujillo ran for re-election last year, no one else received a vote. He was re-elected unanimously. He doubtless will continue to be, every four years hereafter.

AIDED BY NEW DEAL

"It was the 'Old Deal' that put Trujillo in power. But it is the 'New Deal' that keeps him there. The American policy of 'the good neighbor' is splendid. But in this particular case Washington's determination not to intervene in any way is playing into the hands of a tyrant."

The republic occupies the eastern two-thirds of the Island of Santo Domingo, second largest of the Greater Antilles group. It is in the Caribbean between Cuba and Puerto Rico. Haiti occupies the western end.

In size it is about 19,000 square miles, twice as big as Vermont, and it is very fertile. Its population is estimated at 1,200,000, of mixed European, Indian and Negro races. Spanish is spoken.

Today, over that corner of the southern sea hovers an ominous silence. Almost no news comes out. Nothing critical of the regime goes in. But in New York, Havana, Europe and elsewhere hundreds of exiles are waiting and wondering when a turn will come and they can go home.
