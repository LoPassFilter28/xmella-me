BACKGROUND OF EVENTS

BY PAUL V. COLLINS.

When the Senate ruled a few days ago that a certain appeal from Haiti (which Senator King of Utah proposed to read) was out of order because all our foreign relations must pass through the President via the Department of State, the suppression piqued curiosity. The appeal was referred to the committee on foreign affairs, which action took it off the floor of the Senate.

According to the Haitians and their champions, the appeal, in the form of a letter addressed to Senator King, disclosed a chapter of American imperialism. The writer of the appeal, Percival Thoby of Haiti, states:

"The 61 committee of the Union Patriotique have intrusted me with the mission to lay these facts before you:

"The indictment of present conditions in the Haitian republic falls under five main heads:

"1. Popular elections deferred in violation of the constitution, and election of the Haitian President by the unlawfully constituted council of state.

"2. Depreciation of Haitian currency and maladministration of the national debt.

"3. Changes in land laws, depriving peasants of their land, resulting in increased emigration of Haitian workers to Cuba and elsewhere.

"4. Increase of prostitution and social evils, owing to military occupation.

"5. Secret and unlawful extension from 10 to 20 years of the treaty of 1916, between the United States and Haiti."

* * * *

The Thoby appeal is a bit confusing to one not previously familiar with the story of the United States relations with Haiti, but the story is given more in sequence by Rev. Father Raymond A. McGowan, who has recently returned from that country, and whose facts are discussed by M. Thoby.

Father McGowan thus summarizes the history:

"1. Free government and civil liberty have been destroyed.

"2. The City National Bank of New York, a Rockefeller institution, has acquired complete dominion over Haitian finances.

"3. A nation of independent farmers is rapidly being transformed into a nation of plantation laborers, and their farms are being absorbed into plantations owned by American corporations."

Father McGowan cites the fact that an investigating committee of the United States Senate reported in 1921 that 1,500 Haitians had been killed by the United States Marines, including women and children, but the committee justified the killing as "necessary to preserve order." The Navy Department officially stated at the same time that there had been "no unnecessary force used." According to Father McGowan, to the Marines the rebellious natives are "bandits"; to the Haitians they are "patriots," fighting for their country's independence.

* * * *

Our relations with Haiti began in 1891, when our Navy sought a base on Mole St. Nicholas, which was refused by the Haitian government, on the ground that it would impair Haitian sovereignty.

Since 1881, the finances of Haiti had been managed by the National Bank of Haiti. The bank was reorganized in 1910 and certain French banks acquired an interest in it. Later, our Secretary of State, Mr. Knox, called a conference of banks in New York, at which he suggested that some American banks should be included in the Haiti bank, and, following his advice, several New York banks bought its stock.

When the World War broke out, Secretary of State Bryan urged that American interests should acquire all the European holdings in Haitian finance, and the National City Bank of New York bought the French stock and subsequently bought all American interests.

It was then that the United States proposed to Haiti that this Government take over control of all Haitian taxes, collecting and expending them and thus insuring interest to the American bank.

This advice was five times rejected by Haiti.

In the midst of a revolution United States Marines landed at Port au Prince, the capital; took possession of the Bank of Haiti, removed its funds, $500,000, to the gunboat, and thence to deposit in the National City Bank, in New York. This account is verified by testimony of Roger L. Farnham, president of the National City Bank, before the Senate investigating committee. It was justified as necessary, in order to protect the stockholders, the National City Bank owning the controlling interest in the National Bank of Haiti.

* * * *

In the revolution of 1915 President Sam of Haiti was killed by a mob and United States Marines took possession of the capital to restore order. By direction of Secretary Bryan, Admiral Caperton, in command, demanded that the Haitians elect a President who would consent to the United States control of customs and to American advice in foreign affairs. This resulted in the election of President Dartiguenave, who made such a treaty.

The Haitian Congress, or Assembly, was dismissed. When the time came for a new election of a President by the Assembly, according to the constitution, the President of Haiti, instead of following the constitutional channels, decreed that a council of state, appointed by the President, should act as adviser. Subsequently this council of state, instead of the Assembly, was given, by decree, the power to elect the President.

A new constitution was prepared by Assistant Secretary of the Navy Franklin D. Roosevelt and adopted by Haiti, by decree of its President, acting as dictator.

* * * *

The council of state elected President Borno in 1922. It is claimed that he is ineligible to serve as President, since he was the son of alien French, although he was born in Haiti. The decree establishing the council of state was for a term of 10 years, which term has now expired, but President Borno, a candidate for re-election next April 12, has decreed its extension for another 10 years, and it is alleged he has filled several vacancies on it with his own relatives and cabinet officials and expects it to re-elect him. In the meanwhile the people have not been permitted to vote for a new Assembly, whose constitutional function it is to elect the President.

* * * *

The treaty of 1915 with the United States has been extended by presidential decree until 1936, without coming before our Senate. This extends our control over Haitian finances. The National City Bank is negotiating an additional loan of $16,000,000 to Haiti, the bids for the loan having been advertised by our State Department. In 1922 the Haitian foreign debt was $6,971,874.

* * * *

Haiti has a population of 3,000,000, mostly small farmers. In 1924 there was an emigration of 21,013 from Haiti to Cuba; in 1925 there were upward of 30,000 emigrated to Cuba, and many more to the Dominican Republic. Since American occupation, it is stated, 150,000 natives have migrated, so that today there is a scarcity of labor.

It is alleged that President Borno has decreed a policy of confiscating land holdings of the peasants, where they are unable to show deeds, and, as the ignorant peasants have occupied the land for generations without recording titles, many are losing their farms, the land being then bought by Americans for extensive plantations on which rubber is to be exploited. In addition to the financial interests of the National City Bank of New York, there are large holdings of land by the Haitian-American Sugar Co., the Haitian-American Pineapple Co., and the United West Indies Trading Co.—all financed by American investors, now owning in excess of 200,000 acres and employing laborers at 30 cents a day.
