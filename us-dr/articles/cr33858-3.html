The Response to Coups d'Etat

This Government has been obliged to fashion its response to the recent coups d'etat in South America amid a flood of emotion that reflects the frustrations on Latin affairs and obscures the realities with which the policy-makers must contend.

Much of the emotion has overlooked the basic point that the United States is not omnipotent in the internal affairs of these countries and must encourage their stability without exerting a heavy-handed authority that will feed the Communist charge of "Yankee imperialism."

It is one thing to regret, as most officials did, the eclipse of a fragile democratic government in the Dominican Republic and another to propose, as Senator Gruening, Democrat of Alaska, did, that the United States return the deposed Dominican President, Juan Bosch, to Santo Domingo by warship.

Middle Course Sought

It is one thing to use the weapons of aid and diplomatic to discourage the emergence of military juntas and another to refuse completely and forever to deal with the military governments after they have become accomplished facts.

The administration has had to seek a rational course between fervent emotions on the left and right. The line of the left is that the President does not deal severely enough with the military insurgents to discourage their imitation. The argument of the right is that it will be disastrous to ignore the capacity of the military to provide interim stability in countries where constitutional stability has faltered.

Liberals maintain that the officers are acting as agents of the interests to avoid the reforms that are essential to the objectives of the Alliance for Progress and the future of the continent. Conservatives argue that they seize power because they are responding responsibly to the danger that unstable civilian governments will fall to Communist subversion.

The heat of the debate has tended to obscure the necessity and virtue of forming separate judgments on each military coup in the light of the circumstances in which it takes place. It is possible, in the words of one high official, "to go to hell in a basket with a constitutional government." Few have sincerely bemoaned the juntas' replacements of a drunken President in Ecuador and an unpromising President in Guatemala. In both places the new regimes seem to be trying to do the right thing.

Juntas in the Argentine and Peru have accomplished their commitments to hold elections and restore constitutional governments. No one can say with certainty that conditions in these countries are now worse than they would have been if the military had not taken power in moments of crisis.

The Dominican army's overthrow of Bosch, whose popularity was marginal, is clearly less menacing to Latin stability than the danger that the military in Venezuela will try to take power from Romulo Betancourt, a resilient politician who enjoys heavy popular support. Betancourt has become a symbol of the new era and the administration would be willing to contemplate an intervention in his behalf that has not seemed worth the risk in the other places.

But intervention is no certain answer, because a politician maintained in power by foreign forces would hold slim prospects of future standing with his people. He would have the fatal aspect of a puppet.

Tough, Cautious

Short of this drastic step, the administration has found its most constructive response to the military coups d'etat in a process of tough and cautious negotiation. The benefits of aid and recognition are bargained hard for pledges by the military regimes to avoid autocratic abuses, pursue reforms, and schedule elections.

After the elections are held and the new civilian governments are formed, as in Peru and Argentina, the Alliance for Progress has the opportunity of assisting the new leaders to develop and expand their stature through economic and social progress.

The lesson of the experience to date is that there is no doctrinaire answer to the problem of the military coups. In South America as in South Viet Nam, the United States can encourage democracy but cannot impose it.
