'Building' Envoy Prepares to Build Another Residence

By EVELYN PEYTON GORDON

Henry Norweb, our new Ambassador to Peru, might be called the "building envoy." No sooner had he gotten the new legation at Ciudad Trujillo, Dominican Republic, well under way than his duty as Minister to that country terminated, with a nice little promotion to the Lima post. And Henry never even lived in the new residence whose construction he nursed and watched during his days in the little republic.

The end of this month the Norwebs will set sail for Lima. And shortly after their arrival plans and drawings for a new residence will follow. The appropriation—$100,000, I believe, to be the figure—is all in order, but as yet no plans have been submitted. So it'll be up to Henry, after he sees what he thinks is needed in the way of offices and a residence to pick the best. We've owned a nice piece of property on the Avenida de Mira Flores for some time, but our envoys have been living in a hired house. "Enough of that," we're saying more and more, as more and more our ministers are raised to ambassadors and our relations with foreign countries become more and more important.

PLAN OTHER EDIFICES

According to information from the State Department, we've got a lot of irons in the embassy-and-legation-building fire. Down in Montevideo, Uruguay, the fine new residence is nearly finished. Governmental building moves in a leisurely way. The appropriations for that legation were made, plans approved, and all in order, way back in the early '30s, when the late Butler Wright was our envoy to Montevideo. But it's Edwin C. Wilson who will first occupy the house—unless there's a hitch and Mr. Wilson has become an ambassador ere the move is made.

George Messersmith, who recently sailed for his duties as Ambassador to Cuba, may be the first occupant of the new embassy we are planning in Havana—out in the beautiful country-club district near Marianao. But there again it takes lots of time. In Rio de Janeiro our new embassy offices are nearing completion and soon work will start on the residence with Jefferson Caffery standing by to see that things go along. That is one of our most beautiful properties, overlooking the bay of Rio with its hundreds of islands and a fringe of mountains.

Other projects in the offing are legations at Porto Prince, Haiti; Managua, Nicaragua, and an embassy in Panama. Looks as tho Mrs. Warren Delano Robbins, whose job it is to see that draperies and furnishings are in good taste, that iceboxes are adequate and kitchen utensils complete, has some busy months ahead of her.

PLANNING TOUR

In fact, Mrs. Robbins is planning to leave later this spring for a tour thru South America to line up the needs for each new residence. It's on her return that the real work will begin. So if you see the fair lady talking to herself or hear muttering "yards, feet and inches," you'll know what it's all about.

"What building are we doing in Europe?" I had the temerity to ask the department yesterday. I'd been thinking of that nice old Bleucher palace, we bought in Berlin that's been standing awaiting the first renovations for more than a year. "Well, of course, there's the legation in Helsinki," they told me. "Where Arthur Schoenfeld was planning to spend next summer. That was nearly finished. And as far as I can figure the Russians are finishing it for us. Our pal, Joe Stalin!"

WARY OF EUROPE

So, with that as just one experience in this turbulent world, we aren't sinking any more money in Europe for properties or buildings. In the old days when our envoys were fewer and our relations not very vital, we just went out and hired a house to suit the purse of our ministers. But now we are a great, big, grown-up country and it is more fitting for us to own our houses.

Can we help it if the beautiful Schonebrunn Palace in Prague is lying idle? Or that our Charge d'Affaires in Berlin lives in an apartment at the Hotel Adlon? Or that the scaffolding in Helsinki is lower than the ground these days? No, we can't. But we're just not taking any more chances on that side of the world. Not at present, anyhow.
