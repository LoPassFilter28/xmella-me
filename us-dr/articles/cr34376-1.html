What Jamaica Thinks of Annexation.

The Kingston (Jamaica) Morning Journal of March 14, 1871, has the following editorial on the San Domingo Commission:

Now that the United States Commissioners to Santo Domingo are in this city, the time seems propitious to say a word or two on the object of their mission—the annexation of the Dominican republic to the United States. Time was when such a measure would have appeared to us fraught with calamity to these islands of the West Indies, or at least to that class of their population comprising the great majority of their citizens. That time has passed by, however. A mighty revolution, throwing into the shade all other revolutions that had ever taken place on the American continent, not even excepting that which culminated in the erection of the United Colonies into the United States of America, has recently been effected in those States, by which some four millions of the human race have been raised from a state of chattelism to the position of men—of freemen; and not only of freemen, endowed with the bare privilege of calling their lives, their limbs, and their tangible property their own, but freemen in the highest sense of the word, eligible to the elevated offices of emolument, trust, and honor in the land of their nativity.

Never has a political mission ever left the United States composed of such material. Never before has any man with the least tinge of negro blood in his veins been considered eligible to fill the positions held by the two Messrs. Douglass, father and son. Thus a new era has opened up for the black and colored race in America. It will be seen that the commission has lost none of its importance, the United States none of their prestige, because of these two gentlemen being included in that commission, and having taken part in the negotiations. The Americans are a shrewd people. They have given evidence of the fact in the composition of the Dominican commission. They have displayed a tact worthy of all admiration in including the Messrs. Douglass among the representatives of the great Republic sent to treat with the people of a race to which these two gentlemen are kindred, in a matter so vitally affecting persons of that race in their own country.

It is an act of concession to that race that will be appreciated by every individual belonging to it, in both the United States and the Dominican republic, and why not throughout the entire West Indian archipelago? For the first time in the history of North America we find colored gentlemen associated with white citizens of the United States—citizens of the first rank, more than one of whom might justly aspire to the Presidency—on that ultra aristocratic platform of American Democracy, the quarter-deck of a vessel of war—in a mission, the most important that can be conceived—a mission, be it remarked, having for its object the reception into the Union of a magnificent territory, containing a population of hundreds of thousands of black and colored persons—persons of the whilom proscribed race—embracing equal rights of citizenship with any citizen of the "Old Dominion" itself, with the bluest of cavalier blood in his veins, or of any Yankee of Massachusetts claiming descent from the most round-headed Briton that ever fought or prayed in the ranks of Cromwell's Ironsides.

We trust the mission will be found to have been a most successful one. There is a guarantee for that in the personnel of the commissioners. Than Mr. Wade, or Judge Wade, as the people of America delight in calling him, we know of no one who could have been more properly intrusted with a mission, at once so delicate and so important. We are not so well acquainted—we mean by reputation—with the other gentlemen associated with the distinguished Senator and judge, save the elder Mr. Douglass. We may be sure, however, that they have been selected with care, for their scientific attainments. In fact, their association with the distinguished Senator—himself no mean scholar—is a guarantee of their proficiency for the high mission on which they are sent. We have been informed that some sensitiveness has been expressed on the part of the Haytians at Port-au-Prince in regard to the objects of the commission. We do not think they have anything to fear on the score of having the Americans as their near neighbors. On the contrary, we have reason to believe that the vicinity of an energetic, enterprising, and intelligent people will be of benefit to them, as furnishing them examples by which they can profit, not only in the art of government, but in those other arts, the knowledge of which denotes a civilized and enlightened people, in proportion as that knowledge approaches perfection.

For our part we have not the least apprehension because of the probability of the United States flag waving over territory so near to us. We have no fear of molestation on the part of our neighbors that are to be. On the contrary their proximity to this island cannot but be beneficial in the revival of trade, and the strengthening of the friendly relations which exist between the American people and the people of this island in a commercial point of view. And commercial friendship can hardly exist without good political understanding between two peoples of different nationalities. Not only may we look for an increase of American visitors to this city and the island at large, but our own people may be induced to return such visits, partly in the interests of trade and partly for pleasure. They and our people may be brought to interchange ideas. They may teach us many things with which they are familiar, and which it may be profitable for us to know, while they can learn from us those things in which we are proficient, such as the growth of the sugar cane and the manufacture of our great staples.
