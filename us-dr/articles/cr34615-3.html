Red Carpet for Tourists Spread by Dominicans

By JACQUES FUTRELLE

The Star's Travel Editor

(One of a series on Caribbean Travel.)

Most of the islands of the Caribbean lay claim to the story of Columbus' graphic way of describing the new world to Queen Isabella. In the literature of nearly every island of the Caribbean—and by literature we include the printed word enthusiasms of the travel industry—is recounted the episode in which the discoverer crumpled up a piece of paper, placed it on a table and said: "There is Santo Domingo de Guzman" (or Jamaica or Haiti or Puerto Rico, according to the fancy of the teller.)

Whatever land inspired it, the description applies well to today's Dominican Republic. It has four parallel mountain ranges in an area no bigger than New Hampshire and Vermont; also, the highest peak in the West Indies, Pico Trujillo. These cool and forested mountains serve the tourist well in summertime travels to the island. Alternative are miles and miles of white-sand shore, where some beaches have been developed into resorts.

Added to such holidaying attributes as mountains, sun, sea and sand—and air conditioning, good highways and modern hotels—is the spirit of the country. It seems to be a land where things get done. Right now, development of the tourist industry is high on the list of things to do, so Americans will hear more about it in the next decade.

Recently installed as director general of tourism is Miguel De Moya, one-time secretary of the Dominican Embassy in Washington and brother of the former ambassador, Manuel De Moya.

Four Hours Away

Although many signs in recent years point to a more favorable welcome for tourists, the island the Dominican Republic shares with Haiti has not been considered off the beaten track since Pan American World Airways began pioneering the Caribbean area.

Hispaniola of Columbus' day is sandwiched loosely between Cuba on the northwest and Puerto Rico on the east, and lies directly on the well-whipped air between Miami and San Juan. One such Pan Am flight, via Jamaica, leaves Miami at 9 a.m. and puts the traveler in Ciudad Trujillo, the Dominican capital, by mid-afternoon. A direct flight from Miami takes less than four hours.

Not too many years ago, the United States traveler as well as others, was required to go through the trouble of obtaining a visa to venture beyond the confines of the airport customs inspector. Now a tourist card, costing $2, opens the gate.

In those days, too, a customs examination could go on for hours. On my first visit there in 1948, my customs clearance was slowed immeasurably by an inspector thumbing through countless pages of travel publications I happened to be carrying. Recently, I went through in a breeze and 10 minutes later (the airport is within the city limits) I was registering at the Hotel Jaragua.

Easy entrance requirements are not the only tangible evidence that the American can expect red-carpet treatment. New hotel facilities are going up, supplementing the modern and comfortable hosteries in the cities, by the sea and in the mountains. The Dominicans expect 30,000 or more travelers this year, largely from the United States.

Finishing touches are being applied to an addition to the Hotel Jaragua, which for 10 years has held its place as queen among the Government-built hostelries. Another large hotel is under construction at the outskirts of the capital city near the site of the International Fair for Peace and Progress, which opens in December. And open it will—and on time—if the island's modern-day tradition holds. Should the necessity arise, a group of tile-setters, electricians, painters and plumbers can be pulled in from the Army to fill any skilled labor shortage.

The cobalt Caribbean laps virtually at the broad lawn of the Jaragua, a chalk-white, modernistic structure that seems to have anticipated the architectural trend of the newer city hotels in that area. It is a resort in itself, with a large, blue-bottomed swimming pool, tennis courts, tiled terraces and beautifully landscaped grounds, separate cottages for those who like the individuality such quarters give, and a casino.

Although the casino is little more than a bare room when compared with the rich draperies, gold walls and crystal chandeliers of its counterparts in Cuba and Puerto Rico, money can be lost—and made—quickly and legally. The Jaragua stakes each of its arriving guests with $5 worth of complimentary chips, which can be built into a fortune, or at least hotel expenses. Roulette chips as low as 25 cents can be played. Dice and a long battery of "one-armed bandits" provide the other ways of courting Lady Luck.

New Road by Sea

Already laced with good roads, over which shiny new American cars flash, the Dominican Republic is building a new highway that skirts the sea to the popular resort area of Boca Chica. One of the principal attractions there is the Hotel Hamaca. Sailing and swimming in the calm and clear waters draws Dominicans in great crowds over the 27 miles from Ciudad Trujillo, particularly on week ends.

Unless the sightseer wants mountain scenery and a closer look at the native life, he need not leave the clean, orderly and tranquil capital city. History can hold him within the older part of today's Ciudad Trujillo for hours. He can see the spot where stood the tree Columbus used as a mooring for his ship, inspect the ruins of the Alcazar de Colon, built by Don Diego, Columbus' son. Plans are being perfected to restore this building as accurately as possible to its original appearance, even furnish it.

The cathedral is the Basilica of Santa Maria la Menor, within easy walking distance. The fine carvings, the treasure room and the Columbus Cross are things to see within the cathedral.

Lighthouse for Columbus

Across the River Ozama from the capital, visitors can see the early stages of construction of the long-projected Columbus Memorial Lighthouse, which will contain the tomb of Columbus. The lighthouse, which will serve ships of the air and sea, is to take the form of a recumbent cross.

For a glimpse of the hustle and bustle typical of Latin America, the original part of the city is the place to wander. The narrow streets are full of cars, carts, carriages and people. Some from the country push through on little dust-colored ponies. But the new main thoroughfares are broad and beautified by either landscaping or the lush foliage growing casually.

At night, the visitor has a choice of dancing under the stars or a visit to one of several clubs, and if his choice is right, there will be air conditioning.

In summer as well as winter, the Dominican Republic has much to please the vacationer. One big handicap, however, faces any director of tourism. The Dominican Republic is quite a mouthful, as compared to such easy-to-say names as Miami, Jamaica or Ocean City. It needs a catchy nickname. "D. R." is not catchy, but it might catch on.
