Building the Dominican Republic President Bosch's Austerity Program Is Called a Brave Beginning

SANTO DOMINGO, Dominican Republic—President Juan Bosch said to me that he had not been given a government to rule. It was more like a city that had been destroyed by an earthquake.

For over 30 years the people of the Dominican Republic had been in bondage to the Trujillo dictatorship. The tyranny had been cruel and greedy. It fed on fear and washed in blood.

But its worst crime was not its brutality.

A nation can survive corruption and oppression, but its spirit may break when a tyrant for many stricken years attacks a people's self-respect and curses its dignity. For worse than the stolen treasure and the broken structure of government and the impoverished land was the lack of trust among the Dominicans themselves.

They had suffered so many spies and informers, had been betrayed by so many false leaders, that they might easily have winced away from the challenge of building a free nation on the ruins of a dictatorship. A sense of nationhood had to be restored before the nation could reform.

That was President Bosch's first task and most urgent duty. Before he could become a successful leader he had to be a great teacher. He had to teach the Dominicans that democracy could be their servant, just as Trujillo had been their master.

The lesson has taken root. After 26 years of exile Mr. Bosch, chosen as President of the Dominican Republic with an invulnerable majority, has begun a task quivering with difficulties whose end no one can see.

President Bosch has no illusions about the size of that task. He thinks it will take 25 years to finish the work of reform and redemption. But he has already made the brave beginning.

At five in the morning he comes to his office and he stays at his desk 16 hours a day. He smiles at the suggestion that he should give more duties to other officials. Where are these trusted and trained people to be found? The problems in themselves are serious, but still more serious is the shortage of people to grapple with them.

He is in a desperate race with time. To hold the confidence of the submerged masses he must produce quick results. Yet the change may only sharpen the conflict between the rich and the poor if the results are too swift and massive.

After the election, Mr. Bosch wanted to form a government of national unity. His overtures gained no response. The divisions in the country were too deep for such a gallant experiment.

He has begun his task fully aware that the Communists hope he will fail and the reactionaries are afraid he will succeed. He cannot conjure up a miracle, but with the people on his side he is ready for all storms.

President Bosch talks about people as if he is in communion with them. He came from the poor and he has never forgotten their aches and their hopes. He believes they will trust him to the end of the road. If that hope fails, his plans will crash. He admits the risk, but is undismayed. For he can see no one else in the Dominican Republic who can speak the language of the poor and who can win the trust that has been given to him.

This faith in his own star may be the secret of leadership. It can also fester into a vanity that will breed its own dangers. President Bosch does not deny the hazards. But with a quiet integrity that is very impressive, he says he will commit suicide rather than lie to the Dominican people. He asks no fairer test than to be judged by results.

President Bosch has come to office with a philosophy of government and a faith in freedom that are as rare as they are precious in Latin America today. He believes that without honesty, and without justice no democracy can survive. For without honesty, a democracy does not deserve to endure, and without social justice it cannot hope to last.

Against the challenge of Fidel Castro and of all dictators, he plans to build the barricades of freedom, and he hopes his example will be followed in other countries.

His conflict with President Francois Duvalier of Haiti has one explanation that has eluded world opinion. Dr. Duvalier and Gen. Trujillo were united by a pact of mutual convenience. The dictator of Haiti would have liked a similar arrangement with President Bosch—who was outraged by the suggestion.

Haiti is a tyranny. The Dominican Republic has had a new birth of freedom. Can one island thus be divided? There alone is the source of the tension; it will revive unless Haiti quits its bluster and ends its threats. (Haiti and the Dominican Republic share the island of Hispaniola.)

To everyone's surprise, President Bosch began not with a program of reform but with a program of austerity. No more than a quiet beginning has been made on the problems of agrarian reform and education, housing and public health.

The primary emphasis has fallen on the payment of debt, strengthening the currency, saving the budget from a swollen deficit.

In a few months from now he expects the whole country to see the wisdom of this policy which has disappointed many people and perplexed even more. The new stability will sustain the policy of reform and economic growth to which he is committed and which he intends to apply with vigor.

President Bosch knows his success will bring hope to other democratic leaders in Latin America, just as his failure will stimulate the ambitions of the forces of repression.

He intends to campaign for a stronger inter-American system devoted to human rights and no longer fettered by the doctrine of non-intervention so that it cannot act when freedom is being stifled.

The intervention would always be collective and would always be in defense of personal rights and national freedom, but that is a task for the long future. Right now he is thinking of his own country and of its deferred hopes and pitiless martyrdom.

The Dominican Republic is a small country, but President Bosch is a big man well worth watching and, one hopes, well worth following elsewhere in Latin America.
