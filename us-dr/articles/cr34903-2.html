Savannah Morning News · December 23, 188123 de diciembre de 1881

Original in English.Original en inglés.

THE SAN DOMINGO JOB IN A NEW SHAPE.

Curious Operations of an Alleged Syndicate of New York Speculators — West India Annexation Schemes.

Washington Correspondent N. Y. Commercial Bulletin.

It is strongly believed here that something is going on with reference to San Domingo. There was a treaty for the acquisition or lease, November 29, 1869, of Samana Bay to the United States. This led to the sending of General Babcock and the well known commission to the city of San Domingo. In 1871 or 1872, or somewhere about that time, General Babcock was sent as a commissioner to negotiate a treaty with the Dominican republic, with the view also of absorbing Hayti and every part of the autonomy of these two republics from the Bay of Samana to Saint Marc, and around and about to the Bay St. Nicholas la Mole. It was discovered that these two parts of the island of Santa Domingo had been in chronic revolution, and it was thought that through these Spanish and French concessions the Americans would not only utilize the entire island, but would also make such a showing, by enterprise and energy, as would make Cuba and Porto Rico drop like a ripe apple into the lap of the new empire, and make the West Indies a part of the United States. A few years before this, the Samana Bay Company, a company chartered at Albany, under the laws of the State of New York, led by men who run the steamer “Tybee,” for instance—such men as Marshall O. Roberts, Wm. R. Grace, the present Mayor of New York, Commodore Garrison, and others—undertook to make a trade between New York and San Domingo City, for the purpose of developing San Domingo and bringing hence to this country the products of that island, which were principally mahogony, coffee, and tobacco equal to the best produced and called Vuelra Abajo in the Cincho Vias of Cuba. The company had a concession from the Dominican Government, taking in the waters and islands and the frontage of Samana Bay, and a railroad grant reaching from Samana to Santa Domingo. The opposition to this scheme of the government to acquire the island brought about the well-known fight against the administration of Charles Sumner which resulted in his deposition from the Chairmanship of the Foreign Relations Committee of the United States Senate, and which fight, created a commission, consisting of Benjamin F. Wade, of Ohio, as Chairman, Professor Howe, Frederick Douglass, and a number of specialists, who were directed to proceed to Santa Domingo and investigate and report upon the character of its climate, its agricultural productions, and upon the general resources of the country. They were further directed to report upon the financial condition of the government, debts and obligations due to foreign governments or creditors, giving in detail a full statement of such debts or obligations. This commission was further instructed to report fully as to the character of all grants and concessions of whatever nature given by the Government of Dominica to any individual or corporation, particularly with respect to concessions granting lands upon the Bay of Samana. The committee's report is well known. There is a little island around there somewhere which has been bought up by a syndicate of New York speculators, who seem to have an object just now, knowing all these facts, to make a coaling station for American vessels. They are trying their best to have our government purchase it in expectation of the annexation of the West Indies. This idea is in connection with the Isthmus canal. Their agents are here and have secured already the detailing of a vessel from the North Atlantic squadron to make a thorough survey of Samana Bay. This bay was surveyed in 1870 or thereabouts, but the survey was only partial. The English Government made a survey for their own use some years ago.

At the Hydrographic Office in this city, where the navy records are kept, they say that this is a preliminary affair simply to the survey of the harbors of the world that have not been surveyed, for purposes of general information. I do not think there is anything in the statement of the Hydrographic Office in this matter of exploring West Indian harbors beyond a desire to establish a coaling station somewhere down there. Whether it be in the Bay of Samana, the Bay of St. Nicholas la Mole, or the Island of St. Thomas, or a point in Cuba, is, with the proposed coaling station itself, a matter of the future.
