New Form of Latin American Unity Arises in Puerto Rico's Rebirth

By FRED THEROUX, Star Staff Writer

SAN JUAN, Puerto Rico.—This lively little island commonwealth is discovering that its amazing economic progress is not its only attraction to the world. Increasingly, and especially since the ugly experiences of Vice President Nixon in Caracas, Bogota and Lima, the political institutions of this "Estado Libre Asociado" are getting a closer and mostly favorable look from statesmen of other nations.

In Washington, the attacks on the Nixon party last month brought dismayed calls for a "re-appraisal" of United States policy toward Latin America. Diplomats and political leaders, long accustomed to depending almost without question on the solidarity of the Western Hemisphere in international affairs, began asking the bewildered question—"Why?"

Ready at hand for the questioners was the contrast between the reception Mr. and Mrs. Nixon got in Caracas and the one they received in this capital of an American Commonwealth.

Despite a driving rain, Puerto Ricans thronged the streets along which the vice presidential party drove to La Fortaleza, ancient and beautiful residence of Puerto Rico's governors. But these Latin American crowds were all smiles, cheers and friendliness. As in Caracas, the crowds halted the motorcade—but there were no rotten eggs, rocks, decayed fruit or faces contorted by hate.

Autonomy Under Two Flags

Luis Munoz Marin, Puerto Rico's poet-governor and chief architect of the unique arrangement by which this crowded little island enjoys complete internal autonomy under two flags—the Stars and Stripes and the flag of Puerto Rico—welcomed the Nixons with true Hispanic regrets that insults had been offered to a lady. The evening at La Fortaleza was a great success and a great relief for the Nixons. But it has turned out to be much more than that.

What Gov. Munoz hopes for as a result of the Nixon incidents is a wider appreciation of the opportunity afforded United States foreign policy in the example of Puerto Rico. This once-Spanish colony is both thoroughly Latin and thoroughly American. In the years since the present Commonwealth was established, by a compact between the United States Congress and the insular government, ratified by the Puerto Rican people in a referendum, Puerto Rico has served as both a window and a showcase.

As a window, it gives American policymakers a good look at Latin American methods, hopes, aspirations, accomplishments and limitations. It demonstrates in concrete terms that the nations of Central and South America need not be constantly torn by revolutions, governed by "caudillos" who fight only for power and wealth, nor impoverished by widespread and grand-scale corruption. And it provides a Spanish-speaking American culture as a ready-made link between North America and the Latin nations to the south.

Thousands Visit Islands

As a showcase, it has been enormously popular to visitors from all over the world anxious to see what the Puerto Rican economy has done under the vigorous "Fomento" program, administered by an energetic and enthusiastic man who won't slow down even when the doctors tell him he must, Teodoro Moscoso.

Literally, a steady stream of thousands of official and semi-official visitors have come to Puerto Rico over the last few years, representing countries all over the world. For the most part, they have come from nations and areas where the problems of colonialism and economic underdevelopment have been similar to those faced by Puerto Rico.

But the amazing successes of the Puerto Ricans in lifting themselves out of a morass of poverty have been described many times, always with overtones of incredulity that an island almost totally devoid of any natural resources "except people," as Gov. Munoz is fond of saying, could have achieved the results of the last 10 years.

Increasingly, visitors from other parts of the world are showing interest in the political institutions that have made "Fomento" and "Operation Bootstrap" possible, while preserving for the citizens of Puerto Rico a freedom politically under an honest and efficient representative government probably unmatched in any similarly economically unfavored region in the world.

A week ago, for example, Dr. Cheddi B. Jagan, leader of the majority party in British Guiana, visited La Fortaleza en route to London. Dr. Jagan, who would bear the title of Prime Minister if British Guiana achieved a change from its present status as a crown colony, has been a very controversial figure in the past. He has been labeled a Communist, as has his American-born wife, the former Janet Rosenberg of Chicago. In 1953, his elected government was deposed by London authorities, the Guianan constitution suspended, and all power ordered exercised by the Crown-appointed governor.

Wants Self-Government

The constitution, with major modifications, has been reinstated, and Dr. Jagan's party continues to win elections. But the handsome young East Indian denies that his party—the People's Progressive—is either Communist or Marxist. What he wants for his country, he told this writer, is a constitutional system similar to Puerto Rico's, by which British Guiana could be self-governing within the British Commonwealth. With that achieved, Dr. Jagan said, "we could promote a 'Fomento' program for Guiana that could be enormously successful, because we have immense resources in minerals—how immense we don't even know because our country is only half-explored."

Dr. Jagan was on his way to a conference with officials of the British Colonial Office, at which he was to seek approval of long-term loans and credits for British Guiana's economic development. Under the crown colony status, British Guiana cannot arrange directly with international lending agencies or with other governments for such assistance, but must operate through the Colonial Office. He contrasted that unwieldy and time-consuming system with the brisk efficiency of the Puerto Rican system—and took pains to stress his admiration for the "incorruptibility" of the Puerto Rican government.

Elsewhere in the West Indies, the Puerto Rican example has been a galvanizer also. The British West Indies Federation, established this year, hopes through measures similar to Puerto Rico's to raise the standards of living in Jamaica, Barbados, Antigua, Dominica and the other former "possessions" associated in the new federation. (Per capita income in Jamaica, for example, is estimated at $252 annually; in Puerto Rico it is $443, a dramatic advance from the $121 of 1940.)

French West Indies

In the French West Indies a similar move is already under way. Officials from Fort-de-France, the beautiful capital of the island of Martinique, are now in Paris seeking major changes in the relationship between the French West Indies and the mother country. Their prospects of success appear good, particularly since the residents of French West Indian lands already enjoy a large measure of independence. Visitors to Martinique and Guadeloupe are invariably impressed by the "we are Frenchmen" attitude of residents of many races and colors—an attitude clearly not an affectation but a profound conviction.

But curiously, the French West Indies delegation in Paris is not stressing Puerto Rico's example in talks with the De Gaulle regime. In private conversations, Martinique leaders are lavish in their praise of Gov. Munoz and his achievements, both political and economic, of Puerto Rico in the last decade. But, as one man observed with a grin, "We aren't about to wave the American flag at De Gaulle!"

The Netherlands areas of the West Indies, with Surinam (Dutch Guiana) already enjoy internal autonomy and are well under way with extensive economic development. Unlike Puerto Rico, Aruba, Curacao and Surinam are rich in resources, thus simplifying the problems of standard-raising. But the Dutch and their former Colonials have accepted the Puerto Rican formula: Internal sovereignty in association with the mother country, plus economic development programs made possible by the autonomy plus the tariff protections of the association.

Influencing Students

Gov. Munoz hopes to make the most of his big successes for his little island. At the University of Puerto Rico, students from all over Central and South America can come to study nuclear physics as well as labor leadership. Seminars in these and other vital fields are held regularly, at the Rio Piedras and Mayaguez campuses of the university, and while the students learn their speciality, they can also observe the way free institutions can operate in a nation similar in language and culture and background to their own.

When they return to Chile or Costa Rica or Venezuela—or wherever their homeland—they will bear with them the things they have learned. Since many of these young men and women will be the leaders of the future in their countries, the influence of Puerto Rico's freedom and progress must be considerable.

"Reappraisal" of American policy toward Latin America, brought to the fore by the unhappy experiences of the Nixon party, has so far been confined to the Congress and to individuals. Many officials here fear "foot-dragging" in the State Department will lose a chance for the United States to make of all Latin America a loyal and dependable ally in peace and war.

Fear Dictator's Attitude

Of particular concern to Puerto Rican leaders, and as well to statesmen in other West Indian areas, is the "equivocal" attitude of the State Department to Latin American dictatorships. Continued tolerance, and even encouragement, of dictatorial regimes, they warn, means a dangerous weakening in United States prestige among those elements, growing swiftly, who would like to see political freedom and economic progress such as Puerto Rico enjoys become the rule for all the West Indies, and Central and South America.

There are indications that strong forces in the Eisenhower administration, with some silent but eager support among State Department officials, are pressing strongly for major alterations in United States policy toward our southern neighbors. Vice President Nixon has spoken with vigor on the subject several times since his return to Washington, with considerable support from among Senators and interested executive departments.

But Puerto Rican leaders, with grimly wry humor, like to recall the remarks broadcast to all the Caribbean area by the Dominican Republic's radio transmitter after Mr. Nixon's experience. The Vice President, said Dictator Trujillo's spokesman, "must have been referring to the dictatorship of Gov. Luis Munoz Marin over the unhappy people of Puerto Rico" when he called for an end to dictatorship in Latin America.
