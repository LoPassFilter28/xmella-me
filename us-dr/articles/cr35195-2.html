The Cecil Whig · October 5, 18725 de octubre de 1872

Original in English.Original en inglés.

THE SAN DOMINGO QUESTION.

WHAT PRESIDENT GRANT HAD TO DO WITH IT.

To the Editors of the Boston Daily Advertiser:

The annexation of San Domingo is not at present a party question. The Philadelphia platform did not endorse it. The Cincinnati and Baltimore did not oppose it. The New York Tribune has heretofore favored it. Other Liberal and Democratic papers have been neutral in regard to it.

* * * * * * * * *

In his farewell speech on the Presidential election, Senator Sumner made the following specific charges against the President:

1. That he had perpetrated an outrage upon Hayti and menaced her independence with war, in violation of international law.

2. That he kept our navy hovering on the Haytian coast "like a hawk sailing in air," and has employed United States ships of war, at great expense, in maintaining "the usurper Baez" in power, thus acting in flagrant violation of the Federal Constitution.

3. That, through his agents, he has connived at and prolonged the imprisonment of Mr. Hatch, "an American citizen," and that Hatch was "immured" because it was feared that, if released, he would write against annexation.

4. That he has insulted Frederick Douglass, first because Mr. Douglass was excluded from the Commissioners' table on board of a Potomac steamer, and again because Mr. Douglass was omitted from an invitation to a dinner given to the Commissioners at the Presidential mansion.

The facts are briefly these:

Spanish Santo Domingo comprises two thirds (22,000 square miles) of the island.—The Government de facto, through its Minister Plenipotentiary, formaily applied to President Grant three years ago; proposing annexation to the United States as a territory, upon the sole condition of our assuming its national indebtness, which was estimated as a million and a half dollars. The President, before entertaining the proposition, sent down his private secretary, Gen. Babcock, ann honorable and reliable man, to ascertain the competency and stability of the Dominician Government, the sentiment of the Dominican people in regard to annexation, the amount of the debt, and the value of the territory. Being fully satisfied upon all these points, two treaties were negotiated by the State Department—one for the lease of the peninsula and magnificent Bay of Samana at a rent of $150,000 a year, the other for the annexation of the whole country; the former to terminate upon the ratification of the latter, and both subject to the approval of Congress. The matter was brought before Congress in due time, and an annexation was recommended by the President, sustained by facts and arguments which have since been reiterated and sustained by the report of the Commissioners appointed to investigate the subject.

These negotiations were frank and explicit. So far from shunning publicity, the President called in person upon Senator Sumner at his own house, and in presence of Colonel Forney, asked for his co-operation. Mr. Sumner returned an evasive answer, which both the President and Col. Forney understood to be affirmative and favorable. Soon afterwards, without previous remonstrance, without any effort to induce the Administration to forego or modify its plans, Senator Sumner publicly assailed the Administration and the President as guilty of a violation of all laws, human and divine.

1. The alleged outrage upon Hayti occurred as follows: No sooner was it known that a negotiation for annexation was pending than preparations were made by the Haytian authorities to invade their feeble neighbor, instigated to this course by the deplomatic representatives of certain European Powers, who were jealous of American suprmacy. Hayti, which comprises less than one-third of the island (10,000 square miles,) has repeatdly sought to subjugate the whole (32,000 square miles.) She feared that Santo Domingo would forever escape from her clutches. The population of Hayti (500 000) is four fold that of Santo Domingo (125,000,) and is governed by a military despotism under the forms of a republic. Hayti was possessed of an armed steamer. The seaports of Santo Domingo, protected by only two small schooners, were liable too bombardment. The crisis was iminent, and the Government of Santo Domingo appealed to our Government for protection against a danger growing out of this very negotiation. Congress was not in session at the time.

What did the Administration do? Just this: In the interest of peace and humanity it notified the Haytian authorities that a preliminary treaty of annexation had been negotiated, and that, until Congress should have expressed its decision, an invasion of the Dominician territory by any foreign Power would be regarded as an act unfriendly to the United States.

Was this an outrage? Was it a violation of international law? So far from it, any fair-minded man, no matter what his views concerning annexation may be, would say that it was a duty. To have done less or to have done otherwise, under the circumstances, would have been a base desertion of the gallant little nation whose only crime was its aspiration to merge itself in the great American Republic.

2. With a sagacious knowledge of human nature, the President prevented any necessity of forcible intervention, and kept the peace between Hayti and Santo Domingo, by sending several American vessels of war (which would otherwise have been cruising at equal expense in the Mediterranean or upon the South American coast) not into Haytian, but into Dominician waters, at the request of the Dominician Government. Not a gun was fired, not a sailor was landed upon Haytian territory. Nor was the Dominician Government, which Senator Sumner so insultingly terms "the usurper Baez," upheld by our fleet. No American interference in the internal affairs of Santo Domingo was offered, nor would it have been welcomed.—The presence of American vessels was valued only as an evidence of sympathy and as a guaranty against foreign invasion; their subsequent withdrawal in deference to the clamor of the opposition was followed by no popular uprising. The Government of President Baez, after the lapse of years, is to-day stronger and more popular than ever. Was it a violation of international law for the Russian fleet to visit the harbor of New York? Just as much so, and no more, was the weicome presence of American ships of war in the Bay of Mansanillo and the port of Santo Domingo!

3. The Congressional committee of investigation have demonstrated that Mr. Hatch, though a native of Connecticut, afterwards claimed that he was a naturalized subject of Spain. Being a resident of Santo Domingo he was subject, as such, to Dominician laws, and was legally tried and sentenced to death for actual participation in civil war and revolution. Nevertheless, at the intercession of the American Consul, his sentence was commuted to imprisonment, and he was finally released at the request of our Government. The Dominician authorities frankly avow that his imprisonment was prolonged by them because they did not choose to be subject to his calumnies. This they had a perfect right to do, nor had we any right to insist upon his release.

The appointment of Mr. Douglass as a Secretary of the Commission was an honor, not an insult. Every one who accompanied the Commissioners will testify that he was treated by the Commissioners with the consideration and respect to which he is so justly due. He was one of the five or six gentlemen who dined with the Commissioners at the Captain's table on board the Tennessee, to which most of the correspondents and members of the scientific corps could not be admitted for want of room. While on the island Mr. Douglass resided with the Commissioners, and accompanied them upon the most intimate and confidential footing. Just before reaching Washington the Captain of a Potomac steamer, over whom the Commissioners had no control, refused to allow Mr. Douglass a seat at his table, whereupon the Commissioners and every member of their party, with one exception, resented the insult by leaving the table.—That exception, by a singular coincidence, was a reporter of the New York Tribune, who retained his seat. But no one charges the Tribune with having insulted the colored race in the person of Mr. Douglass.—The only reason why Mr. Douglass was not invited by the President to dine with the Commissioners was that Mr. Douglass was not one of the Commissioners. But let Mr. Douglass speak for himself. He says:

"I never was received by any gentleman in the United States with more kindness, more cordiality, I may say with more confidence—never felt more at home in the presence of any gentleman—than I have in the presence of this true and steady man. You know what he is to-day what he was yesterday and what he will be to-morrow, for he does not turn with every wind of doctrine, and for that reason we want him."

Since Mr. Sumner has made so great a mistake in the case of Mr. Douglass, the public may well pause before crediting the other charges which are contradicted by the three Commissioners—viz: Dr. Samuel G. Howe, Hon. B. F. Wade and Hon. A. D. White—also by Hon. Allen C. Burton, Frederick Douglass, General Sigel, Major Hazzard, Dr. Necomb and many other gentlemen who acocmpanied the Commission. Such charges, if made by men of less eminence, would be severely characterized. As it is, they constitute a case of misconception and consequent misstatement unparalleled in our political history.

The great grossness of the charges will create a reaction of the public mind. I confess that my own regard for the character and services of President Grant is stirred into enthusiasm by my keen sense of the scandalous injustice of aspersions which he has too much self-respect and reticence to deny. But above all, I feel indignant at the opprobrium which has been cast upon the Government and people of San Domingo, who deserve better treatment from the friends of the colored race.

Respectfully,

HENRY B. BLACKWELL.

Boston, September 10, 1872.
