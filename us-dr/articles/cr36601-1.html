Americus Times-Recorder · June 11, 191911 de junio de 1919

Original in English.Original en inglés.

MARINES TAKE CIVILIZATION TO ISLE OF HAITI

NEW YORK, June 11.—Marines as missionaries! Men of arms teaching the arts of peace!

It is a new idea under the sun and one that the shades of Kubla Khan, Alexander the Great, Caesar, Napoleon and the other great warriors, if they ever could revisit the earth, could scarcely credit.

Yet the United States Marines in Haiti and the Dominican Republic are real and effective missionaries, according to the Rev. Arthur Gray, D. D., Secretary for Latin America of the Board of Missions of the Protestant Episcopal church, who has just returned from a trip to that part of the world.

There are quite a number of Marines in Haiti and the Dominican Republic. They are down there teaching the people how to manage their finances, how to build roads, how to establish schools, how to fight disease—and incidentally, how to preserve law and order so that the national habit of sporadic revolutions may be overcome.

Haiti has long been a country of brigands—of plunderers in high office of the people. One official issued a government loan and, after selling the bonds, skipped out with the proceeds. Foreign buyers of this kind of insecurity got "sore" and a European government was on the point of taking an action which would have resulted in the loss of her autonomy to Haiti if the United States had not stepped in.

By the terms of the treaty of 1915 between Haiti and the United States this country "occupied" Haiti for the purpose of teaching its people to become independent and free from foreign influence. We were given the right to have certain officials at Port-au-Prince, capital of the republic. One of these was a financial adviser through whom, and subject to whose approval, the revenues of the Haitian government are expended. Another is a general of gendarmerie who directs and trains the native constabulary; Commander Gayler of the United States navy is in charge of all public works in Haiti while Commander McClean is in charge of all sanitation. Brigadier General A. W. Catlin, who recently served in France and who is the author of one of the recent war books, "With The Help of God and a Few Marines," is now in charge of the United States forces in Haiti.

Aiding them are a body of United States Marines. A number of these have been appointed officers in charge of the native constabulary. They protect the people from brigands, build roads from one town to another, repair and keep clean the city streets, aid in projects to improve sanitation—particularly by engaging in warfare with the disease-breeding mosquito—and become a sort of Big Brother to the natives.

These marines are missionaries, and prepare the way for the missionaries later sent out by the various churches, by teaching the peoples among whom they are sent that the United States is their friend, has stepped in and been spending money and giving the lives of her sons with no expectation of reimbursement, and familiarizing them with American ideals of law and order, cleanliness and democratic education.

"Only a few years ago and even today," said Dr. Gray, "one can journey through Haiti without seeing any men. The women till the soil and carry the products of the plantations to the markets. The men stay at home keeping carefully within the doors lest they should be impressed into military service by some roving revolutionist. This condition is improving since the United States took hold of Haiti's finances, public works and police in 1915 and the farmer is beginning to appear from his long hiding in his home and beginning to work again.

"In Port-au-Prince docks have been built, the streets have been paved, roads have been built, war has been made on the mosquitoes, dispensaries established, schools cleaned and bettered and disease reduced by the enforcement of quarantine regulations. A surgeon who is a commander in the United States Navy, Commander McClean, is in charge of all sanitation. He has out $1,600 per month for sanitary work among more than 2,000,000 people. He needs trained nurses and pyhsicians to help him preserve the public health. I very much hope that he may obtain both nurses and physicians and thus bring to pass that the nation-wide campaign of the Protestant Episcopal church in developing the spiritual life of the church may not only succeed but may indirectly add to the efficiency of the Missionary Marines.

"I also earnestly hope the work of our church in educating the boys and girls of Haiti will be aided through this campaign. Haiti's great need is education for her people. President Dartenave, his minister of education, and other officials asked me while I was in the island to establish trade schools. Funds have been lacking. If the members of the churches are awake to the needs of the world and the church's opportunity, money will be forthcoming for this work as a result of the nation-wide campaign.

"The church must co-operate with the United States and with the marines in making the Haitiens a strong self-sustaining nation, free from foreign interference. We don't want to keep our troops there a minute longer than necessary and the length of time largely depends upon what the church is willing to do in Haiti and the Dominican Republic. The sooner church workers go south the sooner the marines can come north. The marines cannot come home until the Haitian people have been taught the art of self-control and those other virtues without which a republic will not work. Policemen cannot build character and that is where the church must come in. When she is well established the pioneer missionaries—the marines—can come home."
