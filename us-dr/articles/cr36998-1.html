DOMINICAN REPUBLIC, 1962

Caribbean Atlantic Airlines deserves a high compliment for its program of cementing ties between the United States and the Dominican Republic.

The company has made it possible for Dominican leaders to visit Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands; and in return prominent citizens from the two American island groups were enabled to visit the picturesque Spanish island. Recently government and business leaders from St. Thomas and prominent hotel and tour personnel from Puerto Rico were guests of Caribair in a complimentary flight.

These trips will have far-reaching effects on the two nations in many significant ways. Particularly will it have a salutary effect upon the fiscal affairs of Santo Domingo, which is emerging from political and economic chaos and entering a new stage of growth and development.

As a guest of the airlines and of the Dominican Republic in the recent visit to the island, we viewed the "new" nation with mixed feelings. We enjoyed the trip and were gratified to make many friends.

We found, however, that the elegant hotels abounding in the island are either closed or virtually empty. One wonders where all the guests are. We found the hospitable, willing service personnel somewhat strangely unprepared.

In a few years the Dominican Republic will become an important segment of the tourism industry in the Caribbean, but there is still a program of reconstruction necessary before that can be achieved.

In some ways the people seem to be mentally and emotionally unsettled. It is difficult for an American to become accustomed to the presence of armed guards about.

One gets the impression that there is still an anti-climax of some sort to be reached before the people, severely shaken by the sudden turn of political events, can settle down to the hard work of renabilitation.

Unfortunately, in the heat of the recent conflict, the emotions of the duped citizens were given full sway, and they were allowed to destroy many things which had considerable cultural value, but which were destined to perish because they bore the stamp of Trujillo.

The island abounds in natural resources, and there are many square miles of fertile, undeveloped land. We found the people generally enthusiastic about the revitalization of the economic and social program of their nation, assisted by the "New Frontier" government.

We predict that the island, rich in culture and tradition, will have a brilliant future once it can bury the immediate past.
