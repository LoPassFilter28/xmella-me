LURED TO DEATH.

San Domingo Officials Shot by Order Prest. Heureaux.

Explanation of the Killing of Minister of War Castillo and Governor Estay — How a Revolutionary Movement Was Nipped in the Bud.

New York, May 8.—Newspapers received in this city from the republic of San Domingo, a few days ago, brought news of the execution of no less a personage than Gen. Ramon Castillo, Minister of War. El Listin Diario, a newspaper supposed to be inspired by the government, published with details an account of the shooting of Gen. Castillo and also of Gen. Jose Estay, governor of the district of Macori. Great excitement was produced by the news among the Dominicans established in New York, and nearly all of them attributed the double execution to some personal revenge of the president of San Domingo, Gen. Heureaux.

Private letters received since explain the affair in the following manner: Gen. Ramon Castillo, Minister of War, asked President Heureaux some months ago for 1,000 rifles and the necessary ammunition to suppress, as he said, an outbreak against the president, which was going to occur in the province of San Pedro Macori. He described so vividly the importance of the conspiracy, and the urgency of suppressing it with a strong hand that the 1,000 rifles were given to him, and, besides, he was ordered to go directly to San Pedro Macori and take command of the government forces. He did so, but time passed, and President Heureaux heard no more of the rebel movement. Instead of that, what he heard was that Gen. Castillo himself was conspiring against him with the aim of supporting by means of arms, his own candidacy for the presidency of the republic. Moreover, the president learned that his 1,000 rifles had been distributed among disloyal persons in the province of Macori.

Immediately after the news reached the capital orders were sent to Gen. Jose Estay, governor of Macori, to kill Gen. Castillo before he could make any attempt to raise a rebellion. A son of Governor Estay took charge of the execution of this order. He fired on Castillo one evening while the latter was taking a walk out of town, but missed him and mortally wounded a son [of] Castillo, who died a few hours later. Gen. Castillo could not recognize the murderer, who escaped in safety, but suspected that the attack came from President Heureaux. This suspicion was confirmed by a summons from the President to return immediately to the capital to take charge of important affairs in the War Department; so he refused to go. He replied frankly to Gen. Heureaux that he remembered the fate of Gen. Generoso Marchena and Gen. Abelardo Manita, who was also Minister of War in 1892, both of whom were shot, and the narrow escape of Ygnacio Maria Gonzalez, Minister of Foreign Relations, who saved his life by escaping in disguise as a passenger in a foreign steamer. Castillo added that he was well acquainted with the methods of the President. It was not Castillo himself, he asked, who, in the year 1893, shot Gen. Ysidro Pereira, governor of that same State of San Pedro Macori, obeying secret orders of the President of the republic?

Seeing that menacing attitude was not likely to be successful, and that Castillo was strong enough to resist any armed attack from the government’s troops, the President decided to employ tricks of diplomacy. He sent a letter saying that Castillo was deeply mistaken in all his fears, and that the only author of the murder of his son and the attempted murder of himself was Governor Jose Estay. He ended by offering a fair trial of the case in the capital and justice to Castillo in case the latter arrested Estay and brought him to San Domingo. Castillo was caught in the trap. Indignant at the news of Estay’s crime, he went with a strong force of his men to the Governor's house and, overcoming a slight resistance by the guard, arrested Estay and carried him on the steamer Mario Emilia to the capital. He was accompanied by some of his intimate friends.

As soon as Castillo landed he and his companions were arrested. Two days later the national Congress deprived Castillo of his office as Minister of War and at midnight of the same day he was conducted as a prisoner back again to San Pedro Macori. This time he was on board the war ship La Independencia, carrying also Gen. Ulises Heureaux, president of the republic; I. B. Pichardo, governor of the capital; David Wenceslao Figuerdo, vice president of the republic, and Jose Estay, who was not going as a prisoner, but among the intimate friends of President Heureaux. The war ship Presidente went along as escort and had on board a strong force from the battalion El Pacificador, the best military corps of the republic of San Domingo.

The party landed at La Caleta, near San Pedro Macori, and Vice President Figueredo addressed Castillo with these words: “My general, I feel very sorry to say to you that you are sentenced to death.”

“That is impossible,” answered Castillo. “How can I be sentenced to death without a trial? How can I be sentenced without my judge first hearing my defense?”

All his pleading was of no avail. The soldiers at El Pacificador fired on him and he fell dead, pierced by several bullets.

“At last justice is done,” exclaimed Jose Estay, when a doctor certified the death of Castillo.

“Not yet, sir,” answered President Heureaux, approaching him. “Another sentence has to be executed before we can say that justice is satisfied. That sentence is yours, Gen. Jose Estay.”

After these words the unfortunate Estay was carried to the place where Castillo was shot and the soldiers of El Pacificador again did their duty.

President Heureaux entered San Pedro Macori with his two war ships and his military force. He appointed a new governor and returned to San Domingo, leaving in the province not the least desire to rebel against his authority.
