Original in English.Original en inglés.

HAYTIEN REVOLUTION.

Consul Astwood on the Recent Haytien Trouble.

A Description of the Dominican Election and Revolution—General Heureaux, the Brave Warrior, and His Success—An Interesting Letter from the Haytien Consul.

Mr. T. T. Fortune has received the following, part of a letter from Mr. Astwood: SAN DOMINGO, Dec. 3, 1886. —* * * * * * The recent political contest here was interesting, and for the first time, and unfortunately so, the color-line made its appearance. The young men of this city have learned in recent years by foreign association to consider themselves white, and they have been learning gradually to discriminate. They are the educated class. The masses, who are in the majority blacks, neglected this important factor in making the nominations recently. General Heureanx, the most popular and able man of his country and a valiant military chief, was nominated as the representative of the masses. General Casimira N. de Moya was nominated as the elite candidate. Electoral committees were formed all over the country and the largest latitude possible was allowed by the government to these committees. You know that the election here is by popular vote. The Moya crowd made large and expensive demonstrations and their candidate would have been elected by an overwhelming majority. They laid great stress upon the fact that the time had come when the country could be no longer ruled by the Negro. Heureaux refused to make a single demonstration, but organized quietly, and it did seem like a wet blanket. In this city the so-called whites have a large majority. On election day Moya took up his headquarters in the inland city of La Vega and Heureaux established his in this city. During the three days of election the greatest enthusiasm prevailed, and when the result was declared it was found that Heureaux had carried the country by twenty thousand majority. Moya and his principal followers were so much surprised and chagrined that they refused to accept the result and before Congress could meet to count and declare the vote they resorted to arms as a means of gaining the control of the government. The city of La Vega revolted on the 21st of July. It was immediately followed or responded to by the District of Monti Christi and the Haytien Frontiers, under the lead of the great Chief Benito Muncion, whose will no government had dared to question or even to inquire into his gubernatorial actions. This of course gave the revolution at once formidable proportions and its success did seem inevitable.

General Heureaux, the daring, mounted his horse on the 29th of the same month, followed by one thousand men. His ranks were swelled all along the route and in the early days of August he surrounded the city of La Vega. The night preceding his well planned attack upon that city, Moya and his followers became alarmed and evacuated the city, taking refuge in the Mountains of Jarabacoa. Heureaux entered the city the next morning without firing a shot. The contest then became fearful, almost a hand to hand struggle, but this daring man pushed on, the enemy fleeing before him in every direction. He passed through the city of Moca, reached Santiago and then encountered Muncion with all of his forces in front of this important city, the largest and wealthiest of the country. Muncion fought desperately. He was fighting for his home and his fortune and the district that he had so despotically ruled ever since the dawn of this Republic; but he was forced to fly and took refuge across the Haytien frontiers, from which country he has gone into exile. He has by his foolish ambition lost all of the prestige he had gained in a lifetime. Heureaux's victory is complete and he stands before the country as one of the most remarkable men in connection with its history. I am glad of this. It is another evidence of the Negro's superior ability and tact whenever an equal chance is afforded him. I have written you at such great length that I must now bring my communication to a close. With my best regards to you and yours, I am with great respect, your friend, H. C. C. ASTWOOD.
