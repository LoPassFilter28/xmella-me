The Phillipsburg Herald reviewed the military career of General Máximo Gómez, noting his early service with Spanish forces during the Dominican annexation before becoming a leader in Cuba's ten years' war for independence. In 1887, Gómez returned to Santo Domingo to cultivate tobacco in Guayubín near Monte Cristi, where his widespread popularity quickly aroused political suspicion. Fearing that Gómez harbored presidential ambitions and planned an insurrection, President Heureaux secretly arranged for his arrest and execution.El Phillipsburg Herald repasó la carrera militar del general Máximo Gómez, destacando su servicio inicial en las fuerzas españolas durante la anexión dominicana antes de convertirse en un caudillo de la guerra de los Diez Años por la independencia de Cuba. En 1887, Gómez regresó a Santo Domingo para cultivar tabaco en Guayubín, cerca de Monte Cristi, donde su inmensa popularidad despertó pronto suspicacias políticas. Por temor a que Gómez albergara ambiciones presidenciales y tramara una insurrección, el presidente Heureaux dispuso secretamente su arresto y ejecución.

“He is what we call in Santo Domingo a Banilero, born as he was in the hostoric town of Bani, on the south-ern coast of Santo Domingo, between Santo Domingo City and Azua. He is known throughout Santo Domingo as an annexationist. In the early '60's, when Santo Domingo was annexed to Spain, through the intrigue of Gen. Santana, of Ciebo, Gomez, then a young and brave soldier, allied his destinies with the annexationists. When the war of the restoration began he took sides with the Spaniards instead of the patriots. The patriots won, the Spaniards were forced to capitulate, and Gomez, with other Dominicans, retired to Havana, with the title of Mariscal de Campo.»Él es lo que en Santo Domingo llamamos un banilejo, nacido como fue en la histórica localidad de Baní, en la costa sur de Santo Domingo, entre la ciudad de Santo Domingo y Azua. Santana, de El Seibo, Gómez, entonces un joven y valiente soldado, unió sus destinos a los anexionistas. Cuando comenzó la guerra de la restauración, se puso del lado de los españoles en lugar de los patriotas. Los patriotas vencieron, los españoles se vieron obligados a capitular, y Gómez, junto con otros dominicanos, se retiró a La Habana con el título de mariscal de campo.

“Gomez soon grew tired of Spanish rule, and when the ten years' war for Cuban independence began, under the leadership of Maceo and others, Gomez was one of the leading spirits who battled for Cuban independence. After the capitulation his political life was one of adventure and uncertainty.»Gómez pronto se cansó del dominio español, y cuando comenzó la guerra de los 10 años por la independencia cubana, bajo el liderazgo de Maceo y otros, Gómez fue uno de los espíritus principales que batallaron por la independencia cubana. Tras la capitulación, su vida política fue de aventura e incertidumbre.

“In 1887 Gomez seemingly tired of exile. Perhaps it was to delude the Spanish that he came to Santo Domingo and settled in the district of Monte Christi and began the cultivation of tobacco in Guayabin, near the Haytian frontier. He became at once a popular idol of a large number of Dominicans, which caused some political jealousies.»En 1887 Gómez pareció cansarse del exilio. Quizás fue para engañar a los españoles que vino a Santo Domingo y se estableció en el distrito de Montecristi y comenzó el cultivo de tabaco en Guayubín, cerca de la frontera haitiana. De inmediato se convirtió en un ídolo popular de un gran número de dominicanos, lo que provocó ciertas rivalidades políticas.

“Gen. Heureaux, who was then the president of the republic, became suspicious of Gomez' political aspirations, and his arrest was ordered. Private advices from Guayabin announced that Gomez was aspiring to the presidency, and an uprising was imminent to usurp the reins of the government. “Being in the confidence of the president, and the United States consul being then the most influential consular officer there, Gen. Heureaux consulted with me, and I advised against the movement. I told him that he would do a very foolish thing if he injured or imprisoned Gomez. I argued that Gomez was not thinking of the presidency, and if he was afraid of him he should give him his passport, but never persecute him, because the sentiment of the foreign governments would be very much against it. I further told him that I had just returned from the United States, where I had learned that an uprising in Cuba was imminent. An armed Cuban who had come with me had confided the whole matter to me, and that I had given him a personal letter to the president and one to the Bay State Fruit company. This man's mission was to confer with Gomez, and that if Gomez was making any warlike preparations it was to strike the blow at Spain in Cuba, and not at Santo Domingo.»Gozando de la confianza del presidente, y siendo el cónsul de los Estados Unidos el funcionario consular más influyente en aquel lugar, el Gral.
