SAN DOMINGO. Favorable Report of the Commission.

As the report of the commissioners is rather too lengthy to publish in full, we give our readers an abridgement which contains the leading facts set forth in that document. It is more favorable to annexation than we expected it would be, and if we need any more territory—which we question very much—doubtless the acquisition of San Domingo will prove a good investment:

The report opens by giving the resolution under which the commission of inquiry was appointed. In accordance with the resolution the commission proceeded to the island of San Domingo. They traversed the Dominican Republic from end to end in several directions, either by their agents or in person, one commission crossing it from south to north and another from east to west.—They spent several weeks at the capital in daily conference with the president and chief officers of the government, and in examining the official records, and as at all other places, in constant intercourse with the people and taking testimony of witnesses. They visited the vicinity of the western border of the country, where it was reported there were disturbances, and remained a week at the capital of the neighboring Republic of Hayti. The form of government of

THE DOMINICAN REPUBLIC

is in theory a constitutional Republic. According to its constitution the government is divided into three branches, executive, legislative and judicial. The first consists of a president and vice president elected by an electoral college for a term of six years, with the difference of three years in the time of their election. The president and vice president are ineligible to the presidency during the following term. The president appoints the council of State, consisting of a Minister of public instruction, interior, police and agriculture, and of public works, commerce, war and marine.

THE LEGISLATIVE BRANCH

of the government consists of the Senate (Senado Consulto) elected by primary assemblies, and has two members for the city of San Domingo, two for Santiago and one for each of the other provinces and districts—nine members in all. These hold office for six years and may be re-elected. Each province and district has a government, and each parish and military post has a commandant nominated by the executive and responsible to him. The towns are governed by councils elected by primary assemblies for three years.

THE JUDICIARY

consists of a supreme court, whose seat is at the capital, with a president, four ministers and an attorney general, all of whom are chosen by the Senate from nominations made by the electoral colleges and hold office for five years. In every province and district there is a court of the first instance sitting in the respective capitals, consisting of a judge and an attorney general, all nominated by the executive holding office for five years. It was found that this court had, in many parts of the republic, fallen into disuse. Finally, each town and parish, or commune, has an alcade, appointed by the executive and holding office at his pleasure and corresponding to our justice of the peace.

THE CONDITION OF THE COUNTRY.

The commissioners found the government organized and in complete operation in all of its departments, exercising every function of legitimate government, with Baez as chief magistrate; in full and peaceful possession of all parts of the republic, except on the Haytien border, which is disturbed by insurrectionary leaders and by Haytiens and political entriguers. These are incited to be extraordinarily active at this time by fear of annexation to the United States. From all that the commissioners could ascertain, President Baez has the respect of the great majority of the Dominican people for his administrative abilities and the strong attachment of many leading men, who regard him as the only statesman among them who can hold the nation against domestic faction and foreign foes.

CIVIL COMMOTIONS.

The frequency of civil commotions during a long period, and the consequent insecurity of property, have paralyzed industry, discouraged accumulation, and so impoverished the country that for the last two years the financial resources of the government, as its officers informed us, have been inadequate to pay expenses.

Small colonies of negroes, formerly of the United States, have created in various parts of the country a strong sentiment in favor of annexation to the United States.

THE INSURRECTIONS,

which still exsit, are headed by Cabral and Luperon. The former is universally conceded to be more important, but neither has a distinct flag or a regularly organized army. The commissioners find that Cabral does not claim to be the legal head of the Republic, and in his proclamation and communication to the commission he styles himself chief of the revolution. Luperon is simply a bandit, stained with crimes, his main exploits being in sundry robberies and piratical operations.

CABRAL

is in the western part of the republic, on the Haytien frontier. The force at Cabral's command does not exceed a few hundred men, who, in case of immergency, force into their service all the male population upon whom they can lay their hands. It has been claimed that he has controlled the Dominican port of Barahara, and received supplies through it; but this certainly is no longer the case. On the other hand, it is charged by the present Dominican government that he has received supplies through Hayti, and that Haytien soldiers and arms have been at his disposal.

Among the other

DOMINICAN DIFFICULTIES

is the jealousy between the north and south sides of the island, a number of military chiefs, heading small clans in various districts and in convenience to the neighboring islands.

All these difficulties the commissioners believe would disappear should the Dominican Republic be effectually protected by connection with a strong nation. Local self government in the island has been greatly obstructed by internal difficulties and disturbances; yet many town councils still preserve efficient existence, and are composed of members of good character and ability.

THE SPANISH OCCUPATION.

of the island in 1861 was found to have been consummated by fraud and was most unsatisfactory and oppressive in its results, civil and religious, threatening danger to the Masonic fraternity, a large and influential order on the island, the re-establishment of slavery, either by importation or the reduction of the inhabitants to bondage.

ANNEXATION.

The people were found to be familiar with the question of annexation to the United States, and have discussed it among themselves with intelligence. All classes, in all points of the republic were consulted, magistrates and ecclesiastics of every grade, official, civil and military; citizens of all professions and occupations, in town and country, and everywhere was a general agreement in the declaration that their only hope of permanent peace and prosperity is in annexation to, and becoming a part of the people of the United States. They generally declared their belief that the strong arm of this republic, taking them under its protection as a part of the nation, would at once end the efforts and hopes of every seditious revolutionary leader, and establish law, order and prosperity.

The commissioners found that the majority of the population were composed of honest, mixed blooded Catholics. The Spanish indulged in petty gambling.—Labor was abundant at $10 or less per month. The people are generally ignorant and uneducated, only one small printing office existing on the island. The population is estimated at 150,000.

THE RESOURCES OF THE COUNTRY

are vast and various. Its products may be increased with scarcely any other limit than the labor expended upon them.—There is evidence of mineral wealth in several parts of the island. The Geologist's expedition report the existence of ores of iron, copper and gold, with deposits of lignite, rock salt and petroleum. Iron ore is abundant, of easy access, and will doubtless be made available for the cheap production of pig iron. The reported coal of Samana peninsula, and the neighborhood of Porto Plata was examined and found to be lignite of little value as compared with Pennsylvania or English coal.

There are five classes of lands in San Domingo, viz: 1st. The mountain slopes and valleys. 2d. The intensive region of Llanos, lying east and north of San Domingo city. 3d. The rolling plain of Vega, which is generally wooded, and is the finest body of agricultural ground upon the island. 4th. The Dog lands, like a portion of the plain of Azed and the valley of the Togia, where rain is partly or wholly wanting from topographical causes. 5th. The red clay lands, mostly along the coast, underlaid by corrallion limestone.

Hardly any portion of the island is incapable of cultivation, and, taken as a whole, it is one of the most fertile regions on the face of the earth.

THE AGRICULTURAL PRODUCTS

include all the tropical fruits and vegetables, as well as commercial staples—sugar-cane, coffee, valuable fibrous plants, wax, honey, ginger, indigo, Indian corn, tobacco and rice. Cattle, swine and goats abound, and the forests are made up of all sorts of valuable woods. The once flourishing fisheries have fallen into neglect.

THE CLIMATE.

of the island is generally considered healthy, especially in the interior, where white men labor safely and successfully.

The report goes on at great length to state the resources of the island. The debt of the government is put down at $156,583,159. The financial condition of the government is treated at much length.
