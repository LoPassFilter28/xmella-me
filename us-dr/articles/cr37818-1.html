Santo Domingo, Land of Promise

James N. Rosenberg of New York, president of the Dominican Republic Settlement association, has returned from a month's visit to the republic as the guest of Gen Trujillo, confident that the plan for settlement of 100,000 European refugees on the island will be carried through, and hopeful that the first few hundred families will actually be settled there in the coming spring.

Previous brief notices of the enterprise left an impression that the establishment of the refugee colony had been definitely provided for. Mr Rosenberg's report, however, hopeful as it is, is a “report of progress.” While government officials and association officials have agreed upon a plan, and Gen Trujillo holds to the promise which he made, when he was president of the republic, of a personal gift to the association of a 25,000-acre tract for settlement, the Dominican Congress has not yet ratified the agreement; nor has the settlement association formally acted upon it. So, while these perfecting touches are confidently awaited, one is reminded of the cup and the lip. Mr Rosenberg recalls that at the Evian conference, at which representatives of 32 nations grappled with the problem of refugee settlement, President Trujillo made the only offer of a practical welcome to the refugees. The reminder permits the unpleasant suggestion that objections found weighty in 31 countries can scarcely be negligible in the 32d.

Under the agreement, which awaits final ratification, or rejection, the settlement association would be exempt, by specific legislation, from all taxation so long as it refrains from engaging in commercial or competitive enterprise. This invites examination in connection with Mr Rosenberg's opinion that the Dominican Republic's hospitable attitude toward refugee immigration is due, in part, to its long-standing desire to increase its population from people of white European origin who have special skill or are capable of specialized training. The population of the republic is a little over a million, the very large majority Negroes. The settlement there of 100,000 white Europeans, as proposed, would mean a leaven in the lump that would have potentiality for trouble as well as for the country's development and improvement. This, no doubt, has been carefully considered by both the Dominican government and the settlement association. Just now, the question is whether the consideration jeopardizes the inauguration of the enterprise.

More pleasant to contemplate is the description of the 25,000-acre tract which has been offered to the refugees. It is in Sosua, about seven miles from Puerta Plata, the chief Atlantic port on the north coast. The tract extends from the ocean to the mountains and contains several thousand acres of rolling pasture land. The tract was formerly owned by the United Fruit company. The company abandoned it about 15 years ago, because the soil was found unsuitable for the cultivation of bananas, but it is said to be suitable for many other crops. The land is well watered and there are modern irrigation works near by, and there are 17 buildings, one of them large enough to serve for the association's headquarters. Mr Rosenberg gives warning that it is “no Eden,” but the description reads as if making it approach one would be less difficult than the similar task that is being successfully performed in Palestine. And, at least, there will be no Arabs there—or a negligible few, among the polyglot population that every country has in greater or less degree.

[February 15]
