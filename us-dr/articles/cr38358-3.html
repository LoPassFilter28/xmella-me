OAS Soon May Drop Dominican Sanctions

The Inter-American Sanctions Committee is reported ready to recommend that American states resume full economic and diplomatic relations with the Dominican Republic.

Qualified diplomatic sources say the recommendation will come in a meeting of the full nine-member committee on Friday.

They say that an investigating subcommittee just back from the Dominican Republic found that conditions for the hemispheric boycott of the former Trujillo regime no longer exist.

The feeling is that reports from Santo Domingo today about the continuing impasse between President Joaquin Balaguer and the opposition over a coalition government add urgency to the need for quick action on the subcommittee findings.

Democratic Trend

The five-member investigating subcommittee was hurriedly dispatched to the capital of Santo Domingo last week after the forced exile of remaining members of the Trujillo clan. After the assassination of Dictator Rafael Trujillo Molina last May 30, successor President Joaquin Balaguer undertook a program of "democratization."

Five members of the Organization of American States are represented on the subcommittee. They are Panama, Colombia, Chile, Ecuador and the United States.

They are reported to be unanimously in favor of lifting the boycott in order to help bring stability to the troubled Caribbean nation. They are scheduled to meet on Thursday to finish their report to the full Sanctions Committee.

If—as expected—the Sanctions Committee acts on that report and recommends lifting sanctions, a two-thirds majority of the 21-member OAS Council will be needed to give the recommendations effect.

Far-Reaching Effects

The results would be far-reaching. All members of the OAS would resume diplomatic relations severed after August 1960, and also could begin trade in embargoed items of arms, petroleum, trucks and spare parts.

But the heaviest impact would come in new United States aid to the faltering Dominican economy. So long as the economic and diplomatic boycott is in effect, the United States is hindered from extending large-scale economic help.

When the boycott is dropped, the Dominican Republic will qualify for its $28 million share in the annual sugar windfall taken away from Cuba and distributed among other sugar producers.
