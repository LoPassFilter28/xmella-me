December 5, 19135 de diciembre de 1913

The Wauwatosa News prints a feature: Washington. By George Clinton, in the chapel of the government's Naval Academy at Annapolis there are supposed to repose the remains of John Paul Jones, the Scotch-American naval hero of revolutionary days. As everybody probably remembers, the admiral's body was found, or thought to be found, in a French cemetery, and after as conclusive identification as was possible had been made it was brought to this country, accorded high honors, and buried beneath the Naval Academy's chapel. Now it seems likely that the State Department will become involved semi-officially in the same kind of trouble over the identity of the remains of Christopher Columbus which the naval department had over the identity of the bones of John Paul Jones. An Indiana man, James F. Stutesman of Crawfordsville, must shoulder advance responsibility for the State Department's future troubles. Mr. Stutesman is the commissioner general of the United States to Central America and the West Indies, in the interest of the Panama-Pacific exposition. It was at a dinner given in Santo Domingo by the president of the Dominican Republic that the Indianan suggested that the bones of the great navigator be taken from their resting place in the Santo Domingo cathedral.

Como probablemente todos recuerdan, el cuerpo del almirante fue encontrado, o se creyó encontrado, en un cementerio francés, y después de hecha una identificación tan concluyente como fue posible, fue traído a este país, recibió altos honores y fue enterrado bajo la capilla de la Academia Naval. Ahora parece probable que el Departamento de Estado se vea envuelto semioficialmente en la misma clase de problema sobre la identidad de los restos de Cristóbal Colón que tuvo el departamento naval sobre la identidad de los huesos de John Paul Jones. Un hombre de Indiana, James F. Stutesman, de Crawfordsville, debe cargar con la responsabilidad anticipada de los problemas futuros del Departamento de Estado. El Sr. Stutesman es el comisionado general de los Estados Unidos para América Central y las Indias Occidentales, en interés de la exposición Panama-Pacific. Fue en una cena ofrecida en Santo Domingo por el presidente de la República Dominicana donde el hombre de Indiana sugirió que los huesos del gran navegante fueran sacados de su lugar de reposo en la catedral de Santo Domingo.

Se admitió, sin embargo, que este gobierno está tratando de garantizar la limpieza de las elecciones como resultado de las seguridades dadas el verano pasado, cuando, gracias a los esfuerzos de los Estados Unidos, la revolución llegó a su fin. El plan de tener 'observadores' ha sido adoptado como el procedimiento más conveniente en vista del corto tiempo entre hoy y el día de las elecciones. December 8, 19138 de diciembre de 1913

President Wilson made it clear that the United States has used its good offices in persuading the revolutionists to lay down their arms on the promise that everything possible within international right would be done by the United States to insure fair elections. The Washington government decided to send some observers speaking the language of San Domingo to act as impartial witnesses but not as referees or officials, so that in case of dispute it would have persons to turn to for impartial testimony. The paper also prints a dispatch: San Domingo, Dec. 8. Women as well as men were speakers today at an immense mass meeting in favor of the free and legal elections in the Dominican republic. The meeting was held in the public square here under the auspices of the Independence party, and perfect order prevailed. A protest against the supervision of the election by American commissioners was put in circulation for signature by the audience.

WILSON EXPLAINS DOMINICAN ACTION Commissioners Go to Be Peaceful Spectators and Not to Supervise the Elections Next MondayWilson explica la acción dominicana. Los comisionados van como espectadores pacíficos y no a supervisar las elecciones del próximo lunes.

December 9, 19139 de diciembre de 1913

The United States had used its good offices in persuading the revolutionists to quit hostilities on the promise that everything possible within international right would be done by the United States to insure fair elections. Protest from San Domingo, it was explained at the White House today, was founded on the belief that the United States intended a supervision of the elections, but now that the notion has been dispelled the administration here is confident there will be no objection to the presence of the American observers. Senor Francisco J. Peynado, Dominican minister, called on Secretary Bryan to protest that the Dominican government cannot see how representatives of the State Department can visit Santo Domingo for the purpose indicated, and without official status. Before the elections next Monday the United States will have four ships in Dominican waters. The paper also prints a dispatch: Santo Domingo. The Salt Lake Tribune

UNITED STATES WILL SIMPLY TAKE NOTESEstados Unidos simplemente tomará notas

December 9, 19139 de diciembre de 1913

The United States does not intend to supervise or interfere with the approaching elections in the Dominican republic. A special commission from Washington will merely observe the election as 'friendly and impartial spectators.' That was the way President Wilson today outlined informally the mission of State Department officials now on their way to San Domingo. President Wilson made it clear that the United States had used its good offices in persuading the revolutionists to lay down their arms on the promise that everything possible, within international right, would be done by the United States to insure fair elections. The paper also prints an item under the head 'PROTECTORATE IS NOT INTENDED BY THE U. S.': Washington, Dec. 8. President Wilson told callers today that the United States did not wish to establish a protectorate over any country of Central America or over any other part of the globe. Published reports that the United States contemplated a new and far-reaching policy with respect to Central America, especially Nicaragua, brought a prompt denial from the White House.

Una comisión especial de Washington se limitará a observar la elección como 'espectadores amistosos e imparciales'. Así fue como el presidente Wilson esbozó hoy informalmente la misión de los funcionarios del Departamento de Estado ahora en camino a San Domingo. El presidente Wilson dejó claro que los Estados Unidos habían usado sus buenos oficios para persuadir a los revolucionarios de que depusieran las armas, con la promesa de que se haría todo lo posible, dentro del derecho internacional, por parte de los Estados Unidos para asegurar unas elecciones limpias. El periódico publica también una nota bajo el título 'PROTECTORATE IS NOT INTENDED BY THE U. S.': Washington, 8 de diciembre. El presidente Wilson dijo hoy a quienes lo visitaron que los Estados Unidos no deseaban establecer un protectorado sobre ningún país de América Central ni sobre ninguna otra parte del globo. Informes publicados de que los Estados Unidos contemplaban una política nueva y de gran alcance con respecto a América Central, especialmente Nicaragua, provocaron una pronta desmentida de la Casa Blanca.
