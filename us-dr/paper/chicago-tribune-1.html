The cattle of Dominica are celebrated; its mahogany and sandal wood are the best in the tropics; many American colored men have settled in this republic, and, but for the civil war, none would ever have quitted it. The bay of Samana, the feature continues, is the outlet of the Yuna River, and is fifty miles long and from fifteen to twenty miles wide; the ocean entrance to it is only a mile wide, and is commanded by an old fort, while high mountains enclose both shores. Twenty-five miles up the bay, upon an inlet to the north, is the town of Samana, the inlet very deep and almost land-locked; the bay has been compared both to the Bay of Naples and to the Chesapeake, to the former for its dazzling tropical and mountain scenery, to the latter for its innumerable wild fowl. This bay has been pronounced the key to the Gulf of Mexico; fine beds of coal surround it, and navigators say that the future route to Darien and Mexico will be by the Mona Passage, just outside of Samana. Samana town, the passage adds, is no town at all, a mere semi-detached peninsula with town vestiges upon it, and the site is swampy; Bonaparte planned a city near Samana, to be called Napoleon, and had the site surveyed. December 23, 187023 de diciembre de 1870

This Chicago Tribune editorial window, opening in mid-argument, argues against annexation: if the Republic of Dominica cannot last six weeks without civil war, if no authority is recognized there but the bayonet and life and liberty depend on the will of the faction leader of the moment, what invites the United States to incorporate so distant and squabbling a republic, when it has enough of reconstruction on its hands; it asks why Americans should incorporate a foreign people it describes in contemptuous terms as degraded, ignorant and speaking a different language, and it says the attempt to force the annexation of this Dominican Republic is being made as an administration measure at a present cost of several millions and a perpetual annual cost of twice as many, with the President, his official family, the Vice President and influential senators and representatives behind it — the window closing in mid-list.

Esta ventana editorial del Chicago Tribune, que se abre a mitad de un argumento, se pronuncia contra la anexión: si la República de Dominica no puede durar seis semanas sin guerra civil, si allí no se reconoce autoridad alguna sino la bayoneta y la vida y la libertad dependen de la voluntad del caudillo de la facción dominante, ¿qué invita a los Estados Unidos a incorporar una república tan distante y pendenciera, cuando tiene bastante reconstrucción entre manos?; pregunta además por qué habría de incorporarse a un pueblo extranjero al que describe en términos despectivos como degradado, ignorante y de lengua distinta; y afirma que el intento de forzar la anexión de esta República Dominicana se impone como medida de la administración, a un costo presente de varios millones y un costo anual perpetuo del doble, con el presidente, su familia oficial, el vicepresidente y senadores y representantes influyentes detrás — y la ventana se cierra a media enumeración.

The two marplots in the whole business, he said, were St. John, a British consul, and a former Spanish consul at San Domingo city, who had brought about the Spanish occupation: the Spaniard opposes the annexation of San Domingo by the Americans because Spain had failed in it, and St. John's conduct he attributed to the general cussedness of the British character in any matter of territorial emulation. Saget, the present usurper of Hayti in Fabens's word, he described with racial contempt, while Salnave, who had been killed at Cape Haytien, he called a hero; Saget, he said, had Salnave's body dragged behind an ox cart before the American consul's residence, where the wife and family of the dead President remained, as a token of his contempt of the United States.

Los dos entorpecedores de todo el asunto, dijo, eran St. John, un cónsul británico, y un antiguo cónsul español en la ciudad de Santo Domingo, que habían provocado la ocupación española: el español se opone a la anexión de Santo Domingo por los americanos porque España fracasó en ella, y la conducta de St. John la atribuyó a la general maldad del carácter británico en cualquier asunto de emulación territorial. El general Pimentel desembarcó cerca de Montecristi con 500 dominicanos procedentes de Santo Tomás y las Islas Turcas, pertrechados desde El Seibo, y amenaza Puerto Plata, mientras Cabral marcha hacia Azua.
