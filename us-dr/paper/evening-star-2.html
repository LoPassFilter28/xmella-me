Writing editorially, the Evening Star observes that the San Domingo revolution

September 2, 18992 de septiembre de 1899

Writing editorially, the Evening Star observes that the San Domingo revolution succeeded far more rapidly and with less bloodshed than expected, arguing that the republic could enjoy a prosperous future if the victors avoid bitter factionalism and if the newly established government proves stable and capable. Although present indications point to Jiminez ultimately succeeding the newly named provisional government, the paper warns that rivalries could quickly spark another revolt unless a strong hand guides affairs. The editorial attributes the insurrection's swift advance to widespread fear among citizens and officials alike of trusting any direct successor to Heureaux, whose cruel rule they remembered with hatred; with Heureaux gone, the revolution became a process of crumbling and reassembling that needed only a nucleus to draw growing support.

En un comentario editorial, el Evening Star observa que la revolución en San Domingo triunfó con mucha mayor rapidez y menor derramamiento de sangre de lo previsto, y sostiene que la república podría gozar de un futuro próspero si los vencedores evitan el faccionalismo enconado y si el gobierno recién establecido demuestra estabilidad y capacidad. Aunque todo apunta por ahora al ascenso definitivo de Jiminez tras el gobierno provisional recién nombrado, el periódico advierte que las rivalidades podrían precipitar otra revuelta en poco tiempo si no se pone una mano firme al timón. El editorial atribuye el rápido avance de la insurrección al temor generalizado, tanto del pueblo como de los funcionarios, a confiar en un sucesor directo de Heureaux, cuyos actos crueles recordaban con odio; desaparecido Heureaux, concluye que la revolución fue un proceso de disgregación y reagrupamiento que solo necesitaba un núcleo para atraer cada vez a más personas.

Powell comunicó que la insurrección dominicana se propagaba lentamente y había alcanzado la provincia de Azura, donde los rumores indicaban combates el 7 de abril, lo que obligó al gobierno a enviar tropas y disponer de unos 2.400 hombres en campaña, con refuerzos adicionales previstos. No obstante, tras remitir dicho informe por escrito, Powell telegrafió al Departamento de Estado informando que la rebelión ha sido aplastada.

Following the withdrawal of Spain in 1865 and the promulgation of new constitutions, the newspaper tracks successive political convulsions in Santo Domingo, including the banishments, returns, and overthrow of President Baez alongside administrations headed by Cabral and subsequent leaders. The dispatch records concurrent insurrections, executions, and presidential successions in Haiti, underscoring decades of recurrent turmoil across both nations.

Tras la retirada de España en 1865 y la promulgación de nuevas constituciones, el periódico registra las continuas convulsiones políticas en Santo Domingo, entre ellas los destierros, retornos y derrocamiento del presidente Baez junto a las administraciones de Cabral y mandatarios posteriores. El recuento consigna insurrecciones, ejecuciones y sucesiones presidenciales paralelas en Haití, subrayando décadas de agitación recurrente en ambas naciones.

El Cherokee trajo diecinueve pasajeros de cabina desde San Domingo, pero ningún refugiado.

The Star prints part of a discussion of the island: the island

November 14, 190314 de noviembre de 1903

The Star prints part of a discussion of the island: the island has an area of 28,000 square miles, and upon it are the two republics of Hayti and Santo Domingo; Santo Domingo itself is not thickly populated, and the paper says the business of the republic is conducted by the whites, who number about 20 per cent of the population, and as a rule hold the offices. The paper argues that both republics have retrograded steadily, that both are financially wrecked, and that the Monroe doctrine gives the United States control of the island so far as any European power is concerned. Under the head 'Strategic Value of Samana Bay,' the paper describes Samana Bay, thirty-one miles long, at the eastern extremity of the island, just across from Porto Rico, as one of the most beautiful and valuable bays in the West Indies, and says the United States has sought possession of this bay as a strategic point in the Caribbean Sea for almost one hundred years, Gen. Jackson having first recommended the purchase in a message to Congress.

El Star publica parte de una discusión sobre la isla: la isla tiene una superficie de 28,000 millas cuadradas, y sobre ella están las dos repúblicas de Haití y Santo Domingo; Santo Domingo en sí no está densamente poblado, y el periódico dice que los negocios de la república son conducidos por los blancos, que suman alrededor del 20 por ciento de la población, y por regla general ocupan los cargos. El periódico sostiene que ambas repúblicas han retrocedido constantemente, que ambas están financieramente arruinadas, y que la doctrina Monroe da a los Estados Unidos el control de la isla en lo que concierne a cualquier potencia europea. Bajo el titular «Strategic Value of Samana Bay», el periódico describe la bahía de Samaná, de treinta y una millas de largo, en el extremo oriental de la isla, justo frente a Puerto Rico, como una de las bahías más hermosas y valiosas de las Indias Occidentales, y dice que los Estados Unidos han buscado la posesión de esta bahía como punto estratégico en el mar Caribe durante casi cien años, habiendo recomendado el general Jackson por primera vez la compra en un mensaje al Congreso.

The Star prints part of an account of the exiles' expedition

November 16, 190316 de noviembre de 1903

The Star prints part of an account of the exiles' expedition from Cuba: the exiles decided to co-operate with a general uprising along the frontier of Haiti, taking in the towns of Monte Cristy, Dajabon and Guayubin, and extending to the towns of Santiago de los Caballeros and La Vega, having received notification from the governor of Puerto Plata, Gen. and ex-Priest Don Carlos Morales, of the result of their plans. Then they prepared to take the Cuban steamer Maria Herrera with a view of landing in Puerto Plata, which was held by Gen. Carlos Morales, and so join the revolutionary movement. Unfortunately the Dominican consul in Cuba became aware of their movements and applied to the Cuban authorities to have them prevented from embarking, but he was informed by them that as there was no evidence that these men were taking arms and war stores, and as they were leaving the country as peaceable citizens, they could not be interfered with. The Dominican consul then telegraphed to the president of the Dominican republic at the city of Santo Domingo informing him of their having embarked for some port of the republic, presumably Puerto Plata. Under the head 'Took Passage for San Juan,' the account continues: the Dominican exiles, being perfectly aware of the uncertainty of such uprisings, took passage for San Juan, Porto Rico, intending to disembark in Puerto Plata if all were well, but if not to continue to Porto Rico and join ex-President Jiminez. Upon arriving off the port of Puerto Plata a Dominican warship was sighted, but no attention was paid to her, and they continued their course.

El Star publica parte de un relato de la expedición de los exiliados desde Cuba: los exiliados decidieron cooperar con un levantamiento general a lo largo de la frontera de Haití, que abarcaba las poblaciones de Monte Cristi, Dajabón y Guayubín, y se extendía a las poblaciones de Santiago de los Caballeros y La Vega, habiendo recibido notificación del gobernador de Puerto Plata, el general y ex sacerdote don Carlos Morales, del resultado de sus planes. Entonces se prepararon para tomar el vapor cubano María Herrera con miras a desembarcar en Puerto Plata, que estaba en poder del general Carlos Morales, y unirse así al movimiento revolucionario. Desafortunadamente el cónsul dominicano en Cuba se enteró de sus movimientos y solicitó a las autoridades cubanas que les impidieran embarcar, pero fue informado por ellas de que, como no había evidencia de que estos hombres llevaran armas y pertrechos de guerra, y como salían del país como ciudadanos pacíficos, no se podía interferir con ellos. El cónsul dominicano telegrafió entonces al presidente de la república dominicana en la ciudad de Santo Domingo informándole que habían embarcado hacia algún puerto de la república, presumiblemente Puerto Plata. Bajo el titular «Took Passage for San Juan», el relato continúa: los exiliados dominicanos, perfectamente conscientes de la incertidumbre de tales levantamientos, tomaron pasaje para San Juan, Puerto Rico, con la intención de desembarcar en Puerto Plata si todo iba bien, pero si no, continuar a Puerto Rico y unirse al ex presidente Jiménez. Al llegar frente al puerto de Puerto Plata se avistó un buque de guerra dominicano, pero no se le prestó atención, y continuaron su rumbo.

The Star prints a further part of its account of the Dominican exiles

November 16, 190316 de noviembre de 1903

The Star prints a further part of its account of the Dominican exiles and of President Woss y Gil: the account speaks of a warrior priest, brother of a renowned soldier who perished in the attack upon that town, as a man of great energy and high aspiration, determined to equal his brother in fame. President Alejandro Woss y Gil is a man of considerable worth as a statesman and soldier; he is a well-educated man and has lived abroad for a considerable time, representing the Dominican republic at Washington for quite a number of years during the presidency of General Heureaux. Under the head 'Second Presidential Term,' the account continues: this is his second presidential term, having occupied that position for the first time when he was about twenty-six or twenty-seven years of age, being the first Dominican to occupy that high post at such an early age; he was the intimate friend of President Heureaux, and is said to be a man of his school, that is to say, a man who does not hesitate to give his troublesome adversaries six feet of good Dominican soil whenever he can get hold of them, and a tenacious fighter, who is not to be daunted by either the large number or superior strategic position of the enemy.

El Star publica otra parte de su relato de los exiliados dominicanos y del presidente Woss y Gil: el relato habla de un sacerdote guerrero, hermano de un renombrado soldado que pereció en el ataque contra esa población, como un hombre de gran energía y alta aspiración, decidido a igualar la fama de su hermano. The Star prints part of the profile of General Deschamps, an official of the Dominican republic

November 25, 190325 de noviembre de 1903

The Star prints part of the profile of General Deschamps, an official of the Dominican republic in the government of President Alejandro Woss y Gil up to a few days ago, when he resigned his position to join his former chief, ex-President Juan Isidro Jiminez: General Deschamps is one of the most popular men in the Dominican republic; his popularity is not, however, due to military prowess, but to the fact that he has always been the people's friend, is a great lover of progress, has always had the tact to espouse the right cause, and above all because he has been the victim of the most terrible persecution; due to his bold opposition to President Heureaux's sanguinary and corrupted policy he was forced to leave Santo Domingo and live abroad, where he had to suffer many hardships; although living in exile he did not slacken in his attacks upon President Heureaux's policy, and about seven years ago, while residing in Ponce, Porto Rico, an assassin paid by President Heureaux to assassinate him drove a dagger into his back, very nearly killing him; the assassin escaped and Deschamps slowly recovered, but he has never succeeded in making the wound heal thoroughly, and it is a constant source of annoyance to him; at another time his wife, who had accompanied him in exile, embarked in a small schooner at the British island of Grand Turk for Puerto Plata, but on her arrival at that port she was not permitted to disembark, and though she was accompanied by her children and the eldest boy took ill on board, still she was not permitted to disembark.

El Star publica parte del perfil del general Deschamps, un funcionario de la república dominicana en el gobierno del presidente Alejandro Woss y Gil hasta hace pocos días, cuando renunció a su cargo para unirse a su antiguo jefe, el expresidente Juan Isidro Jiménez: el general Deschamps es uno de los hombres más populares de la república dominicana; su popularidad no se debe, sin embargo, a sus proezas militares, sino al hecho de que siempre ha sido amigo del pueblo, es un gran amante del progreso, siempre ha tenido el tacto de abrazar la causa correcta y, sobre todo, porque ha sido víctima de la más terrible persecución; debido a su audaz oposición a la política sanguinaria y corrupta del presidente Heureaux se vio obligado a salir de Santo Domingo y vivir en el extranjero, donde tuvo que sufrir muchas penurias; aunque vivía en el exilio no aflojó en sus ataques contra la política del presidente Heureaux, y hace unos siete años, mientras residía en Ponce, Puerto Rico, un asesino pagado por el presidente Heureaux para asesinarlo le clavó un puñal en la espalda, casi matándolo; el asesino escapó y Deschamps se recuperó lentamente, pero nunca ha logrado que la herida sane por completo, y es para él una fuente constante de molestia; en otra ocasión su esposa, que lo había acompañado en el exilio, se embarcó en una pequeña goleta en la isla británica de Gran Turca rumbo a Puerto Plata, pero a su llegada a ese puerto no se le permitió desembarcar, y aunque la acompañaban sus hijos y el niño mayor enfermó a bordo, aun así no se le permitió desembarcar.

The Star prints a sketch of General Ramon Caceres, the slayer of President Heureaux

November 25, 190325 de noviembre de 1903

The Star prints a sketch of General Ramon Caceres, the slayer of President Heureaux on June 26, 1899 and ex-governor of Santiago de los Caballeros: he is one of the chief leaders of the present revolution, ranks next to Jiminez, and will most likely be appointed to the vice presidency should the revolution triumph; General Caceres is a man of great energy and courage and has set his heart upon the highest position in the republic, but he is unfortunately a man of the sword and not of the pen, and although he might make an excellent president for the quelling of revolutions, he would certainly not know how to set about extracting the republic from its present financial difficulties, which are many and pressing; if the present revolution triumphs it will have many claims upon its hands: there is a Cuban claim for preventing the Cuban steamer Maria Herrera to enter the port of Puerto Plata to land her passengers, mail and cargo without there being any notification whatever of the existence of a regular blockade; there is just such another claim to be made by the Clyde Line of steamers, there is a German claim, and there is said to be a Spanish claim for the death of the chancellor of the Spanish consulate who was killed by a bullet while writing in his office during the last revolution, eight months ago, and others; meanwhile the resources of the country have been completely paralyzed, and sugar, which is the chief product, is lacking a market, the American market being the only one it ever had.
