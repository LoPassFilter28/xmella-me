In Ciudad Trujillo, capital of the Dominican Republic, newspaper sirens blared and crowds gathered, reacting with audible dismay when the mistake was corrected. The article notes that wire editors caught the error quickly enough that the false flash never appeared in print. In New York and elsewhere, newspaper and radio station switchboards were almost instantly swamped with inquiries from persons who had heard the original and incorrect flash but not the “kill.” The London bureau of the Associated Press cabled this explanation: the flash erroneously stating Eisenhower had announced Allied landings in France was transmitted by a new teletype operator in a wholly unauthorized test of a perforator; it moved without authority of censors and without knowledge of the editorial staff, which had no such copy on hand. Experienced operators saw the flash on the printer and immediately notified New York and the editors, who sent a “kill.” Joan Ellis, the young operator who erred, said she had been practicing on a disconnected machine and thought she had torn up all that tape and thrown it away; however, before transmitting the first “take” of the Russian communiqué, she inadvertently ran through part of the tape that she thought she had destroyed. Miss Ellis is 22 and had been with the Associated Press since January 1944, with four years' experience as a teletype operator in the WAAF (Women's Auxiliary Airforce). The big American radio chains — NBC, CBS, Blue Network and Mutual — all interrupted programs to carry the erroneous flash, and again to announce the correction, which they repeated at intervals during the evening. By coincidence, the British Broadcasting Company was at the time broadcasting a dramatic reenactment of the Dunkerque epic of four years ago; the BBC did not receive the false flash, but anyone who had tuned in on its program might easily have gained the impression that an invasion was in progress.

En Nueva York y en otros lugares, las centralitas de los periódicos y las emisoras de radio se vieron inundadas casi al instante de consultas de personas que habían oído el teletipo original e incorrecto pero no la orden de anulación. La oficina de Londres de la Associated Press cablegrafió esta explicación: el teletipo que afirmaba erróneamente que Eisenhower había anunciado desembarcos aliados en Francia fue transmitido por una nueva operadora de teletipo en una prueba totalmente no autorizada de una perforadora; circuló sin autoridad de los censores y sin conocimiento de la redacción, que no tenía tal texto en su poder. Operadores experimentados vieron el teletipo en la impresora y avisaron de inmediato a Nueva York y a los redactores, que enviaron la orden de anulación. Joan Ellis, la joven operadora que cometió el error, dijo que había estado practicando en una máquina desconectada y creía haber roto toda la cinta y haberla tirado; sin embargo, antes de transmitir la primera entrega del comunicado ruso, pasó inadvertidamente parte de la cinta que creía destruida. Las grandes cadenas de radio estadounidenses —NBC, CBS, Blue Network y Mutual— interrumpieron sus programas para transmitir el falso teletipo, y de nuevo para anunciar la corrección, que repitieron a intervalos durante la noche.
