Daily Kennebec Journal

Daily Kennebec Journal prints a dispatch: at the state department

July 3, 19143 de julio de 1914

Daily Kennebec Journal prints a dispatch: at the state department, it is said, the question discussed was how the United States could enter Hayti and manage her finances as the finances of the Dominican Republic are handled. After the departure Tuesday night of the new American minister to Hayti, Mr. Railly-Blanchard, a diplomat of long training and experience, the report was believed that the United States is trying to find a way to have Hayti ask this government to manage her finances. In the Dominican Republic the United States appoints the collector of customs, who takes charge of all revenues, payments on the national debt and maintenance of the customs force. The balance is then turned over to the island government. Despite a series of revolts in Santo Domingo recently, her revenues have not been disturbed, because of the presence of the Americans there. Hayti is now torn by rebellion and her credit is at a low mark.

Daily Kennebec Journal publica un despacho: la cuestión discutida en el Departamento de Estado, se dice, fue cómo los Estados Unidos podrían entrar en Haití y manejar sus finanzas como se manejan las finanzas de la República Dominicana. Después de la partida el martes por la noche del nuevo ministro americano en Haití, el Sr. Railly-Blanchard, un diplomático de larga formación y experiencia, se creyó el informe de que los Estados Unidos están tratando de encontrar una manera de hacer que Haití pida a este gobierno que maneje sus finanzas. En la República Dominicana los Estados Unidos nombran al recaudador de aduanas, que se hace cargo de todos los ingresos, los pagos de la deuda nacional y el mantenimiento de la fuerza aduanera. El saldo se entrega entonces al gobierno de la isla. A pesar de una serie de revueltas en Santo Domingo recientemente, sus ingresos no han sido perturbados, a causa de la presencia de los americanos allí. Haití está ahora desgarrado por la rebelión y su crédito está en un nivel bajo.

Norwich Bulletin

The Norwich Bulletin prints an item: the Bureau of Foreign and Domestic Commerce of the Department of Commerce

July 3, 19143 de julio de 1914

The Norwich Bulletin prints an item: the Bureau of Foreign and Domestic Commerce of the Department of Commerce has just issued (Washington, July 2) an interesting monograph entitled 'Development of the Dominican Republic' (Special Consular Report, No. 65). Although, next to Cuba, the largest of the West Indies, the island which is divided between the Dominican Republic and Hayti is said to be the least known and to offer the greatest possibilities for future development. It treats of the general features of the republic, its agricultural possibilities, its import and export trade in detail and the countries participating in it, its shipping facilities and other information which should make the publication a useful compendium for consultation as to the country's trade and resources. Copies of the monograph may be procured from the Superintendent of Documents, Government Printing Office, Washington, D.C., for 6 cents per copy.

The Norwich Bulletin publica una nota: Washington, 2 de julio. La Oficina de Comercio Exterior e Interior del Departamento de Comercio acaba de publicar una interesante monografía titulada 'Development of the Dominican Republic' (Informe Consular Especial, No. 65). Aunque, después de Cuba, la mayor de las Indias Occidentales, la isla que está dividida entre la República Dominicana y Haití se dice que es la menos conocida y que ofrece las mayores posibilidades de desarrollo futuro. Trata de las características generales de la república, sus posibilidades agrícolas, su comercio de importación y exportación en detalle y los países que participan en él, sus facilidades de embarque y otra información que debería hacer de la publicación un compendio útil de consulta sobre el comercio y los recursos del país. Los ejemplares de la monografía pueden adquirirse en la Oficina del Superintendente de Documentos, Oficina de Imprenta del Gobierno, Washington, D.C., por 6 centavos el ejemplar.

July 19, 191419 de julio de 1914

Tulsa Daily World prints a dispatch: Vidal has occupied San Cristobal. He was marching toward the capital when met by Charge White, who, backed by two powerful American warships with marine contingents, secured an agreement to an armistice while the federal officers in San Domingo City communicated Vidal's demands to President Bordas. The wireless outfits of the American vessels were used and at Puerto Plata Captain Eberle of the cruiser Washington submitted the messages to President Bordas in his camp outside the city. So far Mr. White has received no answer, but it is believed Bordas either will make a desperate effort to capture Puerta Plata or to abandon the siege and return south to prevent his own capture. The United States naval transport Hancock is about due at Guantanamo with marines from Vera Cruz. They are to be sent to the Dominican Republic in case of emergency. American Minister Sullivan has arrived in Washington and expects to confer with President Wilson and Secretary Bryan regarding the Dominican situation. It will be determined at this conference whether the Hancock's marines shall be landed and a virtual, though temporary, protectorate established. The transport Prairie sailed today from Vera Cruz for Haiti and the Dominican Republic to strengthen the American forces there.

The Washington Herald prints a dispatch: Conditions in Santo Domingo

July 19, 191419 de julio de 1914

The Washington Herald prints a dispatch: Conditions in Santo Domingo are steadily growing worse, according to a report received today at the Navy Department from Capt. Eberle, commanding the cruiser Washington at Puerto Plata. Mr. Sullivan came on the Clyde liner Seminole and left for Washington, where he will meet officials of the State Department either today or tomorrow and explain some phases of the revolution in Santo Domingo. His activities in influencing the American custom receivership in Santo Domingo have led, it is said, to strong anti-American feeling in the island. The food situation at Puerto Plata has been relieved by the arrival of large quantities of produce and cattle. Desultory fighting still continues about Puerto Plata. Two British schooners have come to Puerto Plata to take off eighty British subjects, residents of Turks Island.

The Evening Star prints a dispatch: Charge White had taken counsel

July 19, 191419 de julio de 1914

The Evening Star prints a dispatch: Charge White had taken counsel with the federal officials left in temporary command by Bordas and was prepared to offer terms for at least a temporary suspension of hostilities. The fact that Mr. White has behind him two powerful American warships, with their marine contingents, probably had its effect upon the insurgent leader. At any rate, he agreed to an armistice for a few days while the federal officers in Santo Domingo City communicated his demands to President Bordas. The wireless outfits of the American naval vessels were used in transmitting this information to President Bordas. They afford the only means of quick communication between the north and the south at present, owing to the destruction of the telegraph wires in the interior. At Puerto Plata, Capt. Eberle of the cruiser Washington submitted the messages to President Bordas in his camp outside the city. The United States naval transport Hancock is about due at Guantanamo with nearly 300 marines from Vera Cruz. They are to be sent to the Dominican Republic in case of emergency. Originally it was intended no forward movement should be made by these marines until reinforced by 400 additional men from the Philadelphia navy yard, but as these cannot reach there for two weeks it is probable, if a crisis is reached, the Hancock will be dispatched immediately to Puerto Plata or Santo Domingo City. American Minister Sullivan has arrived in Washington and expects to confer with President Wilson and Secretary Bryan.
