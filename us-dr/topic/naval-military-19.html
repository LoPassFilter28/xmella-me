The Maryville Times

The Maryville Times prints a survey of the West Indies in wartimeEl Maryville Times publica un estudio de las Indias Occidentales en tiempo de guerra

May 14, 189814 de mayo de 1898

Toussaint L'Ouverture's successful struggle against France and his capture by treachery, followed by the complete expulsion of the French, is one of liberty's favorite tales; the Spanish invaded San Domingo in 1861, and their defeat left the islanders free. The Haytians have experimented with kings and discarded them; the treasure of one of these despots, Christophe, remains buried under the rank vegetation, for which Hayti is unsurpassed, among the ruins of a stronghold he built; the story is that the architect, a European, and all of the workmen were thrown from the parapets, so that the secrets of the citadel they have just completed should never be divulged. There are quite a million people on the island, but they dally on the way to civilization; there is no army, or fortification worthy the name, and Hayti's only use to the United States would be in lending it a harbor: there are no harbors on the north coast, but Port au Prince, in the republic of Hayti, is a well-protected port lying behind the island of Gonave; in the Dominican Republic there are the harbors of Santo Domingo and Puerto Plata, which would make convenient bases for operations in the Caribbean. The Mona Passage, only 60 miles wide, separates Hayti from the beautiful island of Porto Rico.

La exitosa lucha de Toussaint L'Ouverture contra Francia y su captura por traición, seguida de la completa expulsión de los franceses, es uno de los relatos favoritos de la libertad; los españoles invadieron San Domingo en 1861, y su derrota dejó libres a los isleños. Los haitianos han experimentado con reyes y los han desechado; el tesoro de uno de esos déspotas, Christophe, permanece enterrado bajo la espesa vegetación, por la que Haití no tiene igual, entre las ruinas de una fortaleza que construyó; el relato dice que el arquitecto, un europeo, y todos los obreros fueron arrojados desde las murallas, para que los secretos de la ciudadela que acababan de completar no fueran divulgados jamás. Hay en la isla cerca de un millón de personas, pero se demoran en el camino de la civilización; no hay ejército, ni fortificación digna de ese nombre, y el único uso de Haití para los Estados Unidos sería prestarles un puerto: no hay puertos en la costa norte, pero Puerto Príncipe, en la república de Haití, es un puerto bien protegido situado detrás de la isla de Gonave; en la República Dominicana están los puertos de Santo Domingo y Puerto Plata, que harían bases convenientes para las operaciones en el Caribe. El paso de la Mona, de solo 60 millas de ancho, separa a Haití de la hermosa isla de Puerto Rico.

The Indianapolis Journal

A Haitian deputy, who professes to be well informed on allUn diputado haitiano, que profesa estar bien informado sobre todo

May 16, 189816 de mayo de 1898

The Indianapolis Journal reported from Haiti regarding proposed constitutional changes concerning foreign property rights. Haitian Foreign Minister Brutus St. Victor denied claims that the government intended to repeal Article 6 of the constitution, which prohibited alien ownership of real estate, stating the public was not prepared for such reform. St. Victor also declined to disclose specific government plans to address the rising premium on paper currency.

The Indianapolis Journal informó desde Haití sobre las propuestas de reforma constitucional relativas a los derechos de propiedad de los extranjeros. El ministro de Asuntos Exteriores haitiano, Brutus St. Victor, desmintió las versiones de que el gobierno pretendiera derogar el artículo 6 de la constitución, que prohibía a los extranjeros la propiedad de bienes inmuebles, afirmando que la opinión pública no estaba preparada para tal cambio. St. Victor declinó asimismo revelar planes gubernamentales específicos para hacer frente al creciente sobreprecio del papel moneda.

May 16, 189816 de mayo de 1898

The Opposing Fleets: from Cape Haytien, that the United States torpedo boat Porter and the store-ship Supply, which have been there awaiting orders, have sailed to join Admiral Sampson's squadron, that the squadron passed Cape Haytien today, though it is uncertain whether it will turn southward to meet the Spanish fleet or go first to Key West, and that no news has been received there of the reported capture of the auxiliary cruiser Yale, formerly the American liner Paris, and the report is discredited; from Puerto Plata, Dominican Republic, that all on board the United States fleet are doing well, that there has been no firing since the bombardment of San Juan de Porto Rico, and that the Spanish torpedo gunboat Terror is being docked at Fort de France, island of Martinique; and from Curaçao, that the Spanish cruisers Maria Teresa, Vizcaya, Almirante Oquendo and Cristobal Colon and the torpedo boat destroyers Pluton and Terror, which arrived off the harbor yesterday morning, are still there, only the Maria Teresa and the Vizcaya having been admitted to the port, where they have bought coal, provisions and medicines, and that the statement that the destroyer Terror is with the squadron of Admiral Cervera conflicts with a previous statement that the vessel had been definitely located at Martinique.

It would appear that the Fanita, instead of having on board only 17 Dominicans, probably had 330 on board; she arrived at Cape Haytien on June 1 half empty, bringing from New York provisions for the navy, which she landed at Matthewtown, in the Bahama Islands, and slipped away about 10 o'clock the same night, to fill up the empty portion of her hold; she had on board Chris Roberts, the most famous pilot of the West Indies. General Ysidro Jimenez and General Pablo Villanueva are believed to have been on board; Jimenez has long been in the public eye in Santo Domingo as a desirable man for the presidency, and Villanueva fought bravely against President Heureaux's alleged usurpation and was exiled.
