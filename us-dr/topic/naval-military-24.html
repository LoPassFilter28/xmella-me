Dispatches on the hurricane in The Indianapolis Journal state

August 11, 189911 de agosto de 1899

Dispatches on the hurricane in The Indianapolis Journal state that the storm passed over Porto Plata and appears to have ravaged the northern Dominican Republic. Because all communication between Porto Plata and the interior, not including San Domingo, is interrupted, no accurate account of the damage can yet be obtained, though losses are believed to be heavy. The railroad from Porto Plata to San Domingo is out of service, and the hurricane later passed over Cape Haitien with reduced violence.

Despachos sobre el huracán en The Indianapolis Journal informan que la tormenta pasó sobre Porto Plata y parece haber devastado el norte de la República Dominicana. Debido a que toda comunicación entre Porto Plata y el interior, sin incluir a San Domingo, está interrumpida, aún no es posible obtener una relación exacta de los daños, aunque se estima que son de importancia. El ferrocarril de Porto Plata a San Domingo dejó de funcionar y el huracán pasó finalmente sobre Cape Haitien con menor violencia.

A Havana dispatch of August 16 in the New-York Tribune reports

August 17, 189917 de agosto de 1899

A Havana dispatch of August 16 in the New-York Tribune reports that General Juan Isidro Jiminez, aspirant to the presidency of San Domingo, will probably depart Havana for the island the following day via a south coast steamer from Batabano, traveling unarmed with two friends. Believing the moment has arrived to place himself at the head of the movement, Jiminez construes a dispatch from a former Dominican interior minister in Santiago urging revolutionists to deny the uprising's success as merely a device to deter Cuban and American adventurers eager to sail for San Domingo, and he likewise dismisses as probably false a telegram received that day reporting a rebel defeat, citing uniformly favorable reports from his own representatives. Jiminez adds that although he wished to leave long ago, he remained in Havana on his friends' advice until his presence was needed at the scene of action, and having cabled his intention, he expects to start the next day unless advised otherwise.

Un despacho fechado en La Habana el 16 de agosto en el New-York Tribune informa que el general Juan Isidro Jiminez, aspirante a la presidencia de San Domingo, probablemente partirá de La Habana hacia la isla al día siguiente en un vapor de la costa sur desde Batabanó, viajando desarmado en compañía de dos amigos. Convencido de que ha llegado el momento de ponerse al frente del movimiento, Jiminez interpreta un despacho enviado desde Santiago por un exministro dominicano del Interior que instaba a negar la prosperidad de la revuelta tan solo como un medio para frenar a los aventureros cubanos y estadounidenses deseosos de embarcarse hacia San Domingo, y del mismo modo descarta por probablemente falso un telegrama recibido ese día sobre una derrota rebelde, alegando que los informes personales de sus representantes solo hablan de éxitos. Jiminez añade que, aunque deseaba partir desde hacía mucho tiempo, permaneció en La Habana por consejo de sus amigos hasta que su presencia fue necesaria en el teatro de los hechos y, tras cablegrafiar su intención, prevé salir al día siguiente salvo aviso en contrario.

The Houston Daily Post

The Houston Daily Post, concluding an item on Jiminez's expedition

August 18, 189918 de agosto de 1899

The Houston Daily Post, concluding an item on Jiminez's expedition plans, reports that a vessel placed at his disposal will meet him at or off Santiago, that he proposes to place all of Santo Domingo under one strong government—claiming that conditions in neighboring Hayti, a country bordering on revolution, favor the idea—and that his son will join him shortly if his Santo Domingo expedition succeeds. A separate New York dispatch dated August 17, citing a Herald report from Santo Domingo under the headline "Dominican Uprising," states that another invasion from Hayti was attempted on Tuesday, when government forces met and dispersed the insurgents near Monte Cristo after severe fighting and reportedly captured some of them. Because the revolutionary movement lacks organization and has waged only sporadic guerrilla warfare, the government is undismayed; interior reports describe everything as quiet, several leaders have been arrested, and despite lingering discontent the public generally hopes the civil troubles will soon be settled.

The New York Journal and Advertiser, citing State Department denials, reports that proposed additional U.S. precautions were not prompted by representations from the Santo Domingo government, but rest on the duty of preventing filibustering against a friendly nation—a stance already shown by military orders barring filibustering expeditions from leaving Cuba. Asking the Navy to assist is viewed as an indication that efforts in Cuba to aid the Dominican insurgents are more active than reports from Havana and Santiago suggest. An accompanying August 18 dispatch from Santiago de Cuba states that Dominican agents are rapidly recruiting expeditions, especially on the north coast; orders arrived from Havana that day to hold recently captured men pending investigation, trading schooners are closely watched, the tug Adonis was armed the previous day and sent to Guantanamo to patrol the coast, and despite several expeditions having escaped unhindered, Military Governor General Wood is determined to enforce neutrality.

El New York Journal and Advertiser, citando desmentidos del Departamento de Estado, informa que las medidas adicionales de precaución propuestas por los Estados Unidos no responden a gestiones del gobierno de Santo Domingo, sino al deber de impedir el filibusterismo contra una nación amiga, postura ya demostrada mediante las órdenes militares destinadas a evitar la salida de expediciones filibusteras desde Cuba. El hecho de haberse solicitado el auxilio de la Marina se interpreta como indicio de que en Cuba hay mayor actividad en apoyo de los insurgentes dominicanos de lo que reflejan los informes de La Habana y Santiago. Un despacho adjunto de Santiago de Cuba fechado el 18 de agosto señala que agentes dominicanos reclutan rápidamente expediciones, sobre todo en la costa norte; ese día llegaron órdenes de La Habana de retener bajo investigación a hombres capturados recientemente, las goletas mercantes son vigiladas de cerca, el remolcador Adonis fue armado la víspera y enviado a Guantanamo para patrullar la costa y, aunque varias expediciones lograron partir sin tropiezos, el gobernador militar general Wood está decidido a hacer cumplir la neutralidad.

A dispatch from Santiago, Cuba, in the New York Journal and Advertiser states

August 22, 189922 de agosto de 1899

A dispatch from Santiago, Cuba, in the New York Journal and Advertiser states that General Juan Isidro Jiminez is expected to arrive at Santiago or Manzanillo within a day or two, and reports that an American passenger aboard the steamer when Jiminez was arrested at Cienfuegos overheard him offering Dominican army commissions to two Cuban army officers. Jiminez's agents are said to be very active among Cubans in Santiago, where two Cubans believed to be in his pay are under surveillance. An accompanying August 21 dispatch from Havana adds that former Cuban soldiers and Italians continue offering their services as would-be filibusters to Jiminez's son despite his repeated statements that he does not wish to organize an expedition, while rumors say an independent group of twenty to thirty men will leave that day to fight for Jiminez in Santo Domingo if his cause prospers.

Un despacho desde Santiago, Cuba, publicado en el New York Journal and Advertiser señala que se espera la llegada del general Juan Isidro Jiminez a Santiago o a Manzanillo en uno o dos días, e informa que un pasajero estadounidense que viajaba en el vapor con Jiminez cuando fue arrestado en Cienfuegos escuchó una conversación en la que este ofreció grados en el ejército dominicano a dos oficiales del ejército cubano. Se afirma que los agentes de Jiminez despliegan gran actividad entre los cubanos en Santiago, donde dos cubanos que se cree están a su sueldo se hallan bajo vigilancia. Un despacho adjunto de La Habana fechado el 21 de agosto añade que exsoldados cubanos e italianos siguen ofreciendo sus servicios como aspirantes a filibusteros al hijo de Jiminez pese a la insistencia de este en que no desea organizar ninguna expedición, mientras se rumora que un grupo independiente de entre veinte y treinta hombres partirá ese día para combatir por Jiminez en Santo Domingo si su causa prospera.

The Denison Review

The Denison Review, following a comment that authorities had no right

August 22, 189922 de agosto de 1899

The Denison Review, following a comment that authorities had no right to arrest Jiminez as an unarmed citizen apparently going to Santiago de Cuba—and that even if bound for San Domingo he would only be returning to his native country—prints an August 19 Kingston dispatch under the headline "Searching for Jiminez." Citing advices from Santo Domingo, the dispatch reports that the government there is desperately apprehensive of the momentarily expected arrival of revolutionary leader Jiminez, which would completely turn partly undecided popular opinion, and has ordered armed vessels patrolling the coast to search every strange ship and capture him under any flag, armed or not. A partially garbled August 19 dispatch from Santiago de Cuba below it notes expeditions being recruited on the north coast, orders to hold recently captured men, several expeditions getting away without interference, and a determination to enforce neutrality.

The Denison Review publica un despacho de Kingston del 19 de agosto bajo el titular «Searching for Jiminez», tras un comentario según el cual las autoridades no tenían derecho a arrestar a Jiminez como ciudadano desarmado que se dirigía aparentemente a Santiago de Cuba —y que aun si fuera a San Domingo solo regresaría a su país natal—. Citando informes de Santo Domingo, el despacho señala que el gobierno dominicano teme desesperadamente la llegada inminente del líder revolucionario Jiminez, la cual volcaría por completo a la opinión pública parcialmente indecisa, y ha ordenado a buques armados que patrullan la costa registrar toda embarcación extraña y capturarlo bajo cualquier bandera, armado o no. Más abajo, un despacho parcialmente mutilado desde Santiago de Cuba del 19 de agosto menciona el reclutamiento de expediciones en la costa norte, órdenes de retener a hombres capturados recientemente, la salida sin interferencia de varias expediciones y la determinación de hacer cumplir la neutralidad.
