ST. DOMINGO. Annexation the Desire of the People of St. Domingo and Hayti—The Leading Men of Both Republics Urge It.SANTO DOMINGO. La anexión, el deseo del pueblo de Santo Domingo y Haití: los hombres principales de ambas repúblicas la instan

November 26, 186926 de noviembre de 1869

President Baez is ready to open negotiations when assured that the Congress of the United States is ready to make the necessary appropriation to meet the requirements of the occasion; the people of St. Domingo are opposed to the dismemberment of the republic, and favor the annexation of the country in its entirety. The partisans of Pimental are scattering handbills throughout the island favoring the project of annexation, and the only opposition exhibited is that derived from European sources. The republic of Hayti is following suit as regards annexation, the letter adds, and Salnave and Saget are loud in its favor; it is said the entire island can be purchased by the United States for $2,000,000, the same figure other papers of the week printed for the same report.

El presidente Báez está listo para abrir negociaciones cuando tenga la seguridad de que el Congreso de los Estados Unidos está listo para hacer la asignación necesaria para cubrir las exigencias de la ocasión; el pueblo de Santo Domingo se opone a la desmembración de la república, y favorece la anexión del país en su totalidad. Los partidarios de Pimental reparten hojas sueltas por toda la isla a favor del proyecto de anexión, y la única oposición manifestada es la derivada de fuentes europeas. La república de Haití sigue el ejemplo en cuanto a la anexión, añade la carta, y Salnave y Saget se pronuncian ruidosamente a su favor; se dice que la isla entera puede ser comprada por los Estados Unidos por $2.000.000, cifra que es la misma impresa para el mismo informe en otros periódicos de la semana.

No nos sorprenderá en lo más mínimo, continúa el editorial, si antes de que expiren seis meses la anexión de la magnífica isla de Santo Domingo, o Haití, sea un hecho consumado. The cattle of Dominica are celebrated; its mahogany and sandal wood are the best in the tropics; many American colored men have settled in this republic, and, but for the civil war, none would ever have quitted it. The bay of Samana, the feature continues, is the outlet of the Yuna River, and is fifty miles long and from fifteen to twenty miles wide; the ocean entrance to it is only a mile wide, and is commanded by an old fort, while high mountains enclose both shores. Twenty-five miles up the bay, upon an inlet to the north, is the town of Samana, the inlet very deep and almost land-locked; the bay has been compared both to the Bay of Naples and to the Chesapeake, to the former for its dazzling tropical and mountain scenery, to the latter for its innumerable wild fowl. This bay has been pronounced the key to the Gulf of Mexico; fine beds of coal surround it, and navigators say that the future route to Darien and Mexico will be by the Mona Passage, just outside of Samana. Samana town, the passage adds, is no town at all, a mere semi-detached peninsula with town vestiges upon it, and the site is swampy; Bonaparte planned a city near Samana, to be called Napoleon, and had the site surveyed.
